## 3  Modeling Approach

This measure replaces traditional existing natural gas or fuel oil boilers in the ComStock baseline models with condensing gas boilers. The implementation of this measure is fairly simple-it upgrades the nominal thermal efficiency of the existing boiler object to represent the more efficient condensing boiler operation. In addition, it modifies the performance curves to represent those of a condensing boiler. Finally, it implements outdoor air reset to allow supply temperatures to drop below the traditional set point of 180°F.

This measure is modeled as a minimal boiler upgrade, in which only the boiler is replaced. All other equipment, such as coils, piping, and pumps, remains the same as in the original building. This is meant to represent a less involved, lower-cost boiler retrofit that may be more realistic for building owners than replacing all hydronic equipment in the building. In practice, boilers are often simply swapped out with condensing boilers with no adjustments to the system [1]. This will result in less-than-optimal performance of the condensing boiler but represents more realistic energy savings for the retrofit. This measure will make some adjustments to the system, including outdoor air reset, to ensure that condensing mode efficiencies are achieved. However, it will not replace or re-autosize any other equipment in the hydronic system, representing a minimally invasive upgrade scenario.

To determine an appropriate nominal thermal efficiency to use for modeling, we analyzed the condensing boilers in the Air-Conditioning, Heating, and Refrigeration (AHRI) Directory of Certified Product Performance [7]. The database contains nearly 500 condensing boiler products sold in the United States and Canada. We plotted the thermal efficiencies of all AHRI condensing boiler products to understand the distribution of possible efficiencies. As shown in Figure 4, thermal efficiencies ranged from 82%-100%. Most products fell into the 94%-98% efficiency range, with the most common being 94%-96%. For this reason, we selected 95% efficiency for modeling to represent the median efficiency level of commercially available products.

In addition to thermal efficiency, we conducted research into new performance curves. Boiler performance curves are typically boiler efficiency as a function of PLR and water temperature (either entering or leaving water temperature). We were unable to find raw performance data from manufacturers; however, there were several sources with performance curve maps or images, including from Lochinvar high-efficiency boilers and water heaters [8], ASHRAE [3], New Jersey's Clean Energy Program [9], and kW Engineering [10]. The curves or images from the four sources were all similar and follow the trend shown in Figure 5. The shape of the curve reflects the boiler performance described in Figure 5, where sharp increases in efficiency are not seen until an EWT of ~135°F or lower is achieved.

Because these curves were all similar, the Lochinvar curves, which are for a Crest condensing boiler unit, were used for modeling. This is because the graph was the most granular and could be parsed. The Lochinvar curve shows boiler efficiency as a function of return water temperature. We performed a manual data extraction on the Lochinvar performance curves to convert the image to biquadratic performance maps compatible with EnergyPlus ® . In Figure 6, the Lochinvar performance curve from their catalog is shown on top, and the manually created performance curve we used for modeling is shown on the bottom.

Another consideration for modeling is a reduced hot water supply temperature, which is required for high-efficiency condensing operation to occur. According to the Lochinvar product manual, a supply temperature of 140°F is recommended [11]. A delta-T of 40°F, which corresponds to a return temperature of 100°F, is reasonable based on a Cleaver-Brooks study [12], as well as other sources cited throughout this document. A return temperature of 100°F corresponds to efficiencies of 93%-96%, depending on the PLR. Although higher efficiencies can be achieved with return temperatures closer to 80°F, this is difficult to achieve in practice; therefore, we used a more conservative delta-T for modeling.

A supply air temperature reset based on the outdoor air temperature will be implemented in this measure to allow for the lower supply temperatures required to achieve condensing operation. The supply air temperature reset will scale linearly between the traditional boiler set point of 180°F and a lower boiler set point of 140°F. When outdoor air temperatures are below 20°F, the supply temperature will be 180°F, but when the outdoor air temperatures reach 50°F, the supply water temperature will be 140°F. This allows for more efficient operation of the boiler during shoulder seasons or warmer winter days, when less heat is needed to maintain indoor temperatures.

As mentioned earlier, this measure is being modeled as a minimal boiler retrofit in which only the boiler is replaced. All other equipment, such as coils, piping, and pumps, remains the same size as in the original building. Therefore, no components are re-autosized in this measure.
