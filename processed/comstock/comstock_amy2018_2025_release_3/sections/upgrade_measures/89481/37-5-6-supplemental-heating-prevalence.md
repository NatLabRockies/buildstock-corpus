## 5.6  Supplemental Heating Prevalence

Adding heat/energy recovery to HP-RTUs reduces the prevalence of backup heating for the median building across all Building America climate zones (Figure 13). This is due to reduced heating loads from pre-conditioning outdoor ventilation air with the heat/energy recovery system. Supplemental electric resistance heating generally has 2-4x lower efficiency than heat pump heating, so its use should be limited where possible.

Figure 13 shows the share of heating electricity input that goes to supplemental electric resistance heating for applicable models, as box plots on an x-axis labeled 'Fraction of Electric Heating Supplemental' running 0.0 to 1.0. Rows are grouped by Building America climate zone, each with two bars, HP RTU E Backup and HP RTU + ER: Hot-Humid, Hot-Dry, Marine, Mixed-Dry, Mixed-Humid, Cold, and Very Cold. In every zone the HP RTU + ER box sits to the left of the HP RTU E Backup box, so adding heat/energy recovery reduces supplemental heating prevalence for the median building in all zones, because preconditioning outdoor ventilation air lowers the heating load. Supplemental fractions rise steadily with climate severity, from near 0.05 in the hot and marine zones to roughly 0.25 for Cold and roughly 0.45 to 0.55 for Very Cold, with long whiskers reaching toward 1.0 in the colder zones. Supplemental resistance heat is used whenever the heat pump cannot meet the load, which happens because units are sized to the design cooling load and because heat pump capacity derates at low outdoor temperatures and during defrost. It is 2 to 4 times less efficient than heat pump heating.

Supplemental electric resistance heating occurs for multiple reasons. First, RTUs in this analysis are sized to the design cooling load, which in some cases means the system design requires the use of supplemental heat to meet the design heating load at design temperatures. Additionally, heat pump heating capacity generally decreases with lower outdoor air temperatures, which can be further exacerbated by increased heat pump defrost operation during colder outdoor periods. Regardless of the reason, the supplemental electric resistance heating in this analysis is used to address any heating load not met by the heat pump. As expected, colder climate zones show higher prevalence of supplemental electric resistance heat on average due to higher heating loads, colder temperatures that derate available heat pump capacity, and insufficient heat pump capacity when sized to cooling loads, which in cold climates may be much smaller than the heating loads.
