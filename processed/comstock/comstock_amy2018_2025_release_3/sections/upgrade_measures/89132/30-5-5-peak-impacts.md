## 5.5  Peak Impacts

Figure 8 shows the impact of the Console GHP upgrade on seasonal peak hours. The winter peak is shifted slightly earlier in the day as morning electric heating now has more influence on peak load in winter. The shoulder peak and summer peak moved slightly later in the day. Because the efficiency of the new GHP system is no longer dependent on outdoor air temperature, as is the case with the direct-expansion cooling systems in the baseline, we may expect the cooling dominated peaks to change timing slightly with the new systems.

Three stacked box-and-whisker panels, labeled summer, winter, and shoulder, comparing when the maximum daily electricity peak occurs for the ComStock baseline versus the Console GHP upgrade. Each panel has two rows under an Upgrade Name axis, Baseline and Console GHP, plotted against a shared x-axis of Maximum Daily Peak Timing by Season (Hour of Day) running 0 to 24. In all three seasons the interquartile boxes are centered in the late morning to mid-afternoon window, roughly hours 10 through 15, with long whiskers reaching from the early morning hours into the evening and scattered outlier points near hour 22 and beyond. Baseline and upgrade distributions are very similar in shape, so the effect is a shift rather than a restructuring of peak timing. The accompanying text reports that the winter peak moves slightly earlier in the day, because morning electric heating gains influence over the winter peak once heating is electrified, while the summer and shoulder peaks move slightly later, attributed to the ground-coupled heat pump's efficiency no longer tracking outdoor air temperature the way the baseline direct-expansion cooling equipment did.

Figure 9 shows the impact of the Console GHP upgrade on seasonal peak intensity for the median building. Summer peaks are reduced across all climate zones due to cooling savings from the GHP. The summer peak intensity was reduced by 3-8% in all climate zones. The subarctic climate zone does not have any days in the year that are considered 'summer,' which is why this part of the graph is blank.

Winter peaks in heating-dominated climates (Cold, Very Cold) experienced a 5-7% increase due to the increase in electric heating load. The median peak intensity in the subarctic climate did not change because there was a very small sample size for this climate zone. In the remaining cooling-dominated and moderate climates, the peak intensity was reduced by 0-4%. This is because heating is not as big a driver of the winter peak in these climates, so the HVAC efficiency improvements outweighed the added heating load. In shoulder seasons, the peak intensity was reduced across all climates by 0%-4% due to the HVAC efficiency improvements of the GHPs.
