## 5.5  Site Energy Savings Distributions

This section discusses site energy consumption for quality assurance/quality control purposes. Note that site energy savings can be useful for these purposes, but other factors should be considered when drawing conclusions, as they do not necessarily translate proportionally to source energy savings or utility bill costs.

Figure 7 illustrates the distribution of site energy savings (%) by end use and fuel type across models that received the measure scenario package compared to their corresponding baseline ComStock models representing existing buildings. On-site combustion fuel heating shows the higher average savings of ~100%, representing buildings in which gas heat was mostly or completely replaced with electric heat pump heating. Buildings with less than 100% savings have a nonapplicable gas heating system (e.g., unit heater).

Figure 8. Horizontal violin-plus-box plots of Percent Site Energy Savings by End Use (%), x-axis about -160% to 100%, titled 'Upgrade 5.0: Std Perf w Roof (unweighted)'. Rows are end-use/fuel combinations with model counts: Other Fuel Water Systems (n=168), Other Fuel Heating (n=6688), Natural Gas Water Systems (n=1380), Natural Gas Heating (n=15608), Electricity Water Systems (n=1601), Electricity Refrigeration (n=7266), Electricity Interior Equipment (n=26), Electricity Heating (n=33914), Electricity Heat Recovery (n=1020), Electricity Fans (n=34348), and Electricity Cooling (n=34342). Natural Gas Heating and Other Fuel Heating show large positive savings distributions (heating fuel-switched off fossil fuels), Electricity Cooling and Fans are positive, while Electricity Heating skews strongly negative (electricity added by the heat pumps).

The data points that appear above some of the distributions indicate outliers in the distribution, meaning they fall outside 1.5 times the interquartile range. The value for n indicates the number of ComStock models that were applicable for energy savings for the fuel type category.

Electric heating shows high savings of around 60% for the median building. However, it is important to note that this distribution only represents buildings that had at least some electric heating in the baseline. Therefore, the high savings are from replacing electric resistance systems with relatively higher-efficiency heat pumps. The distribution does include buildings with only gas heating in the existing building (gas RTUs), which would show increased usage in electric heating due to fuel switching. However, they are not represented here as this case would result in an infinite increase as a percentage. Some models show very high electric heating penalties upward of 100%. These are generally models that started with limited electric heating (e.g., an electric unit heater in a vestibule), but otherwise use gas heating as the primary system. The fuel switching therefore adds much more electric heating in this instance, which yields a high percent increase.

Fans and cooling also show higher savings of around 15% -25% for the median building. Again, this is due to replacing older, less-efficient RTUs with newer RTUs that have more efficient staged fans and cooling systems. Note that these savings are not exclusive to heat pump retrofits and could be achievable with RTU retrofits regardless of the heating source. Some portion of the savings distribution shows increased usage for fans and cooling, although mostly outliers in the distribution. These are primarily models that experience increased operation during cycling operation due to the EnergyPlus inconsistency mentioned earlier with the multispeed coil objects used for modeling the HP-RTUs in this study.

Some models show negative heat recovery savings, noting that the heat recovery end use is for electricity used to operate enthalpy wheels. These negative energy savings are due to the increased prevalence of wheel operation caused by increased cycling operation with the HPRTUs compared to the baseline RTUs. In other words, increased run time for the air handler can cause increased run time for the enthalpy wheel. This is in part due to the EnergyPlus inconsistency that causes longer cycling operation with the multispeed coil objects used for modeling the HP-RTU. However, the heat recovery end use makes up a small portion of building stock energy usage, so negative percentage savings in this case have minimal overall impact.
