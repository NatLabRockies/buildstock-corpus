## 4.1.2 Single Dwelling Time-Series Results

Time-series results from summer and winter days for both dwelling test models are shown below. Figure 3 shows a decrease in energy consumption for Dwelling 1 throughout the summer day because of the more efficient cooling mode of the ASHP. Similarly, in Figure 4, Dwelling 2 sees a decrease in energy consumption of approximately the same magnitude for the summer day. All-time series are taken from 15-minute intervals. Peaks in individual dwelling time series are typically caused by schedules (e.g., appliance usage schedules, setpoint setback schedules).

Figure 3. Dwelling 1 electricity profile for a summer day. Energy consumption decreases because of a more efficient cooling mode.

Figure 4: stacked area chart for Dwelling 2, a single-family detached home in Texas (Climate Zone 2A), on a summer day. Left axis is average electricity demand (kW, 0 to 1.2) by end use; right axis is outdoor drybulb temperature (°F), dashed; the baseline total is dotted. Cooling dominates and the upgrade total peaks near 1.05 kW at about 17:00, staying below the baseline all day.

For the winter days, Dwelling 1 incurs colder outdoor temps because of its location in a colder climate. Because of these colder outdoor temps, Dwelling 1 must utilize its ASHP backup unit. Figure 5 shows the backup heat supplementing the ASHP heat over various times in the day, including a large spike around noon. Dwelling 2 sees milder winter temperatures and therefore does not need to utilize its backup heating element. Figure 6 shows that the household's load is mostly heating energy, but the ASHP is sufficient to cover the home's heating needs. Both dwellings see much more electricity demand in the winter than baseline because of their heating fuel switching from natural gas to electricity.

Figure 5. Dwelling 1 electricity profile for a winter day. Because of low outdoor temperatures, backup heating is utilized to cover the heating load.
