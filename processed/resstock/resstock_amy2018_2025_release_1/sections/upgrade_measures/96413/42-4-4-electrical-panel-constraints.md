## 4.4  Electrical Panel Constraints

Figure 13 shows the distribution of modeled panel constraints overall (see 'all' column) and by baseline heating fuel. It is estimated that 44.8% of dwellings upgrading to the typical cold climate heat pump face either a breaker space or a capacity constraint (or both) while 55.2% have no constraints. Most dwellings that face a panel constraint are those that do not have electricity as their baseline heating fuel (i.e., fossil fuel heated dwellings).

All post-upgrade constraint statistics include a minute fraction of homes that are already capacity-constrained in the baseline. Prior to the upgrade, 2.3% of the existing panels are estimated to already be over-capacity, which is possible as the capacity constraint is estimated based on code. In contrast, no existing panel is space-constrained, which makes sense as it is physically impossible to connect more loads than there is space. Capacity constraints in the existing stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. However, it is important to recognize that this level of uncertainty applies to all panel results, including those that look reasonable. Hence, the capacity-constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on. During the cold climate ASHP upgrade, any homes already constrained before the upgrade will likely need to address their capacity issue to be permitted by code to connect a new load to the panel, whether the upgrade is making the constraint worse or not. Thus, the post-upgrade constraint statistics can be attributed entirely to the upgrade. Users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose pre-existing capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.

Figure 14 and Figure 15 show the modeled state-level distribution of homes that become capacity-constrained and space-constrained, respectively, by state post-upgrade. Figure 16 shows the distribution that is constrained overall based on either capacity or space. Capacity constraints range from 3.0% in Hawai'i to 64.6% in Nebraska. Generally, this trend is in line with electric heating saturation in the baseline scenario. Warmer climate states like Florida, Texas, and California have higher penetration of electric heating in the baseline, requiring higher capacity electrical panels. This trend is also seen in the panel size constraint-states with higher penetration of electric heating have more existing space in their electrical panels.
