## 4.4  Electrical Panel Constraints

Figure 15 shows the distribution of modeled panel constraints overall (see 'all' column) and by water heater fuel. It is estimated that about 20% of the homes upgrading to a HPWH overall face either a breaker space or a capacity constraint, while about 80% have no constraints. Breaking down those constrained, 2.7% are constrained by capacity while 17.9% are constrained by space. 95% of the homes that face a panel constraint are those that previously have a fuel water heating system. This makes sense because the HPWH adds 4,500 W of peak load and requires two breaker spaces to install a new 240V circuit for the water heater under normal practice. Nearly four in ten homes with fuel water heaters become panel-constrained, mostly due not having enough space for a new circuit. For homes with electric resistance storage water heaters, the existing circuit for the water heater is reused, and the power rating is similar between the incumbent and the replacement water heater so no new load is added. For homes with electric resistance tankless water heaters, the total connected load is significantly reduced by the upgrade as the high-power tankless water heater is removed.

All post-upgrade constraint statistics includes a minute fraction of homes that are already capacity-constrained in the baseline. Prior to the upgrade, 2.3% of the existing panels are estimated to already be over-capacity, which is possible as the capacity constraint is estimated based on code. In contrast, no existing panel is space-constrained, which makes sense as it is physically impossible to connect more loads than there is space. Capacity constraints in the existing stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. However, it is important to recognize that this level of uncertainty applies to all panel results, including those that look reasonable. Hence, the capacity-constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on. During the HPWH upgrade, any homes already constrained before the upgrade will likely need to address their capacity issue in order to be permitted by code to connect a new load to the panel, whether the upgrade is making the constraint worse or not. Thus, the post-upgrade constraint statistics can be attributed entirely to the upgrade. Users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose pre-existing capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.

Figure 16 and Figure 17 show the modeled state-level distribution of homes that become capacity-constrained and space-constrained, respectively, by state post-upgrade. Figure 18 shows the distribution that is constrained overall based on either capacity or space. Across states, capacity constraint ranges from 1.5% in NJ to 5.6% in KS. This trend does not mirror the saturation of non-electric water heaters across the U.S. For example, CA has the most number of fuel water heaters but only runs into a capacity constraint about 2% of the time. This is in part because in addition to water heater fuel, panel size can also vary significantly with other housing characteristics, such as floor area, vintage, and the presence of other fuel appliances. In comparison, space constraint is more common, ranging from 3.5% in FL to 29.1% in UT. This is a reflection that an estimated 35% of U.S. homes have 1 or 0 open breaker space on their panel, according to ResStock's baseline housing. As a result, the overall panel constraint for the upgrade is driven by insufficient space, meaning the average home is more likely to run out of space on the panel than out of capacity when upgrading to an HPWH.
