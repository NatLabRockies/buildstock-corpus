## 3  Modeling Approach

ResStock models one water heater for every home and all water heaters as a standalone appliance, with diversity in efficiency and installed location informed by Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) 2020 microdata (US EIA, 2024). Most water heaters are installed in the conditioned space or adjacent to the thermal envelope, with over 40% in the living space and 11% in heated basements. ResStock does not model multifamily centralized water heaters in buildings, which exist in about 14% of the stock according to the 'Water Heater In Unit' parameter in ResStock. 1 Instead, a portion of the central water heater's energy consumption is approximated for the modeled dwelling unit using a standalone water heater model located in a conditioned mechanical space outside the dwelling unit. The out-of-unit location prevents the water heater from interacting with any in-unit systems, but the model does not account for any realistic hot water distribution and recirculation losses, which can be significantly more depending on the location of the dwelling. This means the water heating energy for multifamily units with 'out-of-unit' central water heating is generally underestimated for both the baseline and the upgrade, as they go from a shared water heater to a shared HPWH.

In ResStock, all HPWHs are modeled using a stratified tank model that operates in a hybrid mode with realistic thermostatic control. A stratified tank realistically captures the thermocline that exists inside the tank as hot water is drawn out from the top and cold water comes in the bottom. The heat pump reheats the bottom half of the tank, which is adjacent to the condenser. The tank is controlled using a weighted average of a measured upper and lower temperature. In hybrid mode, the water heater first turns on the heat pump and only resorts to the backup elements in high demand scenarios where the temperature drops substantially. Hot water draw schedules are stochastically modeled (Hendron, Burch, &amp; Barker, 2010), and total demand is tabulated from fixture (showers, sinks, and baths) use, which varies by the number of occupants, and from hot water appliances (dishwasher and clothes washer). Multipliers are additionally applied at random to each of the hot water demands to reflect usage diversity. An impact of this usage diversity is that a small family can use a lot of water when assigned large multipliers and vice versa, and large unmet hot water load is possible. When there is unmet load but no explicit comfort metric currently to track it, it makes it difficult to compare the energy consumption before and after an upgrade. However, as these edge cases are rare, the uncertainty is limited. Refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide or more details on the modeling process of water heating (Reyna, et al., 2025).

1 'Water Heater In Unit' is a misnomer since the parameter denotes whether the water heater is centralized and shared rather than the status of the water heater's physical location.

The efficiency of the heat pump used by the HPWH varies during operation depending on the indoor wet bulb temperature and the temperature of water adjacent to the condenser. The condenser is wrapped around the bottom half of the tank under the jacket insulation, while the inlet air conditions vary based on installation location. While performance in EnergyPlus can be mapped to either wet bulb or dry bulb temperature, using wet bulb temperature captures that humidity does have some impact on performance. The nominal COP of the heat pump is calculated by fitting to a regression of different COP values for a simulated UEF test to derive a relationship between the two.
