## 4.1.2 Individual Dwelling Time-Series Results

Section 4.1 shows that for the dwelling samples, the HPWH upgrade primarily affects water heater energy consumption, with no or small changes in other end uses. So, this section only analyzes the 15-minute time-series data of water heater energy consumption for the three example dwelling units, as illustrated in Figure 5. A day containing the annual peak of the HPWH is selected for each dwelling unit as a representative day for this analysis.

When the electric resistance backup does not run, the HPWH upgrade significantly reduces peak hot water energy consumption compared to the baseline electric standard storage water heaters. This is the case for the HI example, where the peak hot water energy was reduced from 0.54 to 0.13 kWh per 15 minutes, a 75% reduction. When the HPWH backup turns on, there is little reduction in peak consumption. However, the frequency of peaks can reduce significantly because the HPWH tank is larger and it takes more consecutive hot water demand events to deplete enough hot water to trigger the backup. In CA, peak hot water energy was reduced from 1.0 to 0.7 kWh per 15 minutes, a 28% reduction, and the backup only came on during two time steps. In AK, the HPWH upgrade reduced peak hot water energy consumption from 1.4 to 1.2 kWh per 15 minutes, a 18% reduction. Note the AK example's electric profile is nearly identical between the baseline and the upgrade, indicating that the HPWH backup is running fairly frequently during the peak day. Overall though, the use of the HPWH backup for the AK example is reduced from 37% of the time to 11%. In addition to lowering peaks and the frequency of peaks, the HPWH upgrade consistently reduces total hot water energy use. These results highlight the efficiency benefits of HPWHs. In addition, HPWHs have the potential to contribute to grid-friendly load shifting using smart controls and load shaping by reducing power draw when replacing electric resistance water heaters (Butzbaugh, 2023).
