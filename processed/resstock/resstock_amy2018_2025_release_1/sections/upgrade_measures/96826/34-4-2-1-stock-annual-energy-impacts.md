## 4.2.1  Stock Annual Energy Impacts

By applying a high-efficiency HPWH to all homes eligible for such an upgrade-99.5 of the housing stock-the modeled overnight savings potential is estimated at nearly 280 TWh in total site energy, with 73% of the savings comes from natural gas and 19% from electricity, as shown in Figure 6. Figure 7 through Figure 11 present the measure impact for upgraded and occupied dwellings only, which represent 90% of the stock. Vacant units are excluded because they do not have occupant-driven consumption to illustrate realistic energy savings. Figure 7 compares the modeled energy end-use breakdown of the average home before and after the HPWH upgrade. The modeled average energy savings is 2,210 kWh per year, or 8.2% of the total site energy consumption. The savings primarily come from hot water energy reduction, with fuel use for hot water reduced to zero due to the fuel switching and electricity for hot water reduced by 74%. This means the overall savings from switching all electric resistance water heaters in the housing stock (47%) to HPWH more than offsets the electricity increase from installing electric HPWHs in the remaining 53% of homes with fuel water heaters, highlighting the energy efficiency potential for scaled adoption of HPWH. However, the technology does not lead to energy savings for all dwelling units, as shown in the next few figures.

On average, the HPWH upgrade increased space heating energy more than its energy savings from space cooling, resulting in a small net increase in HVAC energy. This is due in part to there being slightly more space heating than cooling in the housing stock. In some cases, where the HPWH is installed in the conditioned space and space heating is provided by a low-efficiency heating equipment, the increased demand for heating can override the HPWH savings, leading to a net increase in total energy. This is shown in Figure 8, where small fractions of dwelling units using conventional, non-heat pump heating (totaling 1.5% of the overall applicable stock) see a small increase in site energy consumption after the HPWH upgrade.

In the ResStock models, it is assumed that the 'interaction factor' between any point source of heating or cooling in a space and the thermostat is 100%. For a HPWH, this means that every unit of heat taken out of the living space and put into the tank for water heating has some impact on the indoor temperature. During the heating season, this causes the HVAC equipment to run more, while during the cooling season this is a net benefit that reduces the run time of cooling equipment. The actual interaction factor in homes can vary substantially depending on where the water heater is relative to the thermostat and is very difficult to quantify, although some experiments that attempted to quantify this have found the impact can be less than 1, and in some cases even less than 0.5, depending on the configuration of the home (Widder, Metzger, Petersen, &amp; McIntosh, 2017). As a result, the impact of HPWHs on HVAC loads in this study may be larger than what is seen in practice in the field.

Across climate zones, the range of energy savings can vary significantly, with the median percent savings higher in hotter climates and lower in colder climates, as shown in Figure 9. This is primarily driven by the difference in overall energy consumption across climate zones. In hotdry climates like HI and southern CA, where energy consumption is the lowest of all climate zones due to lower demand for space heating and cooling, the relative percent savings from water heater is larger. Conversely, water heater percent savings decreases in colder climates where space heating is very energy intensive. In general, HPWH performance decreases in colder climates by increasing the use of electric resistance backup to meet higher hot water demand, as discussed in Section 4.1. This trend is not very visible at the stock level due to other factors, like hot water usage and installation location, that also impact the HPWH performance.

The highest ranges of energy savings from the HPWH measure come from installation that does not affect the dwelling's HVAC, such as out-of-unit conditioned mechanical room and outdoor, as shown in Figure 10. Note that ResStock restricts outside installation to warmer climates only, hence their performance is not reduced. On the other hand, installing the HPWH within or near real conditioned spaces generally experience a HVAC energy penalty because the average U.S. home is heating dominant, and this reduces the energy savings of the measure. In terms of household size, the range of total energy savings generally increases with the number of occupants, as shown in Figure 11. This makes sense because household size is a primary driver of hot water demand. As energy savings scales directly with usage, the savings from higher usage surpasses the HPWH tank loss, thus improving the overall efficiency of the HPWH. A similar trend can be observed across the hot water fixture multiplier, which scales the demand from hot water fixtures.
