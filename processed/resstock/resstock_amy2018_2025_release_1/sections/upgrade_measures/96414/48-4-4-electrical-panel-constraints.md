## 4.4  Electrical Panel Constraints

Figure 11 shows the distribution of modeled panel constraints overall (see 'all' column) and by baseline heating fuel. It is estimated that 8.0% of dwellings upgrading to the typical cold climate heat pump face either a breaker space or a capacity constraint (or both), while 98.0% have no constraints. Most dwellings that face a panel constraint are those that do not have electricity as their baseline heating fuel (i.e., fossil fuel heated dwellings).

All post-upgrade constraint statistics include a minute fraction of homes that are already capacity-constrained in the baseline. Prior to the upgrade, 2.3% of the existing panels are estimated to already be over-capacity, which is possible as the capacity constraint is estimated based on code. In contrast, no existing panel is space-constrained, which makes sense as it is physically impossible to connect more loads than there is space. Capacity constraints in the existing stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. However, it is important to recognize that this level of uncertainty applies to all panel results, including those that look reasonable. Hence, the capacity-constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on. During the Dual Fuel Heat Pump upgrade, any homes already constrained before the upgrade will likely need to address their capacity issue in order to be permitted by code to connect a new load to the panel, whether the upgrade is making the constraint worse or not. Thus, the post-upgrade constraint statistics can be attributed entirely to the upgrade. Users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose pre-existing capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.

Figure 12 and Figure 13 show the modeled state-level distribution of homes that become capacity-constrained and space-constrained, respectively, by state post-upgrade. Figure 14 shows the distribution that is constrained overall based on either capacity or space. Capacity constraints range from 1.4% in Alaska to 13.9% in Kansas. Generally, this trend follows that higher capacity constraints occur during the utilization of the heat pump mode of the system at lowest temperatures possible (greater than 35°F, but not much higher). For very cold climates, the capacity constraint is not as large because those dwellings will utilize the natural gas furnace. Space constraints range from 0.3% in Florida to 7.7% in Wyoming. These space constraint trends follow which states likely have more electric appliances in baseline (e.g., electric heaters or water heaters).
