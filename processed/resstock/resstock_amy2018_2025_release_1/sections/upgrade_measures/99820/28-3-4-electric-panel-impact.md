## 3.4  Electric Panel Impact

This section details the impact the variable-speed GHP with light envelope improvements will have on electrical panels. The electric panel impact of this measure is the same as the stand-alone variable-speed GHP. The modeling assumptions and details about electric panels in ResStock can be found in the single-stage GHP documentation (Maguire, et al., 2026).

First, Figure 8 and Figure 9 show information about the ResStock 2025 Release 1 baseline housing stock electric panel distribution. Figure 8 shows in the first column (labeled 'All') that about 35% of all dwelling units 1 or fewer breaker spaces available. Figure 9 shows in the first column that 97.7% of electric panels have no existing capacity constraint for the baseline housing stock.

Capacity constraints in the existing baseline stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. However, it's important to recognize that this level of uncertainty applies to all panel results, including those that look reasonable. Hence, the capacity-constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on.

Before the variable-speed GHP with light envelope improvements package is applied, the panels experiencing a capacity constraint would likely need to address this issue first. To see results of non-affected households, users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose pre-existing capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.

Figure 10 shows the distribution of panel constraints overall (left column) and then broken out by heating fuel for dwelling units that receive the variable-speed GHP with envelope improvements. It is estimated that about 31% of dwelling units upgrading to a variable-speed GHP with light envelope improvements will experience either a capacity and/or space constraint, and the other 69% will experience no constraint. Looking more closely at the results, we see that dwelling units that are converting from natural gas, fuel oil, or propane heating fuels are far more likely (46%-62%) to face a panel constraint when switching to an all-electric GHP system. This compares to only 6.4% of dwellings that started with electric heating facing a panel constraint.

The next three figures show percentages of dwelling units for each state that would have some type of electric panel constraint after the variable-speed GHP with light envelope improvements is applied. Figure 11 shows a range of 6%-65% of dwelling units per state that would have either a capacity and/or space constraint after this upgrade package, with the largest prevalence of panel constraints occurring in colder states with high heating requirements. Specifically looking at space constraints, Figure 12 shows a smaller range from about 2% to 51% of dwelling units that would have a space constraint. Finally, Figure 13 shows a range of roughly 4% to 40% of dwelling units would have a capacity constraint.

In general, switching to a GHP system is more likely to face panel constraints in heatingdominated climates due to a substantial new electric load being introduced. Compared with the stand-alone variable-speed GHP measure (Maguire, et al., 2026), which shows ranges of 11%70% for either capacity and/or space constraints, 4%-53% for space constraints, and 7%-48% for capacity constraints, these results indicate that light envelope improvements help mitigate panel constraints.
