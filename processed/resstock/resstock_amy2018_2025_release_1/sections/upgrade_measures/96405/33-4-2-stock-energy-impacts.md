## 4.2  Stock Energy Impacts

Replacing existing windows with those that meet the ENERGY STAR V7 criteria is estimated to create 7% (0.25 TWh) annual stock site energy savings for residential buildings nationally (see Figure 6). This includes 12% site electricity heating savings (1.3E11 kWh), 12% site natural gas heating savings (3.2E10 kWh), 10% site electricity cooling savings (4.0E10 kWh), and 11% site fan and pump energy savings (7.11E9 kWh). Heating, cooling, and fan energy savings can be partially attributed to the decreased thermal conductance (U-value) of the replacement windows, which generally reduces heating and cooling loads. This reduces the energy requirement of the heating and cooling system, but can also decrease the amount of time HVAC fans and pumps need to run, which explains the energy savings in that category. Similarly, a lower SHGC reduces solar gains to the building, which can also reduce cooling load, but it some cases may increase heating load due to reduced heat gain when the building is actively heating. Because this upgrade is applicable to all dwelling units, the whole-stock impact and applicable segment impact results are identical.

Diving into the results in more detail, we first segment the savings by the type of window present in the baseline building. Looking at natural gas savings in homes with natural gas heating in Figure 7, we see that higher savings are found in dwellings upgrading from lower-performing single pane windows, while lower savings are found in dwellings that already have higher performance windows. Another notable output is that in some dwellings, upgrading the windows increases natural gas consumption. This is only seen in homes in warmer climates where overall heating energy is low. In these cases, the new windows let in less solar heat than the originals, increasing the need for heating energy. Only 1.7% of the building stock saw increases in total energy consumption. In Figure 8, we see a similar trend for electricity savings in homes with electric heating. In Figure 9, we see a similar trend for electricity used for cooling. In this last case, there are negative savings in the north-central climate, likely because the new windows trap more internal gains inside the home. Note that ENERGY STAR rating encompasses a range of performance values. With more nuanced product selection, it is likely possible to identify an ENERGY STAR window that does not have negative savings in a particular climate.

This upgrade changes both window properties and infiltration rates. In buildings with the same baseline window type, we would expect to see higher savings in buildings that started with a higher infiltration rate. Figure 10 shows savings in natural gas heating energy for natural gas heated homes, which start with single, clear, metal windows. In practice, the trend is visible but is not as strong as our intuition might suggest because of the confounding factors that are not controlled for, e.g. building size, weather differences, glazed area, orientation, etc. This figure also indicates that the bulk of the energy savings from this upgrade do not come from the infiltration reduction, because dwellings with infiltration rates ranging from 50 ACH50 down to 4 ACH50 show little difference in average savings.

As seen in Figure 11, examining the distributions behind the averages shows a range of savings. This highlights the importance of making decisions for individual buildings based on savings results for their peer group. For applications like utility rebate programs where a prescriptive savings number is applied, it also highlights the importance of understanding whether the population of participants is likely to reflect the total population or be a subset. If it is expected to be a subset, savings for this subset should be analyzed.
