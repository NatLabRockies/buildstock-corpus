## 4.1.1 Example Time Series Individual Building Results

Individual heating and cooling energy load profiles are shown below for the three dwelling units. In general, the upgraded dwelling units show a decrease in both heating and cooling energy, as expected given the improved insulation and reduced infiltration rates of these dwelling units. January 1 and July 1 were chosen as representative winter and summer days.

Figure 2 shows the baseline and the upgraded natural gas heating energy for the Colorado model, represented by the top line of figures. On January 1, the upgraded dwelling unit has a lower hourly heating profile than the baseline, which is the expected result.

For the same building, the cooling energy for the upgraded dwelling unit is generally lower than that of the baseline unit on the example day of July 1. In this example, for the one day of data shown, the upgraded dwelling unit uses slightly more cooling energy in the early morning and very late at night. These values are expected, and the winter morning peak shows a decrease, which is the anticipated result.

In general, the North Carolina model shows that the heating energy of the upgraded building is lower than that of the baseline building on January 1. From roughly noon to 7 p.m., the heating energy of the upgraded building is higher. A closer look at the individual indoor conditioned space temperature results and the outside wet-bulb temperature (not shown) reveals that the interior temperature of the baseline building rises above the setpoint during part of the noon to 7 p.m. period, causing the heating energy to decrease and eventually reach zero. This coincides with the hottest part of the day.

After wall insulation is added and air infiltration is reduced, the interior temperature of the upgraded home remains more constant, so the temperature fluctuates less. As a result, the house stays a bit cooler and does not exceed the heating setpoint, so the heating system must remain on to maintain to the setpoint.

The North Carolina model shows expected cooling results, with the upgraded dwelling unit having a smaller, more rounded peak and overall lower cooling energy than the baseline unit on July 1.

For the third building, the Arizona model, heating energy is lower in the upgraded scenario than in the baseline scenario on January 1. The heating system turns on a few hours later in the upgraded scenario, indicating that wall insulation and advanced air sealing keep the home warmer during the night and into early morning, so the heat does not need to run until later in the morning. The heating peak is also significantly reduced. Both results are in line with expectations.

Finally, the Arizona model's upgraded cooling energy profile is lower than the profile for all hours on July 1. The curve is smaller and has a lower peak as well. These results are consistent with the other two example buildings and the expected outcomes.
