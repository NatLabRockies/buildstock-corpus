## 1.1  Introduction to Drill-and-Fill Insulation

Drill-and-fill insulation is a method used to insulate existing walls. The most common drill-andfill insulation materials are cellulose, fiberglass, and spray foam. For cellulose and fiberglass, there are two main types of drill-and-fill installation: loose fill (also known as blown-in) and dense pack, which requires professional equipment (North 2012). This measure upgrade models cellulose as the drill-and-fill insulation material.

Drill-and-fill insulation can be installed from either the inside or outside of the building. In multifamily buildings, drilling from the outside is more common because it allows occupants to remain in place during the work is being performed, and reduces asbestos risk for installers (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency 2011) (Antonopoulos, et al. 2019). For these reasons, external drill-and-fill will be the focus of this upgrade. Drilling from the interior may be preferable in certain situations, such as inclement weather, when siding is difficult to remove, or when the home is vacant. In a drill-and-fill retrofit, small round holes (typically 1- to 2-inch diameter) are drilled into the exterior wall of the home between the studs (e.g., every 16 inches) (U.S. Department of Energy 2022). A hose is inserted into the wall, and insulation is then blown in. Both the small round holes and the hose are visible in Figure 1.

Exterior wall studs in residential construction commonly have three widths: 2x3, 2x4, and 2x6. The 2x3 and 2x4 studs are more common in mobile/manufactured homes and older residential construction, whereas 2x6 studs are more frequently used in newer residential construction (Krigger 2006) (Kochkin and Wiehagan 2014). Mobile/manufactured homes may use 2x2 studs, but these are typically only for interior walls (Dickerhoof 1977). This ResStock 2025 Release 1 assumes that R-13 insulation can fit within a 2x4 wall. In cases where higher levels of insulation are installed, OS-HPXML selects the appropriate wall construction set to ensure the desired Rvalue is achieved. This may include changing the stud cavity depth -for example, changing a wall from a 2x4 to a 2x6 to meet the requirement. More information on this topic can be found on the ResStock 'Wood Stud Depth and Wall RValue Calculations' page . 2

When installing drill-and-fill insulation from the exterior of a building, the cladding material is an important consideration. Exterior wood or vinyl siding is sometimes partially removed to allow holes to be drilled in the exterior sheathing. Wood siding can also be drilled through and then patched. Some types of metal siding may be removable, while others may not be (Allwein and Biddle 2002). In homes with face brick (4-inch) as cladding over wood stud walls, drill-andfill can be performed by drilling smaller holes through mortar joints. Other construction types, such as concrete masonry units (CMUs) or steel framing, are limited to insulation methods other than drill-and-fill for retrofits (U.S. Department of Energy 2019).

Other ways to insulate a wall include adding rigid insulation board to the exterior or interior, or removing interior finishes and adding batt insulation, among others (Baechler, et al. 2021) (Antonopoulos, et al. 2019). Attic, crawlspace, basement, or other building insulation techniques are not covered in this measure. Attic floor insulation for unfinished basements is addressed in a separate measure documentation. 3

There are safety concerns that must be addressed when performing drill-and-fill insulation. The structural integrity of the exterior wall and cladding is important, as is any existing water damage (Pfeiffer 2021). Asbestos is a health and safety concern for insulation installers and is more common in homes built between 1930 and 1970 (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency 2011). Mold or rotted wood may be found in stud cavities, which could also complicate retrofits (Brozyna and Badger 2013). This is not a complete list of safety concerns that should be addressed before upgrading a building's insulation.
