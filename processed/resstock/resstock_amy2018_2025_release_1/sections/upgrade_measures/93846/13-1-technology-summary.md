## 1 Technology Summary

Renewable energy is increasingly being adopted for electricity generation. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) projected that the share of U.S. power generation from renewables will increase from 21% in 2021 to 44% in 2050 (EIA, 2022). This projected increase consists mainly of new wind and solar power, but wind and solar power are inherently variable energy sources, which poses a challenge for grid stability and control compared to fossil fuel-based power generation. For instance, solar power generation fluctuates with the time of day and weather, whereas wind energy is influenced by seasonal and atmospheric conditions. These fluctuations require sophisticated grid management to ensure a stable electricity supply.

Flexible loads help balance supply and demand, especially as renewable energy increases. Many utility companies operate demand response programs. These programs range from interruptible service programs targeting large commercial and industrial customers, to rebates given to large customers for voluntary peak hour load reductions, to direct control of water heaters and air-conditioning compressors (Ryan Hledik A. R., 2022). Utilities such as Duke Energy (2024a), Xcel Energy (2024), and Pacific Gas and Electric Company (2024) enable demand response through time-based pricing where electricity rates vary by the time of day. By charging higher rates during peak hours and lower rates at other times, customers are encouraged to shift their usage to off-peak hours.

Residential buildings account for a substantial share of U.S. electricity demand, representing 38% of the total electricity consumption in 2023 (EIA, 2024), making this sector the largest of all sectors. Demand flexibility within the residential building stock is crucial to achieving demand-side load flexibility, which can help balance grid loads. Within the residential sector, most load flexibility potential is found within the heating and cooling end uses because of the inherent thermal energy storage capability of the building's heated or cooled mass.

This measure models the use of smart thermostats to achieve demand flexibility in residential buildings by controlling their heating and cooling systems. This document describes the technology in general, describes how it is modeled in ResStock ™ , and reviews results at a high level.
