## 2.2.2  Variable-Speed GHP Heat Pump Performance Curves

To model variable-speed GHPs with

Coil:Cooling:WaterToAirHeatPump:VariableSpeedEquationFit and Coil:Heating:WaterToAirHeatPump:VariableSpeedEquationFit EnergyPlus objects, several different performance curves are required. At both part load and full load, for both heating and cooling, curves are required for:

- The efficiency of the GHP as a function of water and air temperature (biquadratic)
- The capacity of the GHP as a function of water and air temperature (biquadratic)
- The efficiency of the GHP as a function of airflow fraction (quadratic or cubic)
- The capacity of the GHP as a function of airflow fraction (quadratic or cubic)
- The efficiency of the GHP as a function of water flow fraction (quadratic or cubic)
- The capacity of the GHP as a function of water flow fraction (quadratic or cubic).

The EnergyPlus auxiliary program HVAC Performance Curve Fit Tool was used to develop the biquadratic curves for capacity and EIR as a function of air temperature and water temperature. 8 The tool is designed for direct expansion coil performance curves, but considering the similarity of the curve requirements between these two coils, it is reasonable to use the same tool for GHPs.

After generating coefficients with the spreadsheet-based tool from EnergyPlus, we compared the original manufacturer catalog data with the curve-fit data to ensure the correctness of the curve coefficients. These comparisons are documented in the appendix.

Curves here were generated based on data for a variable-speed WaterFurnace 7 Series 3-ton GHP unit (WaterFurnace SDW7-0018W 9 ). This unit comes from a major U.S. manufacturer and includes data in a format that is easy to translate into EnergyPlus inputs. Although this is a fully variable-speed unit, the specification sheet for this unit only includes the performance of the unit at full-load and part-load conditions, similar to what is shown for two-stage units. Although the model can handle additional speeds, specification sheets for products that included additional speeds did not have all the details necessary to translate into the required EnergyPlus inputs. Curves for the unit performance were derived in a similar manner as for the two-stage unit, and the overall equations for efficiency and capacity as a function of water and air temperature have the same form as what it used for the two-stage 10 and single-stage units. 11 As a result, only two sets of curves, for full and part load, are shown here. Unlike the two-stage coil, a part-load curve is not applied when the load is less than the part-load conditions because the equipment is assumed to be able to correctly modulate to very low part-load ratios, whereas a two-stage system might be cycling during low loads. The model interpolates when operating between full load and part load in a way that can account for the real variable-speed performance. The pump used here is also assumed to be truly variable speed, so water flow rates will vary proportionally to the load rather than running at fixed low and high speeds, like the two-stage equipment.

Quadratic curves for the impact of airflow and water flow on efficiency and capacity can be generated by fitting directly to the published data of a product at rated conditions. Because these curves are simpler and comprise fewer points, a simple quadratic regression can yield a good fit for each curve. Cubic fits could be used if they yield better results, but they would require additional data of a shape where the impact would be meaningfully affected. Variable-speed equipment also sees a wider range of water and airflow conditions during operation because it modulates to match the load, rather than cycling at low loads.
