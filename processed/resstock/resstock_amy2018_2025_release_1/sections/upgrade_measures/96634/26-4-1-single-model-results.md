## 4.1  Single Model Results

This section analyzes three example dwellings that received upgrades. The upgrades had the following building characteristics, shown in Table 4.

Table 4. Single Model Types

| Building Geometry         | IECC Climate Zone, 2004   | State   | Heating Type   | HVAC Heating Efficiency   |
|---------------------------|---------------------------|---------|----------------|---------------------------|
| Single-Family Detached    | 5A                        | MA      | Fuel Oil       | Fuel Boiler, 76% AFUE     |
| Multifamily With 5+ Units | 3A                        | GA      | Electricity    | ASHP, SEER 13, 7.7 HSPF   |
| Single-Family Detached    | 4A                        | IL      | Natural Gas    | Fuel Furnace, 60% AFUE    |

This section focuses on the percentage energy reduction achieved by the upgrade. The annual energy consumption of the dwellings before and after the upgrades are shown below.

Figure 1: horizontal stacked bar chart for an example dwelling in Massachusetts (Climate Zone 5A). X-axis is annual energy consumption in MBtu, 0 to 160; bars are Baseline and Natural Gas Furnace, stacked by Others (all fuels), natural gas heating, heating fans and pumps, cooling, and cooling fans and pumps. Baseline is 139.1 MBtu of Others; after the upgrade Others falls to 61.9 and natural gas heating adds 77.5, leaving the total nearly unchanged.

Figure 1 shows a slight increase in energy consumption, from 146.9 MBtu in the baseline to 147.6 MBtu after receiving a natural gas furnace, showing how fuel switching from fuel oil to natural gas can have a small effect on annual energy consumption.

Figure 2: horizontal stacked bar chart for an example dwelling in Georgia (Climate Zone 3A). X-axis is annual energy consumption in MBtu, 0 to 7, with the same end-use series as Figure 1. Baseline shows 3.1 MBtu of Others, 2.6 of cooling and 0.6 of cooling fans; with the natural gas furnace, Others is 2.2 and natural gas heating 1.4, and cooling is gone, so the total falls by roughly 40%.

In Figure 2, cooling is removed from the dwelling entirely after the upgrade because the original heating system is a heat pump, consuming 2.6 MBtu in the baseline and 0 after the upgrade. The removal of cooling when dwellings use heat pump technologies to heat and cool is a limitation of this methodology, discussed in later sections as well.

![Stacked bar of annual energy use for an Illinois example unit, baseline vs natural gas furnace](96634_images/image_000004_2c3b121e9c2ef0367fa54f730d30f013447b6cc26b593c0ce3e2d6ed6880d74c.png)

Figure 3: horizontal stacked bar chart for an example dwelling in Illinois (Climate Zone 4A). X-axis is annual energy consumption in MBtu, 0 to 140, with the same end-use series as Figure 1. Others is 27.2 MBtu in both bars; natural gas heating is the largest segment and shrinks with the upgrade, taking the total from about 123 to about 94 MBtu. Heating fan energy falls from 1.3 to 1.0.

The application of the upgrade shown in Figure 3 represents a significant amount of savings in a dwelling heated with a 60% AFUE fuel furnace. Before the upgrade, the dwelling consumed 122.8 MBtu and dropped to 93.8 MBtu after the upgrade, where the majority of energy savings are sourced from natural gas heating.
