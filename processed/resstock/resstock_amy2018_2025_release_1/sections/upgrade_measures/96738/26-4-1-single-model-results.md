## 4.1  Single Model Results

This section analyzes three example dwelling units that received advanced air sealing upgrades. Prior to the upgrades, each unit had air leakage rate of 50 ACH50 (Unit 1), 25 ACH50 (Unit 2), and 6 ACH50 (Unit 3). The upgrade reduced air leakage to 5 ACH50 for all units. The units are in Hawai ' i (climate zone 1A), Texas (climate zone 3A), and Alaska (climate zone 8AK). These represent a range of climate conditions. The floor area, heating setpoint, and cooling setpoint of these three dwelling units are different, so baseline performance across units is not comparable. This section focuses solely on the percentage energy reduction achieved by the advanced air sealing. The modeled annual energy consumption of the three example dwelling units before and after advanced air sealing upgrades are described below.

Figure 3 shows that the advanced air sealing upgrade led to modeled site energy savings for the example dwelling unit in Hawai ' i (Climate Zone 1A), where cooling dominates total consumption. Cooling energy use (including cooling and fan/pumps) decreased from 38.0 MBtu to 27.5 MBtu, a 27.6% reduction. Heating-related end uses decreased from 0.08 million BTU to 0.01 million BTU, an 87.5%. This low amount of heating is expected in a warm, tropical climate where outdoor temperatures rarely fall below heating setpoints, and many homes either lack heating systems or use them infrequently. In Hawai ' i, 85% of homes do not have heating equipment [17]. Note that the site energy consumption and the change heating fans/pumps energy between the baseline and the upgrade in the dwelling unit are negligible.

Figure 4 shows results for an example dwelling unit in Texas (climate zone 3A), which uses electricity for both heating and cooling. The advanced air sealing upgrade resulted in modeled site energy reductions across several heating- and cooling-related end uses, including heating, cooling, heating and cooling fan/pumps, heating heat pump backup, and associated backup fan/pumps. The cooling energy consumption was reduced from 36.8 MBtu to 32.0 MBtu, a 13% reduction. Additionally, the total heating energy consumption (heating, heating fan/pumps, heat pump backup, and associated fan/pumps) was reduced from 14.4 MBtu to 9.3 MBtu, a 35% reduction.

Figure 5 shows that the example dwelling unit in Alaska (climate zone 8AK) uses fuel oil for heating and has no cooling system modeled. This is typical for homes in cold climates where summer temperatures are mild and mechanical cooling is uncommon. Its modeled total heating site energy use (heating and heating fan/pumps) was reduced from 167.9 MBtu to 161.7 MBtu, representing a 4% reduction. While this may seem modest compared to reductions in warmer climates, it reflects several important factors. This smaller savings is expected because the unit started at the minimum eligible infiltration rate of 6 ACH50, leaving limited room for improvement (to 5 ACH50). Additionally, cold climate homes like this often already have tighter envelopes (Figure 2) or efficient heating systems. These factors reduce the marginal benefit of advanced air sealing. Nevertheless, this unit may not fully represent the broader zone 8 stock, where many homes may have lower or higher leakage and therefore show lower or greater savings potential.
