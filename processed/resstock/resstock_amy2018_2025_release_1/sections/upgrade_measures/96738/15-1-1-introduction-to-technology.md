## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

Air sealing is a method used to reduce the amount of unwanted air that leaks in and out of a building through small gaps, cracks, and openings in the walls, floors, ceilings, and around windows and doors. These leaks allow outside air to enter the home and conditioned air to escape, which can lead to higher energy bills, drafts, uneven temperatures, indoor air quality concerns, and moisture issues [3], [4]. Studies show that air leakage can account for up to 30% of a home's heating and cooling costs [5]. By sealing these gaps, air sealing helps lower energy use, improves comfort by reducing drafts, supports better indoor air quality by keeping pollutants and allergens out, and protects the building's structure by reducing moisture intrusion [4]. Common air sealing techniques include: weatherstripping around doors and operable windows; caulking around stationary components such as window frames, baseboards, and wall penetrations; foam sealants or spray foam for larger gaps around plumbing penetrations, recessed lighting, and attic hatches; and airtight electrical boxes or gaskets around outlets and switches [3], [6]. In new construction, house wrap (a breathable material installed under exterior siding) is often used to limit air infiltration while allowing moisture to escape. In most climates, vapor retarders may be installed to manage indoor moisture and prevent condensation within walls [5]. However, this measure upgrade is only concerned with existing homes, not new construction.

| Technique | Typical Application | Notes/Performance Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Aerosol sealing | Whole-home air sealing (walls, ceilings, etc.); more common in new construction | Delivers sealant as fine mist to seal leaks; shown to achieve high reductions in ACH₅₀ [7] |
| Weatherstripping | Around doors and operable windows | Reduces drafts; easy to install and cost-effective |
| Caulking (includes mastic, and high temperature rated sealants) | Cracks around window frames, rim joists, baseboards, small attic penetrations, gaps around heat sources, etc. | Best for small gaps <¼ inch; silicone or acrylic latex types commonly used |
| Foam sealants (cans) | Large gaps around pipes, vents, attic hatches, attic penetrations, and rim joists | Expands to fill irregular gaps; provides insulation value as well |
| Spray foam (closed-cell) | Rim joists, attic penetrations | Offers both air sealing and insulation |
| Airtight electrical boxes | Behind outlets and switches on exterior walls | Prevents air leakage through walls; used with gaskets |
| House wrap | New construction beneath siding; retrofit when combined with insulated siding or exterior rigid foam | Blocks air while allowing moisture escape |
| Vapor retarders | Inside wall assemblies in most U.S. climates | Controls moisture diffusion; not for air sealing per se |
