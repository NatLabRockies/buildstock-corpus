## 4.3  Stock Energy Impacts

Out of 550,000 modeled units (i.e., the total number of units modeled in the ResStock 2025.1 dataset to represent the national building stock), 507,100 units are applicable for the advanced air sealing upgrade (92.2%). For the 507,100 applicable units, the total weighted baseline modeled site energy consumption is 3,140 TWh per year. After applying the air sealing upgrade, the total modeled site energy consumption was reduced to 2,632 TWh per year, a 16.2% reduction. Figure 8 shows that site energy savings from air sealing primarily come from heating and cooling related end uses, with 27.1% electricity heating savings (64.2 TWh), 29.9% natural gas heating savings (318 TWh), 7.8% electricity cooling savings (27.1 TWh), and 3.8% cooling fan and pump energy savings (2.18 GWh). Energy savings were observed only in heating and cooling, while other end uses like lighting and appliances remained unchanged. This is because air sealing improves building performance by reducing air leakage. Thus, its impact is confined to systems that respond to indoor temperature fluctuations.

Site energy savings in space heating is greater than cooling. This is because the temperature difference (ΔT) between the indoor and outdoor environment is typically much smaller during the cooling season than during the heating season. This is one of the reasons why heating loads are often greater than cooling loads. For example, on a hot summer day, an indoor temperature of 72°F and an outdoor temperature of 95°F represents a ΔT of 23°F. On a cold winter day, an indoor temperature of 72°F and outdoor temperature of 25°F results in a ΔT of 47°F , approximately double the magnitude. Since the energy lost due to infiltration is directly proportional to ΔT, due to the HVAC systems conditioning the infiltrating outdoor air to the indoor setpoints, heating seasons experience higher infiltration-driven losses. This pattern aligns with typical climate conditions across the United States, where most regions have higher heating degree days than cooling degree days. Therefore, the dominant share of energy savings from advanced air sealing upgrades is expected to occur during the heating season, particularly in colder climates with leakier building envelopes and extended periods of high ΔT.

After the advanced air sealing upgrade, the stock average consumption is reduced to 20,440 kWh per unit per year, a 16.2% reduction (Figure 9). Similar to Section 4.2, only heating and cooling energy use decreased, while lighting, appliances, and other end uses were unaffected.

Figure 10 shows a general trend that baseline annual site energy consumption increases with higher initial infiltration rates. For instance, units with 40 ACH50 consume 14.3% more energy on average (26,929 kWh/unit/year) than those with 10 ACH50 (23,087 kWh/unit/year). As shown in Figure 11, upgrading all units to a target infiltration rate of 5 ACH50 results in the greatest energy savings for the leakiest units. For example, units starting at 40 ACH50 save 8,386 kWh/unit/year, while those at 10 ACH50 save 1,864 kWh/unit/year. This is expected, as units with higher air leakage have greater uncontrolled heat loss or gain, which increases HVAC runtime. Thus, reducing infiltration in these leakier homes improves thermal retention and reduces HVAC runtime more than in tighter units.

Figure 12 shows that across the 507,100 applicable dwelling units, older units tend to consume more site energy than newer construction. Where the units built before the 1970s typically consume over 25,000 kWh/unit/year on average, compared to around 22,000 kWh/unit/year or less for units built after the 1970s. There are a few exceptions to these trends. For instance, 1950s units have a slightly higher average baseline consumption (30,382 kWh/unit/year) than 1940s units (30,189 kWh/unit/year), which reflects variations in other building characteristics such as size, layout, or HVAC system performance. Nonetheless, older vintages consistently show the greatest absolute and relative energy savings from air sealing upgrades. As shown in Figure 13, upgrading all applicable units to a target infiltration rate of 5 ACH50 results in greater absolute energy savings for older buildings. For example, pre-1960s units save over 6,000 kWh/unit/year, while 2010s units only save 1,600 kWh/unit/year. These differences are primarily due to the higher baseline infiltration rate in older units, which offer more potential for improvement through air sealing.

Figure 14 shows that older units experience the highest percent site energy savings. For example, pre-1940s homes see a 21.4% reduction in energy use, 1940s units 20.0%, 1950s 19.9%, and 1960s 16.1%. The percentage savings continues to decline with newer vintages such as 15.8% for 1970s, 13.8% for 1980s, 11.2% for 1990s, 9.4% for 2000s, and 9.1% for 2010s units. These trends are driven by differences in baseline infiltration levels, as illustrated in Figure 1. Older units tend to have higher air leakage rates, which present greater opportunities for energy savings through air sealing. By improving the building enclosure, air sealing reduces uncontrolled air exchange and lowers HVAC energy demand, especially in older units.

Figure 18 shows that heating savings consistently exceed cooling savings across all zones. This is because heating tends to occur during periods with large temperature differences between indoor and outdoor air, which intensifies infiltration-driven losses. In contrast, cooling seasons are shorter, and indoor-outdoor temperature differences are smaller. Baseline infiltration also plays a key role in explaining energy savings patterns. As shown in Figure 2, many zones, such as 1A, 4C, and 5A, exhibit a wide range of initial infiltration levels, meaning some homes are leakier than others. Leakier homes benefit more from envelope tightening. This results in higher energy savings after air sealing. In contrast, tighter-construction zones like 2B and 3B have lower baseline infiltration rates and less room for improvement. This results in both lower energy consumption and more modest savings from air sealing upgrades.
