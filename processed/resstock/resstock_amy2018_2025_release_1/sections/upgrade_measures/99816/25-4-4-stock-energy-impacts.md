## 4.4  Stock Energy Impacts

Out of 550,000 dwelling models (i.e., the total number of units modeled in the ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset to represent the national building stock), 426,800 models (77.6%) are applicable for the light air sealing upgrade. For the 426,800 applicable models, the total weighted baseline modeled site energy consumption is 2,664 TWh per year. After applying the light air sealing upgrade, the total modeled site consumption was reduced to 2,498 TWh per year, a 6.2% reduction. Figure 3 shows that energy savings from light air sealing primarily come from heating and cooling end uses, with 11% natural gas heating savings (104 TWh), 10% electricity heating savings (21 TWh), 11% fuel oil heating savings (15 TWh), 11% propane heating savings (11 TWh), and 3% electricity cooling savings (8 TWh). Other end uses like lighting and appliances remained unchanged or very minimally impacted.

Reasoning for these results was provided in previous air sealing work (Phoung, Lou and White 2026) and is restated here. Air sealing improves building performance by reducing air leakage. Thus, its impact is confined to systems that respond to indoor temperature fluctuations. The heating and cooling energy savings can be attributed to reduced air infiltration, which limits the amount of cold or hot outdoor air entering the building envelope during the heating or cooling season, respectively. This leads to lower heating and cooling loads, reduced runtime for space heating and cooling systems, and associated reductions in fan and pump operation.

Figure 5 shows a general trend that baseline annual energy consumption increases with higher initial infiltration rates. As shown in Figure 6, reducing infiltration rates by 25% results in the greatest energy savings for the leakiest units. For example, units starting at 40 ACH50 save 9% site energy, while those starting at 10 ACH50 save 4% site energy. This is expected, as units with higher air leakage have greater uncontrolled heat loss or gain, which increases HVAC runtime. In addition, units that start with higher infiltration have more opportunity for savings because they started with higher heating and cooling consumption.

Figure 7 shows that across the applicable dwelling units, older units tend to consume more energy on average than newer construction. Where the units built before the 1970s typically consume over 25,000 kWh/unit/year on average, units built after the 1970s average 22,000 kWh/unit/year or less. As shown in Figure 8, reducing infiltration rates by 25% results in greater absolute energy savings for older buildings on average. For example, the average pre-1970s unit saves over 1,500 kWh/unit/year, while newer units see average savings of 1,000 kWh/unit/year or less. These differences are primarily due to the higher baseline infiltration rate in older units, which offer more potential for improvement through air sealing.

Figure 9 shows that average baseline annual energy consumption generally increases as we move from warmer to colder climate zones. For example, climate zones 5A, 6A, and 7A exhibit the highest average consumption due to long heating seasons and large indoor-outdoor temperature differences. In contrast, warmer zones like 1-3 have lower energy demand due to milder winters. Energy consumption of the average dwelling unit in each region is influenced by the temperature classification (1 through 8), moisture classification (A, B, and C), and many other factors, therefore the relationship between energy consumption and climate zone is not perfectly correlated.

As shown in Figure 10, greater average annual energy savings are generally achieved in colder climates, particularly zones 7A, 6A, and 5A. These cold climate zones start with higher average consumption and therefore have more opportunity for savings through air sealing. Figure 11 further confirms these trends, with the highest percent savings observed in 7A (9%), 6A (8%), and 5A (7%). This is primarily due to large heating loads in these climates and the greater temperature differentials that drive infiltration losses.

Climate zones with moisture classification A (moist) typically exhibit higher energy savings compared to climate zones with moisture classifications B (dry) or C (marine). In moist climates, infiltration air contains more humidity, requiring HVAC systems to expend additional energy to remove both sensible and latent heat. In contrast, dry climates have minimal latent loads, and marine climates generally experience more moderate temperatures, resulting in lower overall energy use associated with conditioning infiltration air. Therefore, improving infiltration will have greater effect in moist climates and demonstrate higher savings.

Figure 12 shows that heating savings consistently exceed cooling savings across most climate zones, except for climate zone 1A. Infiltration has much greater effect on heating loads because the temperature difference between the outside and indoor air is typically much higher during winter (e.g., 20°F outdoor vs. 70°F indoor during winter, compared with 90°F outdoor vs. 70°F indoor during summer). Therefore, an air sealing upgrade is expected to have much higher effect on heating energy consumption.
