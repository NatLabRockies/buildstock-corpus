## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

Fuel-fired furnaces -including propane, fuel oil, and natural gas -are used to heat indoor environments. Fuel ignites in the burner, and the flame heats a metal heat exchanger. A blower fan moves air over the heat exchangers, which warms the air. Most furnaces use ducts to distribute heat, while combustion exhaust is vented outside. In comparison, gravity furnaces do exist but are older and not as common; as such in this study, we only model forced air furnaces.

Fuel-fired wall or floor furnaces are directly installed within walls or floors to serve one or a few rooms. They burn natural gas, propane, or fuel oil in a combustion chamber, transferring heat through a heat exchanger. Heated air rises by convection into occupied spaces, and combustion byproducts should be vented outside the dwelling. Unlike central furnaces, they typically do not use ducts and instead provide localized heating.

Electric furnaces use resistance heating elements rather than combustion to produce heat. When an electric current passes through the elements, they generate heat, which is transferred to air moving across them. A blower fan distributes this heated air through ducts.

Fuel boilers heat water instead of air. Natural gas, propane, or fuel oil is burned in a combustion chamber to heat water. The hot water (or steam in some systems) is then circulated through pipes to radiators, baseboard heaters, or radiant floor systems. Boilers can provide both space heating and domestic hot water. Combustion gases are vented outside the building.

Central air conditioners typically use the same duct network as the heating system. They remove heat from buildings by operating a vapor-compression cycle: an outdoor unit compresses and condenses refrigerant, which then absorbs heat from indoor air as it passes over an evaporator coil. A blower fan circulates the cooled air through the ducts, and the absorbed heat is expelled outdoors; system efficiency is measured by the seasonal energy efficiency ratio, version 2 (SEER2), with higher ratings indicating more efficient cooling.

Room air conditioners are self-contained cooling units designed to cool a single room or small area. They are typically mounted in windows or through walls and operate using a vaporcompression cycle: they draw in warm indoor air, cool it by passing it over the evaporator coil, return it to the room, and expel heat outside. Room air conditioners are rated by their combined energy efficiency ratio (CEER).

Air-source heat pumps (ASHPs) provide both heating and cooling using a reversible vaporcompression cycle. In cooling mode, they operate like a central air conditioner, transferring heat from indoors to outdoors, but in heating mode, the cycle reverses and they extract heat from outdoor air and deliver it indoors. Even at cold temperatures, heat pumps can capture usable energy from the outdoor air. Distribution is typically through the same duct network used for cooling, though ductless systems deliver conditioned air directly to rooms. Efficiency is rated using heating seasonal performance factor, version 2 (HSPF2) for heating and SEER2 for cooling.
