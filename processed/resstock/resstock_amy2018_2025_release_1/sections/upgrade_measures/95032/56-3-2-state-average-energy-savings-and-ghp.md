## 3.2 State Average Energy Savings and GHP Efficiency

Figure 22 shows the average cooling energy savings between the baseline and upgrade scenarios in different states in the contiguous United States. Note that savings can be negative, sometimes substantially, in cases where a substantial portion of the housing stock did not have cooling in the baseline. In the results below many units in Vermont, Maine, New Hampshire, Wyoming, and Montana do not have cooling or do not cool their entire floor area. The adoption of GHPs adds cooling and conditioning to the entire floor area, resulting in a cooling energy increase. Similarly, Washington and California see an increase in cooling load due to a large portion of the dwelling units being in the Marine IECC climate zone, where many homes do not have cooling or do not cool their entire floor area.

In states where cooling loads are substantial and the majority of the state has cooling in the baseline, such as most of the southeastern U.S., average cooling energy savings of 20% -30% are possible across the stock. For states with negative savings, there is a substantial comfort benefit to cooling the entire space. This benefit will become even more substantial with the projected rise in cooling degree days in the coming decades (EPA 2024).

Figure 23 shows the heating energy savings from installing GHPs across the country. Unlike cooling, nearly every home in the contiguous United States has a heating system, so significant savings are seen in all states. The vast majority of homes also use fuel-fired heating systems with a much lower efficiency than the average efficiency of a GHP, so more substantial savings are possible than for cooling. Savings are smallest in the southeastern U.S., which also has some of the lowest heating loads in the country.

Figure 24 shows the average cooling COP for the single-stage GHP. In this context, COP is the ratio between cooling load delivered and the electricity consumed by the cooling unit and the air handler fan to meet that cooling load. This is not to be confused with the rated COP of the system. The COP being reported is the state average simulated COP and does not include duct losses, which may be substantial. Cooling efficiency is highest in heating-dominated states, particularly in the upper Midwest, which have cooler ground temperatures in summer. However, these states also have the lowest cooling loads, so the overall savings are more modest in these locations. Cooling COPs range from 2.9 to 3.7 at the state level, with almost all states achieving a COP over 3. Note that individual buildings within each state may have higher or lower COPs than the state average due to differences in climate within a state and other factors.

Figure 25 shows the average heating COP for the single-stage GHP. The COP does not include duct losses, which may be substantial, particularly in existing homes where ducts are often located in unconditioned spaces and are not upgraded as part of the GHP measure. These losses can significantly degrade overall system performance relative to the rated equipment COP. The reported COPs focus solely on the efficiency of the equipment itself.

Observed heating COPs range between 2.0 and 2.4 across climate zones, with lower values in the coldest states. Colder regions not only experience lower ground temperatures (which reduce heat pump efficiency due to decreased entering water temperatures), but also tend to have higher annual heating loads, further stressing the system. Additionally, these COP values reflect simulated operation conditions that diverge from the idealized lab test settings under which manufacturers report rated COPs. In practice, GHPs often operate at part-load conditions where cycling is frequent, especially during shoulder seasons. This introduces cycling losses not captured in steady-state ratings.

Despite these inefficiencies, GHPs still deliver substantial energy savings over conventional combustion systems. Even high-efficiency condensing furnaces top out at annual fuel utilization efficiencies (AFUEs) around 0.96, and many existing systems-especially older, non-condensing furnaces-operate in the range of 0.6 to 0.8. Therefore, even a GHP with a field COP in the 2.0 to 2.4 range offers a significant performance improvement over a typical fossil fuel heating system.
