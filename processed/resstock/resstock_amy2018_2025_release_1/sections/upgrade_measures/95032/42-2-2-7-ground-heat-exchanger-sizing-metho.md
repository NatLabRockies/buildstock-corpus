## 2.2.7  Ground Heat Exchanger Sizing Methodology

Several software tools (Table 4) have emerged based on the need for a way to optimize ground heat exchanger design to minimize the size required to meet building loads and perform satisfactorily over the life span of the system. The general design approach involves the designer specifying ground thermal properties, number and arrangement of boreholes, the heat pump loads (building loads or the loads on the ground heat exchanger), and the in-borehole design of the ground heat exchanger. Design methodologies involve specifying ground heat exchanger size directly based on longer-term building loads estimation using peak design conditions (design days) or determining the ground heat exchanger size using finer time-series resolution (often from monthly or hourly building loads data). The iterative approach uses an initial ground heat exchanger size determination using borehole constraints and heat pump specifications guided by the building loads, then iteratively adjusts the ground heat exchanger size until it meets the user-specified design criteria for the maximum and minimum entering fluid temperatures.

Ground heat exchanger sizing design practices or methods are often categorized based on the time resolution into five levels from level zero to four (L0 to L4) (Spitler and Javed 2016). The level zero (L0) practice is to use rules of thumb relating ground heat exchanger length to building peak heating or cooling loads or installed equipment capacity. The L0 tools are applicable to only smaller systems and heating-only applications (J. D. Spitler 2016). However, peak heating and cooling loads are variable year to year and do not show a clear correlation with annual heating and cooling loads, resulting in highly variable entering fluid temperature values across different years. Because of this limitation, several other techniques have been developed by researchers over the years that involve 'pulses' of heating and cooling loads.
