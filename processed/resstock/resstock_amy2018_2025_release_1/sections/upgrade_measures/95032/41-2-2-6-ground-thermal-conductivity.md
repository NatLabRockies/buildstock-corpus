## 2.2.6  Ground Thermal Conductivity

With the new ability to specify the thermal conductivity of the ground, 20 better guidance is required to help users pick the right properties for their job site. However, substantial variability in ground conductivity exists by location, time of year, and depth due to differences in the local soil makeup and the presence or absence of groundwater. Many GHP manufacturers recommend performing a site-specific conductivity measurement when considering installing a GHP, with details on how to take this measurement often appearing in product manuals. The default assumption at the start of this project for ground thermal conductivity is to use a value of 1.0 Btu/h-ft-°F. However, there are resources available that can help users make an educated guess at the soil type for a location, absent a more thorough site analysis, such as (X. W. Liu 2016) or (U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) 2019). The USDA data provides conductivity by soil and moisture type. Liu (2016) provides climate zone 50 th percentile (median), 25 th percentile (lower quartile), and 75 th percentile (upper quartile) estimates based on a natural gas well dataset from Southern Methodist University (Southern Methodist University n.d.). While the Southern Methodist University natural gas well dataset is measurements of deep well data, it was the best identified dataset that provides distributions of measurements within the range of expected values of various soil types from the USDA dataset.

The USDA dataset of soil thermal conductivities by climate zone is missing the conductivities for some climate zones, such as 7AK and 8AK. The Southern Methodist University data were combined with USDA data to get the most complete dataset possible. The USDA dataset was sorted by counties and aggregated to the respective IECC 2012 climate zones, then the upper quartile, lower quartile, and median values were computed for each of the missing climate zones. The resulting quartile values were repeated for some climate zones, as multiple sites had identical conductivity values across quartiles. For example, climate zone 1A had the same values of soil conductivity for the lower quartile, median, and the upper quartile. Climate zone 7AK had identical computed values for the lower quartile and median, and climate zone 8AK had identical computed values for the median and upper quartile. The latitudes and longitudes in the Southern Methodist University file were joined to the corresponding IECC 2012 climate zone. These data were then used to create distributions for ground conductivity by climate zone (Figure 12). Due to limitation of samples in climate zone 1A, the climate zone data were combined with samples from Florida in climate zone 2A. Discrete bins of ground conductivity values were created in ResStock. 21 These bins are [0.5-2.6] Btu/h-ft-°F with a step size of 0.3 Btu/h-ft-°F. Updating the default values to vary nationally will allow for a better estimate of the soil conductivity, absent additional information.

Figure 13 shows the median ground thermal conductivity by county modeled in ResStock and informed by the Southern Methodist University dataset. Dwellings with a thermal conductivity below 1.4 W/m-K are deemed ineligible for the GHP upgrade. Figure B-1 in Appendix B displays the count of units by county that are deemed ineligible for the GHP upgrade due to low ground thermal conductivity. Most of the ineligible dwelling units are located in Mountain West and West Coast regions.
