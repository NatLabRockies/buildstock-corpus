## 4.2.1  Annual Energy

By applying a high-efficiency tankless natural gas water heater to all homes eligible for such an upgrade-55.7% of the housing stock-the modeled overnight savings potential is estimated to be nearly 118 TWh in total site energy, with 60% of the savings from natural gas and 36% from electricity, as shown in Figure 3. Figure 4-Figure 8 present the measure impacts for the upgraded and occupied dwellings only, which represent 50% of the stock. Vacant units are excluded because they do not have occupant-driven consumption to illustrate realistic energy savings.

Figure 4 compares the modeled energy end use breakdown of the average home before and after the water heater upgrade. The modeled average energy savings is 1,560 kWh per year, or 5.1% of the total site energy consumption. The savings primarily come from hot water energy reduction, with the electricity, propane, and fuel oil used for hot water reduced to zero due to the fuel switching and the natural gas for hot water reduced by 42% due to replacing the existing gas water heaters with more efficient models. This means that the overall savings from switching all the natural gas storage water heaters (76%) in the housing stock to higher-efficiency condensing gas tankless water heaters more than offsets the natural gas increase from converting the rest of the water heaters (24%)-which are electric resistance, propane, and fuel oil water heaters-to natural gas. This highlights the energy efficiency potential for gas tankless water heater adoption at scale, although the technology does not lead to energy savings for all the dwelling units, as shown in the next few figures.

On average, the gas water heater upgrade slightly decreased space cooling and space heating. Decreasing space heating can be attributed to the average being skewed by a handful of dwelling units with large space heating savings from the upgrade. The impact of the upgrade on space heating can be twofold and in opposite directions. First, space heating slightly increases when the upgrade removes the tank loss from the incumbent water heater located in a conditioned space or other spaces adjacent to the thermal envelope, such as a garage. Most dwellings have this impact, and it explains why most gas-heated dwellings have a net increase in space heating gas energy after the upgrade, as shown in Figure 5. Second, when the upgrade removes the atmospheric flue vent from the conditioned space, infiltration is reduced, and space heating and cooling decrease. This applies to dwellings whose water heater has an atmospheric flue vent in the conditioned space, but their heating system does not, either because it is high efficiency, with an AFUE of at least 89%, or it is located outside the conditioned space. In Figure 5, dwellings to the right of the y-axis have net gas heating savings because they have one such heating system.

Figure 6 compares the range of modeled energy savings by the baseline water heater fuel type. All fuel-based water heaters see energy savings from the upgrade that are primarily due to the efficiency gain. More than half of the electric (resistance) water heaters have a small net increase in total site energy consumption that is primarily due to an increase in space heating. Because the electric resistance water heaters are similar in overall efficiency (0.92-0.99 EF, depending on type) to the condensing gas tankless water heater upgrade (0.98 UEF), the impact of the upgrade is more dependent on the secondary effect on heating, ventilation, and air conditioning. Additionally, unlike with fuel water heaters, replacing electric water heaters does not change any existing flue vent for the heating system, so space heating will either increase or stay the same.

Across climate zones, absolute energy savings from the upgrade generally increase with colder climates, where the mains water temperature is lower, and therefore the overall water heating energy demand is higher when holding the hot water usage constant. But in relative terms, the energy savings do not significantly vary, with the median percent savings between 4% and 9%, as shown in Figure 7. This is because the modeled water heater efficiency stays constant at 90% regardless of hot water usage or temperature rise, as shown in Section 4.1.  When both the total energy and energy savings increase in a similar proportion with climate zone, the resultant percent change stays relatively the same.

Similarly, the variation in the median percent energy savings across the number of occupants in a household is not very substantial, as shown in Figure 8, because both the hot water use and the total energy consumption are correlated with household size. The range of relative savings is higher for smaller households because their total energy consumption can more significantly vary with the ownership of appliances in the smaller dwelling units. As the number of occupants increases, so does the floor area, and the total energy consumption becomes increasingly dominated by space heating and cooling, and therefore it is increasingly correlated with occupants.
