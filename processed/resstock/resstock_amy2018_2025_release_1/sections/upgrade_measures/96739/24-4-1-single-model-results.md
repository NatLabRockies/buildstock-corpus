## 4.1  Single Model Results

This section analyzes three example dwelling units that received attic floor insulation (insulation ceiling) upgrades. These three example units represent a range of climate conditions. The floor area of these three dwelling units is different. Furthermore, the heating and cooling setpoint temperature is also different. As a result, the baseline heating and cooling energy consumption can vary across the three example dwelling units, appearing either high or low. This section does not compare absolute energy consumption among these three example dwellings. Instead, it focuses solely on the percentage energy reduction achieved by the attic floor insulation upgrade. The annual energy consumption of three example dwelling units was evaluated before and after attic floor insulation upgrade. The upgrade resulted in reductions across several heating- and cooling-related end uses, including heating, cooling, heating and cooling fan/pumps, heating heat pump backup, and associated backup fan/pumps. Results are described as follows:

Dwelling unit 1 had baseline attic insulation of R-13 and was upgraded to R-49. It is located in Florida (climate zone 1A), a hot-humid region with high cooling loads and minimal heating demand). Figure 3 shows that this dwelling uses electricity for both heating and cooling. Total cooling energy consumption, including cooling and associated fan/pump energy, was reduced from 32.5 MBtu to 31.3 MBtu, a 3.7% reduction. This moderate reduction aligns with expectations for climate zone 1A, where peak summer cooling loads dominate annual HVAC use. Since attic insulation helps reduce heat gain through the roof, the upgrade improves comfort and reduces energy use, even though the baseline insulation was already moderate. The limited heating season in Florida explains why heating savings were negligible or not observed. This case is a reasonable representation of attic insulation effects in hot-humid climates.

Dwelling unit 2 had baseline attic insulation of R-7 and was upgraded to R-60. It is located in Texas (climate zone 3A), a mixed-humid climate with both heating and cooling needs. Figure 4 shows that the unit uses electricity for both heating and cooling. Cooling energy consumption dropped from 36.6 MBtu to 33.2 MBtu, a 9.3% reduction. Heating energy use, including all associated HVAC and fan/pump loads, was reduced from 14.3 MBtu to 11.1 MBtu, a 28.8%

reduction. These results are consistent with expectations for a mixed climate. The heating savings reflect the low baseline insulation (R-7), which allowed reduction in conductive heat loss during the winter. Meanwhile, cooling savings occurred due to reduced heat gain in summer. The dual-season benefits make this example unit a good representation of attic insulation impacts in mixed-humid zones like 3A.

Dwelling unit 3 had baseline attic insulation of R-38 and was upgraded to R-60. It is located in Alaska (climate zone 8AK), the coldest U.S. climate zone, where heating dominates building energy use, and cooling is negligible. Figure 5 shows that this dwelling uses fuel oil for space heating and does not have mechanical cooling. Heating energy use (including fan/pumps) dropped from 56.5 MBtu to 54.1 MBtu, a 4% reduction. The modest savings are expected because the home already had relatively high baseline insulation (R-38), leaving less room for improvement. However, the small but measurable reduction still reflects improved thermal resistance and reduced heat loss through the attic in an extremely cold climate. This example demonstrates diminishing returns for insulation upgrades in homes that are already well-insulated but also highlights the importance of maintaining high performance in severe climates like zone 8AK.
