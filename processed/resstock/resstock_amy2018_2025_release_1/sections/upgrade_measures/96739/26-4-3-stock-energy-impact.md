## 4.3  Stock Energy Impact

Out of 550,000 units, 235,950 units are applicable for attic floor insulation upgrades (42.9%). For the 235,950 applicable units, the total baseline consumption is 3,454 TWh per year. After the attic floor insulation upgrade, the total consumption is reduced to 3,377 TWh per year or a 2.2% reduction. Figure 8 shows that energy savings from attic floor insulation primarily come from heating and cooling related end uses, with 3.9% electricity heating savings (10 TWh), 3.7% natural gas heating savings (42 TWh), 2.4% electricity cooling savings (9.4 B kWh), and 2.4% cooling fan and pump energy savings (2.0 TWh). Energy savings were observed only in heating and cooling end uses, while other end uses like lighting and appliances remained unchanged. This is because attic insulation upgrades affect thermal performance by reducing heat loss in winter and heat gain in summer, which directly impacts space conditioning loads. Savings from heating is higher than cooling because heating seasons are generally longer and more severe than cooling seasons across most U.S. climate zones.

For the 235,950 applicable units, the average baseline consumption is 24,732 kWh per unit per year. After the attic floor insulation upgrade, the stock average consumption is reduced to 24,187

kWh per unit per year, or a 2.2% reduction (Figure 9). Similar to Section 4.2, energy savings were observed only in heating and cooling end uses, while other end uses like lighting and appliances remained unchanged.

Figure 10 shows that across the 235,950 applicable units, average annual energy consumption varies by existing attic floor insulation levels. For example, units with no insulation consume 36,617 kWh/unit/year, compared to 33,848 kWh/unit/year for R-7, 31,466 kWh/unit/year for R13, and 37,543 kWh/unit/year for R-49. These variations reflect differences in other characteristics such as floor areas, vintages, climate zone, and other characteristics beyond attic floor insulation.

Figure 10. Annual energy consumption by insulation for baseline and attic floor insulation upgrade

![Image](96739_images/image_000011_7be44784ab5279fd00694b351b6402522aa1cbd33dfcf82cabaf04f28e67452a.png)

As shown in Figure 11, when upgraded to R-60 (climate zones 2-8) or R-49 (zone 1), units with the lowest baseline R-values see the highest savings: 9,581 kWh/unit/year for uninsulated attics and 3,223 kWh/unit/year for R-7. In contrast, units with R-13 and R-19 save only 1,808 kWh/unit/year and 1,290 kWh/unit/year, respectively. These results show smaller incremental energy savings from additional insulation, since homes with minimal baseline insulation experience substantial reductions in heat loss and HVAC demand when upgraded, while alreadyinsulated homes see smaller additional gains.

Figure 12 shows that across the 235,950 applicable units, older homes generally consume more energy than newer construction. For instance, the average baseline energy use is 33,415 kWh/unit/year for pre-1940s units, followed by 30,386 kWh for 1940s units, and 22,446 kWh for 1970s units. In contrast, homes built in the 1980s or later tend to have lower energy use: ranging from 20,970 kWh for 1990s units to 19,024 kWh for 2010s units. There are a few exceptions to this general pattern. For example, some 1950s homes show slightly higher baseline consumption than 1940s units, likely due to variations in floor area, occupant behavior, or HVAC system efficiency. Despite these variations, older homes consistently benefit most from attic floor insulation upgrades, as described below.

Figure 12. Annual energy consumption by vintage for baseline and attic floor insulation upgrade

![Image](96739_images/image_000013_2b87be43349c38b73385e33e75e9f8d4e14d6ed64735ce16e3be2c9c6f00d002.png)

As shown in Figure 13, upgrading attic floor insulation to R-60 (climate zones 2-8) or R-49 (zone 1) results in greater energy savings for older homes. For example, pre-1940s homes save an average of 885 kWh/unit/year, and 1940s units save 973 kWh. In contrast, 1990s and 2000s homes save only 306 kWh and 289 kWh, respectively. Figure 14 shows a similar trend in percent savings: 2.6% for 1940s homes versus just above 1.4% for homes built in the 1990s and after. These patterns are largely due to lower baseline insulation levels in older homes, which often have R-values below R-19 or are uninsulated. In comparison, newer homes typically start at R30 or higher, limiting the potential for improvement (Figure 1).

Figure 15 shows that average baseline energy consumption increases with climate severity, especially in colder zones. Climate zones 7A (39,168 kWh/unit/year), 6A (35,242 kWh), and 5A (33,805 kWh) exhibit the highest consumption due to long heating seasons and greater indooroutdoor temperature differences. In contrast, warmer zones such as 1A (17,990 kWh), 2B (17,281 kWh), and 3B (14,978 kWh) have lower energy use, reflecting milder winters and minimal heating demand. Mixed and marine zones like 4C (20,065 kWh) and 3C (14,425 kWh) fall in between. Figure 16 shows that attic floor insulation upgrades result in the highest absolute energy savings in cold and mixed-humid climates. The greatest savings occur in zone zones 5A (738 kWh), followed by zones 5A (738 kWh), 3A (719 kWh), 4B (688 kWh/unit/year), and 6B (513 kWh). In contrast, warmer zones like 1A (125 kWh) and 2B (260 kWh) see lower savings due to smaller heating loads and more consistent baseline insulation.

As shown in Figure 17, percent savings are highest in zones 3C (2.6%), 4B (3.0%), and 3A (3.4%). Despite high baseline consumption, zones 6A (1.6%) and 7A (1.5%) show relatively low percent savings, likely due to higher starting insulation levels, which reduce marginal gains. Similarly, hot climates such as 1A (1.0%) and 2B (1.5%) show limited savings due to minimal heating needs. These trends correspond with baseline attic insulation levels by climate zone (Figure 2). Zones like 1A, 3A, and 4B exhibit wide variability, including homes with low or no insulation (e.g., R-13, R-19, or uninsulated), creating greater upgrade potential. In contrast, zones 6B, 7A, and 8A tend to have higher, more uniform starting R-values, often R-30 or greater, leaving less room for improvement. Overall, attic insulation upgrades are most effective in climates with substantial heating demand and low baseline insulation.
