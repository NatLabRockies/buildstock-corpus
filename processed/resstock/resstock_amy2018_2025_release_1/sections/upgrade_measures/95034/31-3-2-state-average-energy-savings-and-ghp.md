## 3.2  State Average Energy Savings and GHP Efficiency

Figure 28 shows the cooling energy savings for homes receiving the upgrade over the baseline by state for the contiguous United States. Vermont has the least savings, largely due to the low amount of cooling equipment present there in the baseline. Negative savings, or an increase in cooling energy, happens in several colder climate zones, notably in northern New England, where cooling equipment in the baseline is less common. Note that fewer states have negative savings when using a two-stage GHP than when using a single-stage GHP due to the higher efficiency of the two-stage equipment.

Figure 28. Annual cooling energy savings for a two-stage GHP over the baseline

![Image](95034_images/image_000023_0ec920bcc1f52a82ec346b06086d812453b50ad48fc17ba7971eeb64188b95fb.png)

Figure 29 shows the heating energy savings from installing a two-stage GHP compared to the baseline. In general, the Southeast exhibits smaller savings percentages than the rest of the contiguous United States. This is because many homes in this region already use electric heating systems (such as electric resistance or heat pumps) in the baseline scenario. In contrast, much of the rest of the country relies on combustion-based HVAC systems, which have inherent heat losses due to exhaust from combustion products. Georgia shows relatively higher heating savings as a percentage because the Atlanta area (where a large portion of the state's residential stock is located) primarily uses natural gas for heating. The range of cooling energy savings is narrower than the range of heating energy savings. This is partly because nearly every home already has some form of heating in the baseline.

Figure 29. Annual heating energy savings for a two-stage GHP over the baseline

![Image](95034_images/image_000024_795e41b9ca10e3824b22e4ecec25f62622eca5bc7bdc162bf6e59c8e78acaafe.png)

Figure 30 shows the simulated heating COP by state for homes that receive a two-stage GHP upgrade. Efficiency is highest in the hottest climate zones, which have the warmest ground temperature during winter but also the lowest loads, so they may not save as much energy on an absolute basis. All states have a high average simulated heating COP, notably higher than the single-stage GHP case, with the minimum state average simulated COP of 2.7 in North Dakota. Note that individual buildings may have lower COPs than the state average and that duct losses may reduce the overall efficiency of heating and cooling beyond the COP of the equipment shown here.

Figure 30. Average simulated heating COP of a two-stage GHP by state

![Image](95034_images/image_000025_6e26a8e03955c3e0bdf834784f53ca331c62eeda1e107e87c2dd355a7b32af4c.png)

Figure 31 shows the average cooling COP in each state for homes that receive a two-stage GHP upgrade. This COP is highest in cold climate zones, with the highest values seen in Idaho, Montana, North Dakota, and Wyoming. Like with heating, the highest efficiency occurs in the states with the lowest overall load, and the two-stage GHP systems are consistently more efficient than single-stage GHPs. Cooling COP varies more than heating COP across states, but the lowest cooling COP is close to the highest COP seen during heating. Average COPs seen here are also higher than the average COP of air conditioners in the baseline case as shown by the savings seen in Figure 29, indicating that GHPs could lead to substantial savings for the overall stock for both heating and cooling.
