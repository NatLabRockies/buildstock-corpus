## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit end-use load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to energy efficiency retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in (Reyna, et al. 2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles (https://www.nlr.gov/buildings/end-use-load-profiles.html) project.

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure-cold climate heat pump upgrade.

The cold climate heat pump upgrade demonstrated 30% total site energy savings (3,535 TBtu) for the U.S. residential building stock (Figure 7). These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 53.7% stock total heating savings (3,173.8 TBtu)
- 10.1% stock total cooling savings (132.7 TBtu).

## 1  Technology Summary

Cold climate heat pumps are air-source heat pumps (ASHPs) that are designed to work efficiently at cold temperatures. This section will give an overview of ASHP technology and include details about the specific features of the cold climate ASHP included in this measure upgrade.

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

ASHPs provide space heating and cooling in buildings. According to the 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey from the U.S. Energy Information Administration, only ~16.5% of dwellings in the contiguous United States have ASHP systems, with most ASHPs residing in warmer climate regions like the southern United States (U.S. Energy Information Administration 2022).

ASHPs transfer heat from outdoor air to indoor air during heating operation and from indoor to outdoor during cooling operation. During heating, an ASHP's compressor powers a vapor compression cycle that sends heat to the conditioned space of the dwelling. Unlike fossil fuel combustion or electric resistance heating, ASHPs can heat dwellings at an efficiency level over 100%. For example, average heating coefficients of performance (COPs)-the ratio of heating delivered divided by energy consumed-vary from ~2.0 in colder climates to ~3.0 in hotter climates for the ducted ENERGY STAR ®  air-to-air heat pump upgrade (Praprost, et al. 2026). For reference, an electric resistance heater heats at a COP of 1.0.

When an ASHP's compressor cannot meet the heating load of a dwelling, its backup heating system will turn on, if one exists, and aid in meeting the load. Backup heating systems can be either integrated into the ASHP or separate. Integrated backup heating systems use electric resistance heating or ducted fossil fuel furnaces. Separate backup heating systems can use either fossil fuel or electricity to help meet the load and can include a wide range of heating technologies.

The intent of this ASHP measure upgrade is to reflect the specifications of a typical cold climate air-source heat pump (termed 'ccASHP' by the Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships [NEEP]) based on the median metrics for ccASHPs in the NEEP cold climate ASHP database (Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships, Inc. 2023). NEEP requires heat pumps to have a variable-speed compressor to be noted as a ccASHP. More information on the cold climate designation can be found in NEEP's product list documentation. Section 1.2. describes this selection process in more detail (Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships, Inc. 2023).

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

This ASHP option reflects an installation of a ducted ASHP into a building that already has existing ductwork for either heating or cooling. This entails the ASHP indoor coil and blower being installed with the dwelling's existing air distribution system and an outdoor unit coil, fan, and compressor unit being installed outside.

## 1.2.1  Compressor Speed

This ASHP option has a variable-speed compressor, because virtually all cold climate ASHPs on the market include variable-speed compressors. Variable-speed compressors allow the system to optimize energy consumption by ramping the compressor speed up or down depending on the indoor and outdoor conditions. Compared to a single-stage or two-stage options, this ASHP can more efficiently operate at extreme conditions because of the multiple speeds available to the compressor.

## 1.2.2  Efficiency Ratings: HSPF2 and SEER2

This ASHP option has efficiency ratings based on the Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2 (SEER2) rating for cooling and the Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2 (HSPF2) for heating. These ratings are calculated by taking the ratio of the cooling or heating output (Btu) of the system over the electricity consumed (Wh) over the respective heating or cooling season. SEER2 and HSPF2 were introduced in 2023 to replace the energy efficiency metrics of SEER and HSPF. The new metrics are measured with an increased external static pressure by up to five times to reflect the field conditions more accurately (Department of Energy 2025). HSPF2 can be calculated for several different regions to reflect the distribution of heating load hours at different outdoor air temperatures in different climates (Department of Energy 2025). Ratings for energy conservation standards use Region IV, so it is most common. However, Region V HSPF2s are often provided for cold climate ASHPs in addition to Region IV. These regions are as defined by the AHRI 210/240 Performance Rating of Unitary Air-condition and Air-source Heat Pump Equipment documents (AHRI 2024).

This measure upgrade's ASHP has a SEER2 of 17.5 and an HSPF2 (Region IV) of 8.5. These efficiency ratings were chosen to reflect median typical cold climate ASHPs on the NEEP database. These values are not representative of the ASHP's efficiencies at all temperatures, but an average of the efficiency over the respective cooling and heating season. The instantaneous efficiency of the cold climate ASHP will vary according to outdoor and indoor conditions.

## 1.2.3  Sizing Methodology

This heat pump measure upgrade is sized to 'max load'. Sizing to max load involves sizing the ASHP to the maximum calculated load between the cooling and heating loads. This methodology considers the capacity degradation of the ASHP so that it is sized large enough to meet both the heating and cooling loads at design conditions. For example, a dwelling in a cold climate will have its ASHP rated capacity at 47°F be larger than its load at design conditions, since its capacity retention will not be 100% at the design temperature of the heating load.

Max load is chosen for this ASHP option because it has a variable-speed compressor that more effectively meets the heating load at low ambient temperatures. This effectiveness helps reduce the total size of the ASHP in cold climates from being unrealistically large. Additionally, the multiple speeds available for this ASHP allow it to avoid elevated indoor humidity due to short cycling issues that single-stage compressors incur.

Because this ASHP option is sized to max load, applicable dwellings in cold climates will receive ASHPs that are relatively larger than an existing fossil fuel system. Large ASHP sizing can increase the cost of the ASHP installation and require multiple mounted indoor units in the dwelling and possibly multiple outdoor units. However, because of this sizing and the variable-speed compressor, its backup system utilization will be much lower, leading to lower operational energy use.

## 1.2.4 Capacity Retention

As outdoor temperature drops below the rated capacity temperature of 47°F, the available capacity of the ASHP decreases. The capacity that is retained by an ASHP below this temperature can be defined by its capacity retention fraction. At 5°F, this ASHP option's capacity retention fraction at the compressor's highest speed is 0.665. This capacity retention factor specifies the fraction of the ASHP's rated capacity available at 5°F. In the case of this ASHP, its rated capacity at 47°F will be reduced by 33.5% at its lowest and highest compressor speeds, respectively.

## 1.2.5  Backup System

This ASHP option has an integrated electric resistance backup system. This backup system will turn on when the ASHP's compressor cannot meet the heating load of the dwelling unit. Below -15°F, the compressor will be locked out from providing heat, and only the backup system will be used if it remains below -15°F. This compressor lockout temperature is set at -15°F because of the variable-speed compressor's ability to effectively cover heating loads at low ambient temperatures. These two systems can operate coincidentally because the backup heating coil is in sequence with the ASHP heating coil. The airflow can initially be heated by the ASHP coil and then subsequently boosted by the backup heating coil. The backup system is sized according to the 'emergency' choice in OpenStudio ® -HPXML, which means the backup capacity will be sized to meet the ACCA Manual J heating design load. During operation in the EnergyPlus ® model, the software assumes a fully modulating backup coil that will perfectly meet the remaining heating load not met by the ASHP.

Because the backup system is electric resistance heat, there is a significant efficiency drop when it is utilized, as the COP of electric resistance heat is 1.0. This drop leads to higher energy consumption and higher instantaneous power demand by the dwelling unit's heating system.

## 1.2.6 Cooling Levels

Dwelling units that are not 100% cooled by their existing AC system (e.g., room AC providing cooling for just one room of a home) will be 100% cooled by this measure upgrade. This will increase cooling energy consumption of those dwelling units that were not already 100% cooled. The cooling level increase for all applicable dwelling units is implemented because ASHPs in this measure upgrade will be sized as large as the duct system allows to attempt to cool the entire dwelling unit. Although this increases energy use associated with cooling, it also increases health and comfort of the home.

## 1.2.7 Electrical Requirement

This subsection summarizes the electrical information of the typical cold climate heat pump upgrade. Electrical information is used in ResStock to assess whether the home's electrical panel would become space-constrained or over-capacity due to this upgrade. Section 3.3 describes the electrical panel constraint modeling in more details.

The power rating of a ducted heat pump system is the sum of the power rating of the compressor, the air handler, and any electric backup allowed to operate simultaneously, which are summarized in Table 1. The heat pump compressor power rating is estimated based on its design heating capacity using a regression equation derived from product labels. The air handler power rating is estimated from the design airflow rate and OS-HPXML assumption of fan power. The electric resistance backup power rating is directly converted from its heating capacity at an efficiency of 100%. For more information on the modeling assumptions, see the ResStock Technical Reference Documentation (Reyna, et al. 2025).

Table 1 . Default Power Rating of Ducted Heat Pump System Components

| Component            | Power Rating [W]                                   | Note                                                          |
|----------------------|----------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Heat Pump Compressor | (0.631 * Heating_Capacity [kBtu/hr] + 1.615) * 240 | Regression of Minimum Circuit Amp, multiplied by voltage      |
| Air Handler          | Air_Flow [cfm] * Fan_Power [W/cfm]                 | Default to OS-HPXML assumptions                               |
| Electric Backup      | Heating_Capacity [kBtu/h] * 293.07                 | Convert heating capacity directly to power at 100% efficiency |

Ducted heat pumps require at least one 208/240V circuit, each of which is protected by a double-pole breaker. The specific number of circuits depend on whether the heat pump has backup heating (dual-fuel or electric) and how its indoor and outdoor units are configured along with the backup. Table 2 summarizes the wiring configuration for ducted heat pumps assumed in ResStock. For example, a dual-fuel heat pump is assumed to have a 120V air handler on one circuit and another 240V circuit for the compressor. For heat pump with electric backup, the air handler is commonly 240V to allow for the strip heat to be on the same circuit. This is also the assumption in ResStock. The maximum overcurrent protection allowed per circuit is assumed to be up to 50A. Therefore, more circuits may be required for larger heat pump systems, which correspond to the use of multiple compressors and/or heat kits in actual installed systems.

While a heat pump upgrade replaces both heating and cooling, it may take more circuits and breaker spaces to install than removing the incumbent HVAC systems is able to free up. The heat pump upgrade may also add more connected load to the panel, especially when the backup is sized for emergency use. The power rating of heat pump systems is driven by their heating configuration and the presence of electric resistance backup. Therefore, homes in colder climates are more likely to face a capacity constraint when upgrading to a heat pump since a backup is often required to supplement the heat pump performance and they are more likely to have fuel heating previously.

## 1.2.8  Measure Upgrade Specification Process

The intent of this ASHP measure upgrade is to reflect the specifications of a typical cold climate ASHP based on the median cold climate ASHP found on the NEEP database (Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships, Inc. 2023). The NEEP database specifies an ASHP as cold climate if its HSPF2 ≥ 7.7 and SEER2 ≥ 14.3, it is variable speed, and its COP at 5°F ≥ 1.75 at maximum capacity. In an analysis of all ducted ASHP systems on NEEP, the plurality of systems have an HSPF2 between 8.5 and 9.0. Figure 1 shows the histogram of this analysis (Winkler, et al. 2026).

## 2  Applicability

The cold climate ASHP measure upgrade applies to all dwelling units with the following:

- Dwelling units with ducts that meet the following criteria:
- o Have a non-shared HVAC system unless there is evidence of an existing ASHP or central AC condenser (i.e., the existing dwelling unit has space for a condenser; this is a consideration for multifamily dwelling units only)
- o Have a heating fuel that is not classified as 'Wood,' 'None,' or 'Other Fuel.'

Note that this upgrade explicitly is not applied to buildings without heating in the baseline. With these applicability constraints, the measure upgrade applies to 78.0% of the existing building stock.

Applicability of this measure upgrade is based on ResStock housing characteristics that determine the available options and options' details. Specifically, the 'HVAC Heating Efficiency' characteristic in ResStock is used to apply this ASHP option. 'HVAC Has Ducts' describes the presence of ducts in the dwelling. 'Heating Fuel' describes the baseline heating fuel of the dwelling. 'HVAC Cooling Type' describes the type of cooling system the dwelling possesses. 'HVAC Shared Efficiencies' describes the shared HVAC system of the dwelling, if applicable. More information on the ResStock housing characteristics can be found in the ResStock Technical Reference Documentation. The exact logic used in the ResStock project YML file for this dataset is available in the appendix. The remainder of this section provides discussion for why certain apply logic decisions were made.

## 2.1  Treatment of Multifamily Dwellings With Shared HVAC Systems

Multifamily dwellings with ducts and shared HVAC systems will only receive this upgrade if the existing dwelling already has space for an outdoor unit. This is checked by looking at the HVAC cooling type and the HVAC shared system type. If the HVAC cooling type for a ducted dwelling with a shared HVAC system is 'Ducted Heat Pump' or 'Non-Ducted Heat Pump,' the measure upgrade will apply. If the HVAC cooling type for a ducted dwelling with a shared HVAC System is 'Central AC,' the measure upgrade will apply if the shared HVAC system is not a Fan Coil Cooling unit. All other ducted dwellings with shared HVAC systems that do not meet the above criteria will not receive the measure upgrade. This logic is developed to reflect the ease of install of a ducted ASHP system, which requires an outdoor space to install the condenser unit.

## 2.2  Cooling-Level Increase for All Applicable Dwellings

Dwellings that are not 100% cooled by their existing AC system (e.g., room AC providing cooling for just one room of a home) will be 100% cooled by this measure upgrade. This could increase cooling energy consumption of those dwellings that were not already 100% cooled. There are rare circumstances where partially cooled dwellings in hot climates receive a heat pump that is not sized large enough to cover the cooling load. This is because the ducts, previously only used for heating, are not large enough to accommodate an adequate size of the heat pump to cool the entire dwelling.

## 2.3  Exclusion of 'Other Fuel,' 'None,' and 'Wood' Heating Fuels

Dwellings with 'Other Fuel,' 'None,' or 'Wood' heating fuels do not receive this measure upgrade, because retrofits to these dwellings can lead to confusing results (e.g., false negative savings compared to baseline or installing heating in regions that do not need it). 'Other Fuel' includes fuels that are not currently represented in ResStock, so there is not an accurate baseline heating energy consumption to compare to. Additionally, there is not a large impact at scale because dwellings with 'Other Fuel,' 'None,' or 'Wood' heating fuels comprise approximately 2.6% of the total residential building stock.

## 3  Modeling Approach

This section will overview how the upgrade measure is functionally modeled via ResStock into OpenStudio-HPXML. Section 3.1 will briefly cover how OpenStudio-HPXML is used by ResStock to model the dwellings generated by ResStock's sampling. Section 3.2 will cover how inputs from ResStock and default values calculated by OpenStudio-HPXML create the typical cold climate heat pump measure upgrade.

## 3.1  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

The ResStock building stock energy model contains conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that are representative of the U.S. residential building stock (Reyna, et al. 2025). These distributions are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each dwelling model in the stock has an assigned representative option for each characteristic. ResStock utilizes the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio and EnergyPlus through the use of an HPXML file. For reference, the state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository when the ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset was created can be found here. Assumptions about the measure upgrades in a ResStock dataset release that are not explicitly defined in ResStock are taken care of in the BuildResidentialHPXML measure.

In the case of heating/cooling upgrades in ResStock Dataset 2025 Release 1, these assumptions are all HVAC related. This documentation covers the calculations directly related to the heat pump, but HVAC controls (e.g., setpoints, cycling) and HVAC distribution (e.g., airflow systems) are out of its scope. For information on how OpenStudio-HPXML handles controls in ResStock Dataset 2025 Release 1 see here, and for how it handles distribution see here. The following subsections will go through the ResStock inputs and the defaults calculated by BuildResidentialHPXML for the heat pump upgrade discussed in this documentation. Most defaults highlighted from the BuildResidentialHPXML measure stem from the apply\_hvac(), apply\_hvac\_sizing(), and apply\_detailed\_performance\_data() methods found in OpenStudio-HPXML's hpxml\_defaults.rb file. Note: the defaults.rb file calls methods from various Ruby files within the directory it resides. See these Ruby files here.

## 3.3  Electrical Panels

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025. As new electric loads are added into the home during an improvement project, the electrical panel may not have enough capacity or space to accommodate these new circuits. Thus, the panel may become constrained, requiring an electrical upgrade that may include upsizing the service entrance. Panel constraints can also be mitigated in other ways without the panel or service being upsized, by using load shedding devices, circuit sharing devices, or a smart panel to allow more loads to be connected; low-power, combination, or smart appliances to reduce electric loads at the source; slim tandem breakers or subpanels for more space; and more (Armstrong, et al. 2022). The panel constraint estimate from the upgrade measure allows for the costing of any potential electrical upgrade or mitigation, thus helping to capture the total cost of upgrade more holistically across the housing stock.

Panel capacity and space constraints are modeled using a service load and a breaker space calculation, respectively. The load calculation is based on 2023 National Electrical Code's load-based method in Section 220 (National Fire Protection Association 2023), which guides how the load diversity should be estimated and summed to estimate the total load connected to the panel. When this total load converted to ampacity exceeds the service rating of the panel, the panel is considered capacity-constrained in ResStock. Similarly, the panel is considered space-constrained when the total occupied breaker space counted from the bottom-up exceeds the rated space count of the panel. The panel's capacity and space ratings are probabilistically sampled based on other housing characteristics such as heating fuel and the presence of certain appliances. Users are encouraged to reevaluate these constraint metrics according to other considerations, such as a margin of safety to account for uncertainty in the modeling process. For more details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al. 2025).

## 3.4  Modeling Limitations

Ducted heat pump units run their indoor fan during defrost. This would create a 'cold blow' that would be objectionable to some homeowners. To avoid this, the backup heat is brought on to temper the air and avoid discomfort. This results in backup heating usage even at mild temperatures. This backup heating is not presently modeled in EnergyPlus. The cooling delivered during defrost is offset by additional heat pump heating if the building load does not exceed the heat pump capacity after defrost degradation penalties. This limitation causes lower estimates for backup heating (COP = 1.0) in simulations because the heat pump compressor (COP &gt; 1.0) is being modeled to offset the defrost cooling.

Additionally, some dwellings that receive this heat pump will not have a duct system that is sized large enough for the heat pump's capacity. ResStock resizes ducts in the dwelling to accommodate the new heat pump upgrade.

## 4.4  Electrical Panel Constraints

Figure 13 shows the distribution of modeled panel constraints overall (see 'all' column) and by baseline heating fuel. It is estimated that 44.8% of dwellings upgrading to the typical cold climate heat pump face either a breaker space or a capacity constraint (or both) while 55.2% have no constraints. Most dwellings that face a panel constraint are those that do not have electricity as their baseline heating fuel (i.e., fossil fuel heated dwellings).

All post-upgrade constraint statistics include a minute fraction of homes that are already capacity-constrained in the baseline. Prior to the upgrade, 2.3% of the existing panels are estimated to already be over-capacity, which is possible as the capacity constraint is estimated based on code. In contrast, no existing panel is space-constrained, which makes sense as it is physically impossible to connect more loads than there is space. Capacity constraints in the existing stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. However, it is important to recognize that this level of uncertainty applies to all panel results, including those that look reasonable. Hence, the capacity-constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on. During the cold climate ASHP upgrade, any homes already constrained before the upgrade will likely need to address their capacity issue to be permitted by code to connect a new load to the panel, whether the upgrade is making the constraint worse or not. Thus, the post-upgrade constraint statistics can be attributed entirely to the upgrade. Users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose pre-existing capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.
