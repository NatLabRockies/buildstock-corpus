## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact, market-ready (or nearly marketready) upgrades. ' Measures ' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An ' end-use savings shape ' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock ™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson et al, 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape upgrade -Natural Gas Furnace with 95% Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency.

## 1 Technology Summary

This work examines the technical potential for replacing existing heating systems with natural gas furnaces in homes with air ducts across the U.S. residential building stock. Natural gas furnaces are a type of heating system that combust natural gas to produce hot air. Natural gas furnaces distribute this heat through ducts systems to warm indoor spaces, and compose 47.7% of space heating technologies in the United States. The purpose of this upgrade is to provide a comparison point for futures where residential households adopt natural gas furnaces, in order to estimate potential energy and bill changes.

In cases where the heating system also provides air conditioning, like a heat pump, the air conditioning is removed from the dwelling unit.

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

Natural gas furnaces are used to heat indoor environments. The natural gas is ignited in the burner, and the flame heats a metal heat exchanger that the exhaust gas flows through. For forced-air systems, the majority of furnaces, a blower fan moves air over the heat exchangers, which warms the air. Forced-air natural gas furnaces use ducts to distribute heat, while combustion exhaust is vented outside.

## 1.2  Efficiency and System Types

Efficiency rated by annual fuel utilization efficiency (AFUE), capacity, and system types are key features of natural gas furnaces. Other technical upgrades are important to the function and performance of natural gas furnaces as well.

## 1.2.1 Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency

AFUE is the standard rated efficiency for how efficiently a furnace converts fuel into heat at rated conditions. The higher the AFUE rating, the more efficient the system is at converting fuel into usable heat. Energy lost through exhaust gases, radiation, and other components decreases efficiency.

AFUE is the total annual heat output divided by the total annual fuel input -on a scale of 0% to 100%. Note that unlike SEER2 and HSPF2, 1  AFUE does not include the electricity used by the air handler blower, combustion blower, ignition system, or controls. ANSI/ASHRAE 103-2022 provides a procedure for determining AFUE of residential central furnaces and boilers, which is a test cycle structured to simulate ordinary heating conditions throughout the year -including seasons (ANSI/ASHRAE 2022) .

'Residential furnaces manufactured and distributed in commerce, as defined by 42 U.S.C. 6291(16) must meet the energy conservation standards specified in the Code of Federal Regulations at 10 CFR 430.32,' (DOE 2025). 80% AFUE furnaces are the minimum for nonweatherized gas furnaces (i.e., natural gas and propane) manufactured from 2015 through Dec. 18, 2028. After Dec. 18, 2028, the minimum furnace is 95% AFUE. If an investigator would like to understand equipment standard changes over time, a useful approach could blend data from this study with data from the Propane and Fuel Oil Furnaces upgrade (Stenger, Liu, White, et al. 2025) and Reference Space Heating and Cooling (circa 2025) (Stenger, Liu, Wilson, et al. 2025), both of which model 95% AFUE for propane and heating and cooling minimum, respectively. In this study, we model a 95% AFUE natural gas furnace.

1 SEER stands for seasonal energy efficiency ratio, and HSPF stands for heating seasonal performance factor.

## 1.2.2  System Types

Natural gas furnaces can be condensing or noncondensing (sometimes called conventional). Condensing furnaces have high-efficiency units that have a second heat exchanger used to recover latent heat from flue gases and can reach an AFUE between 90% and 99%, according to the Air-Conditioning, Heating and Refrigeration Institute (AHRI) (AHRI 2025). A secondary heat exchanger condenses hot exhaust gases -extracting more remaining heat -resulting in a condensate and requiring a condensate drain line as well as a condensate pump if a floor drain is not available nearby. Condensing furnaces exhaust at lower temperatures of around 100° -150°F and mostly vent horizontally through polyvinyl chloride (PVC) ductwork as opposed to a metal vertical vent commonly used for noncondensing furnaces. Noncondensing furnaces often have an AFUE up to 80% and do not have a secondary heat exchanger, yielding exhaust that leaves at high temperatures -up to 350°F. Since noncondensing furnaces have steel vertical vents, it is important to avoid any condensation of the flue gas in the vent because the condensate is slightly acidic and can corrode the vent over time. A noncondensing furnace often has a lower upfront cost (because of fewer components), but higher operating costs (because of lower efficiencies). In this study, we model condensing furnaces as an upgrade.

## 1.2.3 Other Technical Specifications

A prerequisite for natural gas furnaces is the access to natural gas distribution. In this upgrade, we assume that natural gas is available to homes regardless of prior heating fuel, geography, or other physical infrastructures.

## 2 Applicability

This upgrade applies only to dwellings with existing ducted systems. The choice to apply this upgrade to homes without current access to natural gas was made to evaluate the theoretical impacts of switching to this fuel. Whether or not installing new gas distribution to supply existing homes is financially or technically feasible is outside the scope of this document.

However, this upgrade could be used as part of an analysis into the financial viability of installing new gas distribution and the benefits-costs to households. The upgrade replaces the existing heating system with a 95% AFUE natural gas furnace. If a dwelling unit that has a heating system that also provides air conditioning, like a heat pump, the air conditioning is removed as part of the upgrade. No changes to infiltration are assumed as part of the flue removal or installation process.

Approximately 74.3% of the residential building stock meets the conditions for this upgrade. Details on the application logic are in Table 1.

## 3 Modeling Approach

This section outlines how a universal natural gas furnace upgrade is modeled in ResStock using the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow. In this scenario, every dwelling unit with a ducted heating system is assigned a natural gas furnace, regardless of its original heating system. ResStock uses probabilistic sampling logic based on national housing stock data to assign each dwelling unit a set of baseline building characteristics, including existing heating fuel, system type, and duct configuration. For this upgrade, the workflow overrides the existing heating system and fuel type with a centrally defined natural gas furnace characterized by a specified AFUE. The furnace is modeled as a forced-air system with ducts, and the energy performance is governed by its AFUE rating, which reflects seasonal fuel-to-heat conversion efficiency. The model assumes consistent operation patterns across households and scales furnace capacity based on heating load requirements determined by building envelope characteristics and climate zone. While equipment brand and installation quality are not modeled explicitly, the total heating energy demand and fuel consumption are recalculated to reflect the standardized furnace and fuel use. This upgrade represents a scenario where dwellings with ducted systems could convert to natural gas furnaces, allowing for assessment of energy and infrastructure implications at scale.

The probability distributions are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each sample has an assigned option for each characteristic for each dwelling unit to be simulated. ResStock utilizes the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio ®  and EnergyPlus ®  through the use of an HPXML file (HPXML 2025). For reference, the state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository when the ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset was created can be found here. Assumptions about the airsource heat pump (ASHP) upgrades that are not explicitly defined in ResStock are taken care of in the BuildResidentialHPXML upgrade.

## 3.1  Natural Gas Furnace

In ResStock, building characteristics are translated to OpenStudio-HPXML parameters. Upgrades, such as switching all ducted homes to natural gas furnaces, are modeled as changes to baseline building characteristics. This section outlines the technical details of how a universal natural gas furnace upgrade is implemented in the ResStock workflow.

## 3.1.1  ResStock Inputs

This scenario assumes every household with existing ductwork receives a natural gas furnace, regardless of its original fuel type. The upgrade logic identifies all ducted homes and overrides their baseline heating system with a natural gas forced-air furnace of a defined AFUE, typically representing a modern, high-efficiency unit. The change is reflected in the OpenStudio-HPXML model via updates to fuel type, heating system type, and efficiency parameters. Heating capacity is sized based on Air Conditioning Contractors of America (ACCA) Manual J (ACCA 2016) and Manual S (ACCA 2014) calculations for the respective climate zone and building envelope. The model presumes standard furnace operation and does not include installation-specific variation, instead relying on the AFUE to govern thermal performance. This upgrade scenario enables simulation of the broad-scale impact of transitioning ducted homes to a single fossil fuel heating pathway, including effects on energy use and utility bills.

## 3.1.2 ResStock Inputs for the Natural Gas Furnace

A central natural gas furnace combusts natural gas to heat indoor air, distributing it via the home's duct system. In HPXML, each furnace is represented under :

## 4.5  Electric Panel Impacts

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025 to assess whether the existing panel has enough capacity or breaker space to accommodate new loads from an energy retrofit. For details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna et al, 2025).

Upgrading to a 95% AFUE natural gas furnace generally has no impact on the electrical panel, as no new electrical loads or circuits are being added. Most homes receiving this measure are replacing an existing fuel furnace. However, for the small fraction of homes previously heated by electrically (e.g., by an electric resistance furnace or a ducted heat pump), this measure can free up panel capacity and breaker space by removing those circuits and loads from the panel.

Any panel constraints appearing in the upgrade results reflect pre-existing conditions, as this measure does not worsen them. 2.3% of the existing panels are estimated to already exceed capacity based on code. Pre-existing capacity constraints are an artifact of the panel modeling process, in which panel service ratings are estimated from selected housing variables rather than detailed inventory data. While anecdotal evidence suggests these constraints can occur in practice -manifesting as flickering lights or tripping breakers -the lack of empirical data prevents us from confirming their prevalence.
