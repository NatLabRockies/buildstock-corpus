## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit end-use load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil).

An ' end-use savings shape ' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. This results in a time-series profile broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock ™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model is intended to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. The methodology and results of the baseline model are discussed in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project 1  (Wilson, et al. 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: advanced air sealing with drill-and-fill wall insulation.

The advanced air sealing with drill-and-fill wall insulation upgrade demonstrated 22% total site energy savings for the U.S. residential building stock (see Figure 3 for more details). These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 32% stock electricity heating savings
- 37% stock natural gas heating savings
- 39% stock fuel oil heating savings
- 32% stock propane heating savings
- 11% stock electric cooling savings.

## 1 Technology Summary

## 1.1  Introduction to Drill-and-Fill Insulation

Drill-and-fill insulation is a method used to insulate existing walls. The most common drill-and-fill insulation materials are cellulose, fiberglass, and spray foam. For cellulose and fiberglass, there are two main types of drill-and-fill installation: loose fill (also known as blown-in) and dense pack, which requires professional equipment (North 2012). This measure upgrade models cellulose as the drill-and-fill insulation material.

Drill-and-fill insulation can be installed from either the inside or outside of the building. In multifamily buildings, drilling from the outside is more common because it allows occupants to remain in place during the work is being performed, and reduces asbestos risk for installers (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency 2011) (Antonopoulos, et al. 2019). For these reasons, external drill-and-fill will be the focus of this upgrade. Drilling from the interior may be preferable in certain situations, such as inclement weather, when siding is difficult to remove, or when the home is vacant. In a drill-and-fill retrofit, small round holes (typically 1- to 2-inch diameter) are drilled into the exterior wall of the home between the studs (e.g., every 16 inches) (U.S. Department of Energy 2022). A hose is inserted into the wall, and insulation is then blown in. Both the small round holes and the hose are visible in Figure 1.

Exterior wall studs in residential construction commonly have three widths: 2x3, 2x4, and 2x6. The 2x3 and 2x4 studs are more common in mobile/manufactured homes and older residential construction, whereas 2x6 studs are more frequently used in newer residential construction (Krigger 2006) (Kochkin and Wiehagan 2014). Mobile/manufactured homes may use 2x2 studs, but these are typically only for interior walls (Dickerhoof 1977). This ResStock 2025 Release 1 assumes that R-13 insulation can fit within a 2x4 wall. In cases where higher levels of insulation are installed, OS-HPXML selects the appropriate wall construction set to ensure the desired R-value is achieved. This may include changing the stud cavity depth -for example, changing a wall from a 2x4 to a 2x6 to meet the requirement. More information on this topic can be found on the ResStock 'Wood Stud Depth and Wall R-Value Calculations' page . 2

When installing drill-and-fill insulation from the exterior of a building, the cladding material is an important consideration. Exterior wood or vinyl siding is sometimes partially removed to allow holes to be drilled in the exterior sheathing. Wood siding can also be drilled through and then patched. Some types of metal siding may be removable, while others may not be (Allwein and Biddle 2002). In homes with face brick (4-inch) as cladding over wood stud walls, drill-and-fill can be performed by drilling smaller holes through mortar joints. Other construction types, such as concrete masonry units (CMUs) or steel framing, are limited to insulation methods other than drill-and-fill for retrofits (U.S. Department of Energy 2019).

Other ways to insulate a wall include adding rigid insulation board to the exterior or interior, or removing interior finishes and adding batt insulation, among others (Baechler, et al. 2021) (Antonopoulos, et al. 2019). Attic, crawlspace, basement, or other building insulation techniques are not covered in this measure. Attic floor insulation for unfinished basements is addressed in a separate measure documentation. 3

There are safety concerns that must be addressed when performing drill-and-fill insulation. The structural integrity of the exterior wall and cladding is important, as is any existing water damage (Pfeiffer 2021). Asbestos is a health and safety concern for insulation installers and is more common in homes built between 1930 and 1970 (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency 2011). Mold or rotted wood may be found in stud cavities, which could also complicate retrofits (Brozyna and Badger 2013). This is not a complete list of safety concerns that should be addressed before upgrading a building's insulation.

## 1.2  Drill-and-Fill Considerations

The thermal resistance of drill-and-fill insulation is measured by its R-value, which represents the material 's ability to reduce heat flow (U.S. Department of Energy 2010). A higher R-value results in less heat transfer. As a retrofit, drill-and-fill wall insulation can achieve up to R-13 cavity insulation in 2x4 exterior stud cavities (3.5 inches at R-3.7 per inch), which is the maximum insulation level modeled in this measure (Holladay 2013).

Wall cavities that are insulated to levels less than R-13 (e.g., R-11, R-7) did not receive the drill-and-fill upgrade in this ResStock measure. An experienced drill-and-fill practitioner noted that installing additional drill-and-fill insulation into an already insulated wall could cause the insulation batt to bunch, create uneven insulation levels, and lead to other issues. This is supported by literature on retrofit projects that choose not to perform drill-and-fill insulation on walls with existing insulation below R-13 (Frontier Energy, Inc., Misti Bruceri &amp; Associates, LLC 2022) (Antonopoulos, et al. 2022). However, there is some evidence that drill-and-fill can be applied to walls with existing insulation, but this is not included in this measure (Holladay 2013).

Manufactured housing or mobile homes receive drill-and-fill insulation in this measure only if the walls are uninsulated and wood stud. In practice, this can be extremely difficult and requires caution (Carlson and Bemis 1979) (Judkoff, Hancock and Franconi 1990). As with all ResStock results, this upgrade measure represents theoretical technical potential, and dataset users should independently assess whether this measure is appropriate for their building segment of interest.

Studies often pair air sealing and drill-and-fill insulation and report combined energy savings. One study modeled energy savings from drill-and-fill insulation across different climate zones and found whole-home site energy savings ranging from 8.8% to 13.5% (Polly, et al. 2011). Across the literature, drill-and-fill insulation upgrades are frequently paired with air sealing measures (U.S. Department of Energy 2025) (Brozyna and Badger 2013) (Roberts and Stephenson 2012). More information on air sealing modeling can be found in the advanced air sealing documentation (Phoung, Lou and White 2025).

For modeling purposes, drill-and-fill insulation is assumed to be installed properly to a Grade 1 insulation level. According to the RESNET Grading system, Grade 1 assumes installation follows the manufacturer's instructions and fills the entire wall cavity ( Construction Instruction Experience Center n.d.). However, a field study found that only half of all homes had Grade 1 insulation (North American Insulation Manufacturers Association 2017). In practice, a Grade 1 assumption may be optimistic, and insulation inspection should be conducted carefully (North American Insulation Manufacturers Association 2017). This optimistic assumption about proper insulation installation is a limitation of the work, and may cause modeled results to be different than actual building retrofit results.

In summary, this upgrade measure assumes:

- Advanced air sealing is applied to dwelling units with ACH50 infiltration rates of 6 or greater. This applies to 92% of all dwelling units and heavily influences the results.
- Drill-and-fill insulation is applied to about 30% of dwelling units only
- Drill-and-fill insulation uses cellulose up to R-13 on exterior above-grade walls that separate conditioned from unconditioned spaces only
- Existing wall cavities with no insulation will receive the insulation, and walls with insulation less than R-13 will not
- Proper installation is assumed, similar to Grade 1 RESNET installation.

## 1.3  Advanced Air Sealing Summary

As previously mentioned, drill-and-fill wall insulation is often installed along with air sealing during retrofit projects. Therefore, this drill-and-fill insulation package is modeled with air sealing. Full documentation for the advanced air sealing component of this package is reported separately, but a summary is provided below (Phoung, Lou and White 2025).

Air sealing can take various forms, including weatherstripping, caulking, and spray foam. Two common ways to describe air leakage rates are air changes per hour at 50 pascals (ACH50) and cubic feet per minute at 50 pascals (CFM50). ACH50 is used to compare air leakage rates between dwelling units of different sizes, and this metric will be used in this package.

All dwelling units with an infiltration rate above 6 ACH50 will receive advanced air sealing and then have an infiltration rate of 5 ACH50. This level of advanced air sealing, 5 ACH50, should be considered very aggressive, similar to bringing all residential air leakage down to IECC 2021 new construction levels (3-5 ACH50 depending on climate). In reality, bringing infiltration rates down to this level may require additional enclosure upgrades, such as over cladding of walls, replacing windows, or other techniques that go beyond traditional air sealing practices. This applies to 92% of all dwelling units (Phoung, Lou and White 2025).

## 3 Modeling Approach

This section explains how advanced air sealing with drill-and-fill wall insulation is functionally modeled in ResStock using OpenStudio-HPXML.

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025 to assess whether the existing panel has enough capacity or breaker space to accommodate new loads from an energy retrofit. For details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, Fontanini and Parker, et al. 2025). This measure does not introduce new electrical loads or circuits, so it has no impact on the electrical panel. However, electrical panel metrics are included as a standard output for this and all other envelope measures. Any panel constraints appearing in the upgrade results reflect pre-existing conditions, as this measure does not worsen them.

## 3.1  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

The ResStock building stock energy model includes conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that represent the U.S. residential building stock (Reyna, Fontanini and Present, et al. 2024). These distributions are sampled to create a synthetic stock, with each sample an option for each characteristic. ResStock uses the OpenStudio-HPXML (OS-HPXML) workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models that can be simulated via OpenStudio ®  and EnergyPlus ®  using an HPXML (Home Performance eXtensible Markup Language) file. The state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository at the time the ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset was created is available on the NLR ResStock Resources page on GitHub. 5

The drill-and-fill wall insulation measure leverages the 'Insulation Wall' ResStock option that assigns wall assembly R-values and wall types in OS-HPXML. For example, climate zone 4A shows greater savings than 4B and 4C because the average baseline dwelling unit energy consumption in 4A is higher than in the other zones, as shown in Figure 10. This is because climate zone 4A is a more extreme climate than climate zones 4B and 4C.

Climate zone 7B shows a larger site energy percentage savings than climate zones 7AK and 8AK because climate zone 7B 's results are driven by natural gas heating savings, which are on average more than three times as large as climate zones 7AK and 8AK savings. In addition, climate zone 7AK shows heat pump fan and cooling electricity increasing after the upgrade. These end use increases further add to the difference in total site energy savings experienced between climate zones 7AK and 7B. The difference among the climate zones savings are also influenced by the electricity consumption from the heat pump heating backup. Climate zone 7B shows a 38% savings on average for this end use, while climate zone 7AK only shows a 23% site energy savings, and this end use is not applicable for climate zone 8AK. This is another reason for the large difference in site energy savings between the three climate zones.
