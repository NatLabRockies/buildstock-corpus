## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit end-use load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover most high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model represents the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson, et al. 2022).

This document focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: Residential Two-Stage Geothermal Heat Pump (GHP) (4.0 Coefficient of Performance [COP], 20.5 Energy Efficiency Ratio [EER]) With Light Envelope Improvements. This measure combines a two-stage GHP with light air sealing, attic floor insulation, and duct sealing and insulation as a single package. Because this package is a combination of two other measures, this document focuses on the results associated with this combination of technologies, while individual measure documents for the two-stage GHP and light envelope improvements package provide methodology and results for these measures.

This upgrade package demonstrates 35% modeled total site energy savings (4.12 quadrillion Btu [quads]) for the U.S. residential building stock. These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 80% stock natural gas heating savings (3.14 quads)
- 42% stock cooling savings (0.55 quads)
- 70% stock fuel oil heating savings (0.40 quads)
- 80% stock propane heating savings (0.35 quads)
- 34% stock electricity heating increase (0.29 quads).

## 1  Technology Summary

This measure combines a GHP measure and an envelope measure package into a single upgrade measure package. The first measure is a water-to-air GHP with a two-stage compressor rated at a COP of 4.0 and an EER of 20.5. More details on GHPs and how they are modeled in ResStock 2025 Release 1, including the details of modeling the ground heat exchanger, are included in the single-stage GHP measure document (Maguire, Fontanini, et al. 2026). The details of how two-stage GHPs with this rated efficiency are modeled are included in the two-stage GHP measure document (Fontanini, et al. 2026). The details of the light envelope improvement measure package are included in the light air sealing, attic insulation, and duct sealing improvement measure document (Brossman and White 2026).

## 2  Modeling Approach

## 2.1  Applicability of Two-Stage GHP Measure

Using the same applicability criteria as the single-stage GHP and the stand-alone two-stage compressor, the water-to-air vertical ground heat exchanger GHP upgrade measure applies to all dwellings with:

- Non-shared (in-unit) HVAC and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane
- Shared (across multiple units) HVAC and a building height of &lt;8 stories and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane
- Dwelling units with ducts
- Dwelling units with soil thermal conductivity greater than 0.8 Btu/hr-ft-°F.

With these applicability constraints, the measure upgrade applies to roughly 71% of the existing building stock. 6.4% of the residential units that receive the GHP upgrade lack existing space cooling systems in the baseline and therefore receive a central cooling system via the GHP.

From the previous work on this GHP model, applicability of this measure upgrade is based on ResStock housing characteristics. These characteristics determine the available options and the options' details (Reyna, et al. 2025) (Fontanini, et al. 2026):

- 'HVAC Heating Efficiency' is used to apply each respective heat pump option.
- 'HVAC Has Ducts' describes the presence of ducts in the dwelling.
- 'Heating Type' describes the baseline heating fuel of the dwelling.
- 'HVAC Shared Efficiencies' describes the shared HVAC system of the dwelling, if applicable.
- 'Geometry Building Type Height' describes the height and building type of the dwelling.

More information on the ResStock housing characteristics can be found in the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al. 2025).

## 2.2  Applicability of Light Envelope Improvements Package

While envelope improvements are included for any home receiving a dual-stage GHP, the baseline conditions must warrant an improvement for each component of the upgrade to apply.

For the light air sealing upgrades to apply, the infiltration rate of the baseline home must be at least 10 air changes per hour at 50 pascals (ACH50). The light air sealing upgrade is then applied to reach a 25% infiltration reduction. More details on air sealing applicability can be found in the light air sealing measure document (Brossman, White and Phoung 2026).

For duct sealing and insulation, the upgrade case installs R-6 duct insulation if existing ducts have an insulation level of R-4 or worse. Ducts are sealed to 5% leakage if there is more than 6% leakage in the baseline. These improvements can be installed independently of each other, or both may apply if both the leakage and insulation criteria are met. More details on duct sealing and insulation applicability can be found in the duct improvement measure document (Lou, Liu and Parker 2026).

For attic insulation, this measure upgrades attic floor insulation in unfinished attics where the existing insulation is more than a difference of R-10 below the maximum level recommended by the IECC for each unit's climate zone. The post-retrofit R-value is set to reflect the maximum value recommended by code. For example, while the IECC recommends an R-value range of R-49 to R-60 for climate zone 2, dwelling units in this zone are upgraded to R-60 to represent the upper bound of achievable performance. This approach ensures that the modeled retrofit captures both the energy savings potential of code-compliant upgrades and the levels typically achieved in field implementations. More details on attic insulation applicability can be found in the attic insulation measure document (Phoung, Lou and White 2026).

The ResStock 2025 Release 1 includes an additional, similar upgrade package to this document's upgrade package. The only difference in this upgrade package is that this document's air sealing is less intense. This document has a 25% infiltration reduction across homes with a baseline infiltration level of 10 ACH50 or more, while the similar upgrade package has prescriptive ACH50 values based on climate zone. For more information see the more advanced air sealing documentation (Phuong, Lou and White 2026) and the two-stage GHP with advanced envelope improvements (Maguire, Fontanini, et al. 2026).

## 2.3  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

The ResStock building stock energy model contains conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that are representative of the U.S. residential building stock (Reyna, et al. 2025). These are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each sample has an assigned option for each characteristic for each dwelling unit to be simulated. ResStock utilizes the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio ®  and EnergyPlus ®  through an HPXML file. For reference, the state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository when the ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset was created can be found here. Assumptions about the GHP upgrades that are not explicitly defined in ResStock are contained in the BuildResidentialHPXML measure.

For information on how OpenStudio-HPXML handles controls in ResStock Dataset 2025 Release 1 see here, and for how it handles HVAC distribution see here. Additional details on applying the two-stage GHP measure are included in the relevant section of that measure document.

## 2.4  Two-Stage Water-to-Air Vertical Ground Heat Exchanger GHP Upgrade Details

This section about the GHP is covered in the two-stage GHP document, and is restated here (Fontanini, et al. 2026).

Details specific to the two-stage GHP that may interact with the light envelope improvements are included below. In many cases, the approach used here is the same as when two-stage GHP equipment is installed on its own, but relevant portions of that approach are reiterated here. Since envelope improvements can change the loads and size of equipment installed, this document focuses on the approach to sizing equipment.

## 2.4.1  GHP Sizing Methodology

The two-stage GHP uses the same sizing methodology as the single-stage GHP (Maguire, Fontanini, et al. 2026). The design heating and cooling loads are calculated in accordance with Air Conditioning Contractors of America (ACCA) Manuals J (ACCA 2016), and the equipment size is selected based on ACCA Manual S (ACCA 2014) using a routine included in OpenStudio-HPXML. Note that because the GHP sizing routine includes accounting of the peak heating and cooling building loads, equipment sizes are decreased when envelope improvements are included. This means that for the combined package the equipment capacity, ground heat exchanger size, and associated cost for installing a GHP may be reduced compared to just installing a two-stage GHP.

## Why sizing around envelope improvements matters:

When the building envelope is improved (through measures such as added insulation, air sealing, or high-performance windows) the peak heating and cooling loads are reduced. This directly impacts the sizing calculation for the GHP, allowing for smaller-capacity equipment to meet the same comfort requirements and reducing the capital costs for the heat pump. Smaller systems also require less ground heat exchanger length, which further lowers installation costs and can improve system efficiency. By addressing the envelope before or during GHP sizing, the project avoids oversizing, reduces upfront and operating costs, and maximizes the long-term performance of the system.

## 2.4.2 Ground Heat Exchanger

The two-stage GHP uses the same type of ground heat exchanger as the stand-alone two-stage GHP measure. Both use thermally enhanced grout and pipe materials to improve heat transfer, and the loop is installed in a vertical, rectangular configuration.

## Why the ground heat exchanger matters when adding envelope improvements:

The ground heat exchanger is one of the largest cost components of a GHP system because drilling and installing the vertical loops is labor- and equipment-intensive. When the building envelope is improved, the heating and cooling loads are reduced, which means the GHP can be smaller. A smaller GHP requires less heat exchange with the ground, allowing for shorter ground heat exchangers. This reduction in drilling length can significantly cut installation costs and make the overall system more affordable. Better insulation and air sealing can not only shrink the equipment size, but also shrink the number of vertical feet of drilling.

## 2.5  Attic Insulation, Light Air Sealing, and Duct Sealing

Each individual component of this measure is documented separately (Phoung, Lou and White 2026) (Brossman, White and Phoung 2026) (Lou, Liu and Parker 2026) and documented as a package (Brossman and White 2026).

The rest of this section is restated here from previous work done on the GHP and envelope measure (Maguire, Fontanini, et al. 2026) (Brossman and White, ResStock Measure Documentation: Light Air Sealing, Attic Floor Insulation, and Duct Sealing and Insulation

2026). The goal of this package is to capture efficiency measures that can be applied to existing buildings. This is similar to what might be done as part of weatherization upgrades to a home, where the easiest and most beneficial improvements are made.

This upgrade package integrates three coordinated energy efficiency measures: light air sealing, attic floor insulation, and duct sealing and insulation, to address sources of energy loss in a home. Air sealing targets gaps, cracks, and penetrations in the building envelope to reduce uncontrolled air leakage, improve comfort, and lessen the workload on heating and cooling systems. Attic floor insulation slows heat transfer between living areas and unconditioned attic spaces, keeping heat inside in winter and out in summer. Duct sealing and insulation prevents conditioned air from escaping into unconditioned spaces through leaky ducts, improving HVAC efficiency, maintaining more even indoor temperatures, and protecting indoor air quality. While each measure provides benefits individually, their combined effect is greater, making them most effective when implemented as a package.

The recommended implementation sequence is based on building science principles. First, light air sealing is applied to achieve a 25% infiltration reduction, followed by duct sealing and insulating to reduce leakage to a maximum of 5% and achieve R-8 duct insulation, and finally upgrading attic floor insulation. This order ensures air leaks are addressed before insulation is installed, prevents ducts from being buried before sealing, and optimizes the performance of both insulation and the HVAC system. By following this sequence, field staff can more easily identify and seal problem areas and avoid rework.

Together, these measures improve comfort, lower energy bills, and enhance indoor air quality throughout the year. Air sealing stabilizes indoor temperatures and minimizes drafts, duct sealing reduces waste and protects against contaminants entering the system, and attic insulation locks in the conditioned air for longer periods. When applied together, they create a tighter, better-insulated, and more efficient home, with benefits that are greater and more durable than any single measure alone.

## 3.4  Electric Panel Impact

This section details the impact the two-stage GHP with light envelope improvements will have on electrical panels. The modeling assumptions and details about electric panels in ResStock can be found in the single-stage GHP documentation (Maguire, Fontanini, et al. 2026).

First, Figure 8 and Figure 9 show information about the ResStock 2025 Release 1 baseline housing stock electric panel distribution. Figure 8 shows in the first column (labeled 'All') that about 35% of all dwelling units have one or fewer breaker spaces available. Figure 9 shows in the first column that 97.7% of electric panels have no existing capacity constraint for the baseline housing stock.

Capacity constraints in the existing baseline stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. Hence, the capacity-constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on.

Before the two-stage GHP with light envelope improvements package is applied, the panels experiencing a capacity constraint would likely need to address this issue first. To see results of non-affected households, users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose pre-existing capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.
