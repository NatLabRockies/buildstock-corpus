## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover most high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to energy efficiency retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. This results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model is intended to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in (Reyna, et al., 2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (NREL, 2025).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape package-Advanced Air Sealing, Attic Floor Insulation, Duct Sealing, and Drill-and-Fill Wall Insulation Upgrade.

The Advanced Air Sealing, Attic Floor Insulation, Duct Sealing, and Drill-and-Fill Wall Insulation Upgrade achieved 27.2% total site energy savings (938 TWh) for the U.S. residential building stock (Figure 5). These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 48% stock natural gas heating savings (556 TWh)
- 43% stock electricity heating savings (110 TWh)
- 24% stock electricity cooling savings (91 TWh).

## 1  Technology Summary

This upgrade package which includes advanced air sealing, attic floor insulation, duct sealing, and drill-and-fill wall insulation, combines four key areas of a retrofit measures. The goal of these upgrades is to reduce heat loss in winter and retain cool air longer in summer. Air sealing alone does not eliminate the need for insulation (DOE, 2025). Insulation and air sealing work best together to improve comfort and energy efficiency. Therefore, they are often completed as a package to address complementary aspects of a home's energy efficiency, and their combined effect is much greater than any single measure alone. Together, these upgrades help to lower energy bills, improve indoor comfort, and enhance indoor air quality year-round.

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

## Air Sealing

Air sealing involves identifying and closing large and small gaps, cracks, and penetrations in the home's primary or secondary pressure boundaries such as around windows, doors, attic hatches, and plumbing or wiring holes (DOE, 2025). These leaks, whether big or small, accumulate and can allow a substantial amount of heated or cooled air escape. By reducing air leaks, air sealing improves indoor comfort, maintains more stable temperatures, and reduces the work load on the heating and cooling. Air sealing is especially important in older homes, which typically have more leaks (Alliance, 2025). Refer to the air sealing documentation for more information on common leakage points, as well as best practices for sealing and prescriptive air leakage rate based on climate zones (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a).

## Attic Floor Insulation

Insulating the attic floor reduces heat transfer between the living space and the unconditioned attic. In colder climates, this keeps heat inside during winter. In warmer climates, it helps keep the living space cooler. The effectiveness of insulation is measured by its R-value; a higher Rvalue indicates better resistance to heat flow. Installing insulation in existing homes typically involves blowing loose-fill insulation over the attic floor, especially in unfinished attics. The optimal insulation level depends on the climate zone and the home's existing insulation. Refer to the attic insulation documentation for guidance on recommended R-values and installation approaches (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025b).

## Duct Sealing and Insulation

Ducts distribute heated or cooled air throughout the house. In many homes, especially older ones, these ducts have leaks that allow conditioned air escape into unconditioned or semiconditioned spaces like attics or crawlspaces. As a result, the system must work harder and uses more energy to maintain the desired temperature. Duct sealing addresses these leaks using mastic, metal tape, or an aerosol-based sealant, depending on the situation. Sealing ducts improves system efficiency, maintains more consistent room temperatures, and enhances indoor air quality by preventing dirty attic or crawlspace air from entering the ducts. Duct sealing can also reduce unintentional pressure imbalances, which may cause indoor air quality or combustion safety concerns. See the duct sealing documentation for more information on methods and materials (Lou &amp; Liu, 2025).

## Drill-and-Fill Wall Insulation

Drill-and-fill insulation is one way to insulate an existing uninsulated wall cavity. It involves drilling small holes into the exterior sheeting or interior wall covering and blowing loose-fill insulation material (such as cellulose or fiberglass) into the cavities between the wall studs (EERE, 2025). This measure upgrade models cellulose as the drill-and-fill insulation material. Drill-and-fill insulation can be completed from the inside or outside of the building. See the drilland-fill documentation for more information on the installation technology and applicable situations (Brossman &amp; Lou, 2025).

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

To maximize energy efficiency and based on building science principles, the correct order for implementing this upgrade package is: (1) air sealing, (2) duct sealing, (3) attic floor insulation, and (4) drill-and-fill wall insulation. Reducing air leakage is the foundation of energy efficiency. It helps control indoor temperatures and moisture, and improves the effectiveness of insulation and HVAC systems. Sealing leaks before adding insulation ensures the insulation performs as intended. It also makes leaks easier to identify and reduces the burden on field staff, since they are not working around additional insulation. Once air sealing targets are met, it is essential to address the HVAC system by sealing any leaky ducts to prevent the loss of conditioned air, particularly in unconditioned spaces like attics or crawlspaces. With both air leaks and ducts sealed, attic floor insulation should be installed. Finally, insulation can be added to uninsulated wall cavities to reduce conductive heat loss.

Therefore, we assume that upgrades will be implemented in the following sequence: Advanced air sealing is applied first to applicable dwelling units, with the goal of achieving 5 ACH50. While some regions require tighter envelopes for new constructions, 5 ACH50 reflects the average infiltration rate achieved in empirical studies across representative climate zones and ensures that selected homes do not already comply with 2024 IECC requirements (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a).

After air sealing, HVAC ducts in applicable homes are sealed and insulated to achieve:

- A maximum of 5% duct air leakage to the outside
- Duct insulation level of R-8.

Attic floor insulation is then upgraded in applicable dwelling units to the following levels:

- R-49 in climate zone 1
- R-60 in climate zones 2 through 8.

Finally, uninsulated wood stud walls are upgraded with cellulose drill-and-fill insulation to reach an R-13 level.

More details about the modeling assumption for these three measures can be found in the accompanying documentation (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a) (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025b) (Lou &amp; Liu, 2025) (Brossman &amp; Lou, 2025).

## 2  Applicability

Advanced air sealing upgrades are applied to dwelling units with infiltration rates greater than or equal 6 ACH50.

Attic floor insulation upgrades are applied only to dwelling units with unfinished attics (vented and unvented attic type in ResStock). Specifically, upgrades are triggered when attic floor insulation is below the following levels: less than R-49 in climate zone 1 and less than R-60 in climate zones 2 through 8.

Duct sealing applies to dwelling units with duct leakage to the outside greater than 6% or duct insulation rated below R-6. Dwelling units with 0% leakage to the and uninsulated ducts are not applicable, as these configurations typically indicate that the ducts are located entirely within conditioned spaces.

Drill-and-fill wall insulation is applicable to dwelling units with uninsulated wood stud walls.

Further details on the applicability criteria and stock saturation for each of these four upgrades can be found in the accompanying documentation (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a) (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025b) (Lou &amp; Liu, 2025) (Brossman &amp; Lou, 2025). ## 3  Modeling Approach

The modeling approach of the four individual measures can be found in the accompanying documentation (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a) (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025b) (Lou &amp; Liu, 2025) (Brossman &amp; Lou, 2025).

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025 to assess whether the existing panel has enough capacity or breaker space to accommodate new loads from an energy retrofit. For details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al., 2025). This measure does not introduce new electrical loads or circuits, so it has no impact on the electrical panel. However, electrical panel metrics are included as a standard output for this and all other envelope measures. Any panel constraints appearing in the upgrade results reflect pre-existing conditions, as this measure does not worsen them.

## 4.1.1 Individual Building Annual

Figure 1 shows the annual energy consumption of the example dwelling unit under the full envelope package upgrade and under each of the four individual measures applied separately. Among these five scenarios, heating energy use shows the largest reduction, dropping from 105.5 MBtu in the baseline to 39.9 MBtu with the full upgrade, a 62% decrease. Cooling energy use also decreases slightly, while hot water energy use remains nearly unchanged. All other end uses are unaffected.

The envelope upgrade reduced heat loss from the dwelling, which led to a slight increase in hot water energy consumption. In this example, the water heater is in an unheated basement. By preventing heat loss during winter and retaining cool air during summer, the upgrade improves overall thermal efficiency. However, because the unit is heating-dominated, the reduction in heat loss during winter is more significant than the benefit of retaining cool air in summer. As a result, less ambient heat is released into the unheated basement, causing the water heater to work harder and use slightly more energy to maintain the desired water temperature.

The comprehensive envelope upgrade package reduces the dwelling unit's annual total energy use from 165 MBtu to 95 MBtu, a 42% reduction. In contrast, air sealing, drill-and-fill wall insulation, duct sealing and insulation, and attic floor insulation individually reduced energy consumption by 18%, 18%, 8%, and 1%, respectively. This demonstrates that the full envelope package maximizes energy efficiency, highlighting the synergistic benefits of combining all four measures.

When the surrounding air is cooler, a refrigerator operates more efficiently and uses less energy. The average indoor temperature over the year increased slightly from 71.28°F to 71.68°F.
