## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson et al. 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: Residential Single-Stage Geothermal Heat Pump (GHP). Single-stage GHPs reduce stock-level site energy consumption by 24% (Figure 18). Additional results provided below detail how savings changes for sections of the housing stock with different base heating fuel and in different climate zones, as well as the savings potential by state for both heating and cooling. Utility bills and electric panel impacts are also shown and discussed.

## ResStock 2025 Release 1 Measure Documentation: Residential Single-Stage Geothermal Heat Pump

## Accessing Results

This documentation covers the 'Residential Single-Stage Geothermal Heat Pump (3.8 Coefficient of Performance [COP], 18.6 Energy Efficiency Ratio [EER])' measure upgrade methodology and briefly discusses key results. All results can be accessed on the ResStock™ Open Energy Data Initiative 'End-Use Load Profiles for the U.S. Building Stock' data lake and on the Data Viewer at resstock.nlr.gov.

## Measure Summary

Table 1. Upgrade Measure Overview

| Measure Title                 | Residential Geothermal Heat Pump (3.8 COP, 18.6 EER)-Single-Stage Compressor, Water-to-Air Geothermal Heat Pump with Vertical Ground Heat Exchanger                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
|-------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Measure Definition            | This upgrade measure replaces the HVAC system in any existing dwelling unit in a building less than 8 stories with either ducted heating or cooling with a single- stage compressor, variable-speed fan, water-to-air geothermal heat pump (GHP) with vertical ground heat exchanger.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| Applicability                 | This measure applies to all dwellings with: • Non-shared (in-unit) HVAC and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane • Shared (across multiple units) HVAC and a building height of <8 stories and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane • Dwelling units with air ducts • Dwelling units with soil thermal conductivity greater than 0.8 Btu/hr-ft-°F. With these applicability constraints, the measure upgrade applies to 71% of the existing residential building stock. 6.4% of the residential units that receive the GHP upgrade lack existing space cooling systems in the baseline. ## 1  Technology Summary

## 1.1 Introduction to Geothermal Heat Pumps

Geothermal heat pumps (GHPs), 1 also known as ground-source heat pumps, provide space heating and cooling in buildings. What differentiates a GHP from other heat pump technologies is that GHPs use the naturally stable ground temperature and ability of the subsurface to store thermal energy to heat and cool a building. There are two main parts of a GHP: (1) the ground heat exchanger loop, sometimes referred to as the ground loop, which consists of a set of fluidfilled pipes buried underground that transfers heat between the ground and the heat pump, and (2) the heat pump system that distributes conditioned air (or water) to the building. During the winter, GHPs extract heat from the ground by circulating a working fluid in the ground heat exchanger. The thermal energy absorbed by the fluid is then used by the heat pump to transfer the absorbed heat to the building. During the summer, the system is reversed, using the ground as a heat sink to provide cooling to the building.

GHPs are classified by the configurations of their ground heat exchanger and heat pump. The heat pump can either be a water-to-air or a water-to-water (also called 'hydronic') system. There are four basic ground heat exchanger types: closed-loop horizontal, closed-loop vertical, closedloop surface water systems, and open-loop systems. 2 Closed-loop horizontal systems run the ground heat exchanger close to the surface in shallow trenches, which requires sufficient land available for horizontal loop installation. Closed-loop vertical systems run the ground heat exchanger in vertical boreholes and require less land area than horizontal systems. Closed-loop surface water systems use a nearby body of water as the heat exchanger in the ground heat exchanger. Open-loop systems allow the fluid in the ground heat exchanger to be exchanged with a well or body of water. In some instances, these open-loop systems require clean water and must meet local groundwater regulations. Another GHP configuration is a direct geo-exchange system, which uses the refrigerant lines of the heat pump as the working fluid in the ground heat exchanger rather than a water and antifreeze (usually propylene glycol) mixture.

By rejecting or absorbing thermal energy from the ground, GHPs have a relatively stable cooling and heating performance, as the ground temperature remains relatively constant throughout the year. In contrast, air-source heat pumps (ASHPs) draw thermal energy from the outdoor air. As the outdoor air temperature decreases, the ASHP has less ability to transfer heat from the air to the building. For ASHPs, this results in less efficient performance at extreme cold temperatures. To meet the heating demand when outdoor air temperatures are very low (Sergici, et al. 2023), less efficient backup electric resistance heat is generally used for ASHPs. In a situation with a large deployment of ASHPs in cold climates, the coldest hours of the year could result in large winter peak demand that utilities would have to manage (Murphy, Hagerty and Weiss 2020, Sergici, et al. 2023). GHPs could help reduce winter peak demand, as their performance is less affected by cold outdoor temperatures because they rely on stable subsurface ground temperatures. However, capital costs for GHPs are generally higher than ASHPs, in large part due to the cost associated with installing the ground heat exchanger. There are exceptions; for multifamily buildings the ground heat exchanger could be installed vertically under an existing parking lot (Lienau, Boyd and Rogers 1995). For new construction, the ground heat exchanger could be installed in the lawn or under the driveway.

2 Visual representations and discussion of these types can be found at https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/geothermal-heat-pumps

There are several GHP performance levels, including single-stage, two-stage, and variable-speed systems.

- Single-stage GHPs operate at full capacity whenever they run, which can lead to energy inefficiencies under part-load conditions (when the system needs less than its maximum output).
- Two-stage GHPs improve upon this design by adding a second, lower-capacity stage. This allows the system to operate more efficiently during mild conditions while still having the capability to switch to full output when demand peaks.
- Variable-speed GHPs represent the most advanced option, using inverter-driven compressors and fans to continuously adjust their output in response to real-time heating and cooling needs. Unlike single-stage or two-stage systems that operate in discrete steps, variable-speed systems can modulate across a wide range of capacities. This precise control enhances energy efficiency, provides more stable indoor temperatures, reduces humidity swings, and minimizes noise from cycling.

## 1.2 Residential GHP Technology Review Summary

A review of GHPs in the U.S. building stock was performed as part of this work. A detailed discussion can be found in Appendix A. The review includes: (1) the number of GHP installations, where they were located, and the building types and when the homes were built; (2) the most common mechanical systems used in residential GHP installations; (3) the GHPs' heating and cooling performance; and (4) the common ground heat exchanger configurations and the frequency of installation. Key takeaways from the review are as follows:

- There are approximately 1.28 -2 million GHPs in the U.S. residential building stock (Gertler, Steeves and Wang 2025)
- Tennessee, North Carolina, Florida, Texas, and Alabama make up almost 40% of residential GHP installations. Almost 85% of GHPs are in the IECC 2012 (Burleyson, 2012) moist climate zones, 2A -5A. Climate zone 4A has the most GHP installations, with about 30% of total installations (U.S. EIA 2023).
- About 70% of GHP installations are in homes that were built since the 1980s and primarily are in single-family housing (U.S. EIA 2023).
- Approximately 75% of GHP installations are in new construction and 25% of GHP installations are in retrofit systems (U.S. EIA 2023).
- The most common ground heat exchanger configurations are vertical and closed-loop systems (Battocletti and Glassley 2013).
- Water-to-air heat pumps are the most common type of geothermal mechanical equipment used to deliver heating and cooling to residential buildings (Battocletti and Glassley 2013).

## 2  Modeling Approach

## 2.1 Applicability

The single-stage compressor, water-to-air vertical ground heat exchanger GHP upgrade measure applies to all dwellings with:

- Non-shared (in-unit) HVAC and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane
- Shared (across multiple units) HVAC and a building height of &lt;8 stories and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane
- Dwelling units with ducts
- Dwelling units with soil thermal conductivity greater than 0.8 Btu/hr-ft-°F.

With these applicability constraints, the measure upgrade applies to 71% of the existing residential building stock. Units that receive the GHP upgrade that do not have existing cooling systems make up 6.4%. These applicability rules are further described below.

Applicability of this measure upgrade is based on ResStock housing characteristics that determine the available options and the options' details (Reyna, et al. 2025). Specifically, the 'HVAC Heating Efficiency' characteristic in ResStock is used to apply each respective heat pump option. 'HVAC Has Ducts' describes the presence of ducts in the dwelling. 'Heating Type' describes the baseline heating fuel of the dwelling. 'HVAC Shared Efficiencies' describes the shared HVAC system of the dwelling, if applicable. 'Geometry Building Type Height' describes the height and building type of the dwelling. More information on the ResStock housing characteristics can be found in the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna et al. 2025).

## 2.1.1 Exclusion of Dwellings Without Air Ducts

Dwellings without ducts are excluded from this upgrade measure because they would need to install a new ductwork system, which would increase the cost of the upgrade measure. The current modeling approach also does not have an option for a GHP hydronic (water-to-water) system (i.e., that would not require installation of a new duct system), which is roughly 22% of the stock.

## 2.1.2  Exclusion of High-Rise Shared HVAC Dwellings

High-rise (8+ stories) dwellings with shared HVAC are excluded from this upgrade measure because the outdoor units would have to be mounted directly outside the unit in the high-rise building, creating a uniquely complicated maintenance environment. 3 Due to the current unitbased multifamilty modeling approach in ResStock, these buildings are also complicated to accurately model the application of a GHP upgrade. Because these dwellings only comprise 1% of the total building stock, this housing segment was not upgraded.

3 Multifamily units without shared HVAC systems are included in the upgrade regardless of the number of stories in the building.

## 2.1.3 Cooling Level Increase for All Applicable Dwellings

Dwellings that are not 100% cooled by their existing AC system (e.g., room AC providing cooling for just one room of a home) will be 100% cooled by this measure upgrade. This will increase cooling energy consumption of those dwellings that were not already 100% cooled. The cooling level increase for all applicable dwellings is implemented because heat pumps in this measure upgrade will be sized to cool the entire dwelling.

Units that receive the GHP upgrade that do not have an existing cooling system make up 6.4% of the residential stock. Units with less than 100% (anywhere from 80% to 0%) of the floor area cooled make up about 33% of the stock. These units will see an increase in energy consumption because of the added service.

## 2.1.4 Exclusion of 'Other Fuel' and 'None' Heating Fuels

Dwellings with 'Other Fuel' or 'None' heating fuels do not receive this measure upgrade because retrofits to these dwellings can lead to confusing results (e.g., false negative savings compared to baseline or installing heating in regions that do not need it) and the number of dwellings this affects is relatively small. 'Other Fuel' includes fuels like wood and coal, which are not currently represented in ResStock, so there is not an accurate baseline heating energy consumption to compare to. Additionally, there is not a large impact at scale since dwellings with 'Other Fuel' or 'None' heating fuels comprise approximately 2.9% of the total building stock. The 'Other Fuel' in ResStock is largely composed of solar heating systems. ResStock does not model solar heating systems.

## 2.1.5 Exclusion of Low Thermal Conductivity Soils

We have chosen not to include low thermal conductivity soils in our GHP upgrade logic. In real systems, these soil conditions can significantly reduce the performance of ground heat exchangers and may require larger, more expensive loop fields to maintain system efficiency.

When we try to model these scenarios, we often run into simulation failures or unstable results. These issues make it difficult to trust the upgrade impacts, especially when scaling across a large number of homes. Additionally, soil thermal properties can vary a lot and may not be well represented in the data we have, which adds more uncertainty.

The result is that we are not applying these GHPs to units with a ground thermal conductivity of 0.5 Btu/hr-ft-F to 0.8 Btu/hr-ft-F which make up 7.6% of the total building stock based on (X. W. Liu 2016). This helps ensure the model results are reliable and representative of scenarios where GHPs are more likely to perform well in practice.

## 2.2 OpenStudio-HPXML Single-Stage GHP Model

## 2.2.1  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

OpenStudio-HPXML is a workflow, developed by the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR), used for modeling residential buildings. This workflow uses the HPXML (Home Performance eXtensible Markup Language) standard for defining all the properties of a building that affect energy consumption, such as square footage, the type and efficiency of heating and cooling equipment, setpoints for heating and cooling, etc. NLR leads the development of this standard alongside a working group including a variety of industry stakeholders for determining what features are included in the standard, including updates to cover new technologies and additional inputs as appropriate. The OpenStudio-HPXML workflow uses a series of OpenStudio measures to translate an HPXML file into an EnergyPlus ®  input file to simulate the energy consumption of the building with the given inputs in a standard, repeatable way. The model outputs are time-series and annual energy consumption by end use and may also include outputs derived from energy consumption, such as utility bills. More information about the OpenStudioHPXML workflow can be found at the OpenStudio-HPXML documentation (NREL 2023).

OpenStudio-HPXML has had the ability to model GHPs for over 10 years, but the methods and assumptions it used have not been documented. More details of the approach that were maintained from the initial implementation through the current approach are contained in Appendix B.

The GHP model implemented in OpenStudio-HPXML adds a GHP, the ground heat exchanger loop, and any associated distribution equipment (pumps and fans). Fixed sizes of equipment may be entered, but the most common use case is to 'autosize' both the ground heat exchanger and the heat pump. When autosizing, the design heating and cooling loads are calculated in accordance with Air Conditioning Contractors of America (ACCA) Manual J (ACCA 2016) and ACCA Manual S (ACCA 2014) using a routine included in OpenStudio-HPXML. ACCA Manual J is a design-day method for calculating residential building loads. The outdoor air temperature for heating and cooling is picked as the '99%' temperature, meaning only 1% of hours of the year are colder or hotter, respectively, than the design temperature. ACCA Manual S sizes the equipment based on the calculations in ACCA Manual J. Equipment that meets both heating and cooling loads, such as heat pumps, is sized based on the cooling load unless a system is installed for heating only (which is possible in the workflow but uncommon in practice). However, the ground heat exchanger is always sized based on the larger of the heating and cooling loads to ensure that sufficient length is installed to prevent substantial unmet loads in cooling-dominated climates. A high-level overview of the workflow, including the ground heat exchanger sizing routine, is shown in Figure 1. The ground heat exchanger length is calculated following the equipment sizing routines. The length of the heat exchanger informs the number of boreholes, the depth of the boreholes, and the ground heat exchanger configuration. Finally, the g-functions are selected using a lookup table. G-functions are nondimensionalized ground temperature response functions that can be precalculated for specific ground heat exchanger geometries, and including a precalculated library within the workflow greatly reduces the simulation time required. Details of each step are outlined in the subsequent sections.

The ResStock building stock energy model contains conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that are representative of the contiguous U.S. residential building stock. These are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each sample has an assigned option for each characteristic for each dwelling unit to be simulated. ResStock uses the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio and EnergyPlus through the use of an HPXML file.

ResStock uses the single-unit workflow of OpenStudio-HPXML, which is designed to simulate individual residential dwelling units rather than entire buildings. In the case of multifamily or single-family attached units, this means that only a single representative unit is modeled, not the full structure or all attached units. This approach enables scalable and efficient modeling across a large housing stock, but it also affects system-level design outcomes, particularly for shared HVAC systems.

Because each unit is treated independently in the simulation, the ground heat exchangers are sized to meet the thermal loads of that individual unit alone. This can result in smaller loop field requirements per simulation than would be observed in practice, where multiple units often share a common loop field in multifamily systems. Additionally, this simplification does not account for thermal interactions between units, shared infrastructure efficiencies, or diversity in load profiles that could impact system sizing and performance in a multi-unit building. As a result, while the ResStock framework provides valuable insights into unit-level energy use and retrofit impacts, care must be taken when interpreting or extending its GHP results to real-world multiunit or centralized system designs.

## 2.2.2  Fan and Pump Assumptions

Assumptions made about the fan and pump size have an impact on the nominal efficiency of the GHP, which in turn impacts the overall energy consumption results. The EnergyPlus models require a gross COP, without any impact from the fan and pump, as an input because the fan and pump are modeled separately. Specification sheets for GHPs list the performance across a wide range of conditions, but these represent the net performance (including the impact of the heat from the fan and pump), not the gross (without the fan and pump). More information on converting net to gross can be found in (Cutler 2013). Since we are calculating the size of the GHP as part of the HVAC equipment sizing and explicitly modeling the fan and pump, we need to be able to go from the net to the gross capacity so that we do not include the impact of heat gains from these devices twice. These net-to-gross calculations need to be updated to correctly reflect the impact of both the fan heat and the pump. EnergyPlus models the fan and pump separately, so the proper model input for the coil object is the gross COP without any impact from the fan and pump.

## 2.2.4   Representing Cycling Within a Simulation Timestep

EnergyPlus allows users to enter a curve that impacts the efficiency of equipment at part load. This curve modifies the efficiency of the unit during times when the equipment only needs to run for a portion of the timestep and generally accounts for any losses that may occur due to cycling of equipment. 17 EnergyPlus uses the part load factor (which is related to the part load ratio by a user defined curve, with the specific curve used for GHPs shown in Figure 7) to determine the impact of cycling on HVAC equipment, with the efficiency directly proportional to the part load factor (which ranges from 0 -1).

A new approach to modeling cycling losses is being applied to GHPs in ResStock that differs significantly from the approach used for other residential HVAC systems such as air conditioners and ASHPs. Traditional air-to-air systems in EnergyPlus use a simplified method based on the coefficient of degradation (cd), typically derived from laboratory testing. A cd value of 0 indicates no degradation in performance due to cycling, and if the value is not known, EnergyPlus assumes a conservative default of 0.25, representing relatively high losses during part-load operation.

For GHPs, curves that relate the part load factor and part load ratio can therefore be used to have EnergyPlus account for any cycling losses that might occur for this equipment. For air-to-air equipment, a test to determine the coefficient of degradation (cd) is generally performed and reported. A cd value of 0 would represent no cycling losses, and if cd is not reported, a worst-case value of 0.25 is assumed. However, cd is generally not reported for water-to-air coils like those used for GHPs. Nevertheless, a European standard test, EN14825 (European Committee for Standardization (CEN) 2022) does capture the impact of cycling and allows for determining a way to model these impacts on the overall efficiency of the unit. They report a standby loss coefficient Cc. Unlike cd for air-to-air equipment, a value of 1.0 for Cc would represent no cycling losses, and a default worst-case value of 0.9 is used if it is not reported (Piechurski, Szulgowaska-Zgrzywa and Danielewicz 2018). The implication of using this new method is that GHPs may appear to suffer energy losses during cycling, especially in climates with highly unbalanced thermal loads (e.g., predominantly heating- or cooling-dominated regions) or with long swing seasons or mild climates. In these climates, part-load operation occurs more frequently, and the steeper part-load factor penalty results in a higher modeled energy use. Single-stage GHP systems are impacted more than twostage and variable-speed compressor systems.

For GHPs, additional curves can also be used to set the efficiency and capacity as a function of water flow rate and airflow rate. These curves allow performance to be corrected for airflow rates and water flow rates different from the nominal conditions. However, care must be taken that these relationships are not double-counting the impact of cycling, which is generally handled by the part load factor. While manufacturer spec sheets do report efficiency and capacity at different water and airflow rates, single-stage equipment generally operates at a single flow rate, where the exact flow rate used might vary based on the equipment size and application. Only three water flow rates are reported in the spec sheet for this unit, while a wider variety of airflow rates are reported. Curves for different water flow rates are not used, with the assumption that any cycling losses are fully captured by the part load factor and a purely single-stage pump would always be used with a single-stage GHP. Curves for airflow, however, are provided to allow for the possibility of a variable-speed fan with a single-stage system. Curves for airflow also allow for the model to account for faults that may impede airflow that can occur in real equipment over time or due to poor installation. ## 2.2.6  Ground Thermal Conductivity

With the new ability to specify the thermal conductivity of the ground, 20 better guidance is required to help users pick the right properties for their job site. However, substantial variability in ground conductivity exists by location, time of year, and depth due to differences in the local soil makeup and the presence or absence of groundwater. Many GHP manufacturers recommend performing a site-specific conductivity measurement when considering installing a GHP, with details on how to take this measurement often appearing in product manuals. The default assumption at the start of this project for ground thermal conductivity is to use a value of 1.0 Btu/h-ft-°F. However, there are resources available that can help users make an educated guess at the soil type for a location, absent a more thorough site analysis, such as (X. W. Liu 2016) or (U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) 2019). The USDA data provides conductivity by soil and moisture type. Liu (2016) provides climate zone 50 th percentile (median), 25 th percentile (lower quartile), and 75 th percentile (upper quartile) estimates based on a natural gas well dataset from Southern Methodist University (Southern Methodist University n.d.). While the Southern Methodist University natural gas well dataset is measurements of deep well data, it was the best identified dataset that provides distributions of measurements within the range of expected values of various soil types from the USDA dataset.

20  See section 'Input Schema for HPXML' for updates to the HPXML schema made for this measure development.

The USDA dataset of soil thermal conductivities by climate zone is missing the conductivities for some climate zones, such as 7AK and 8AK. The Southern Methodist University data were combined with USDA data to get the most complete dataset possible. The USDA dataset was sorted by counties and aggregated to the respective IECC 2012 climate zones, then the upper quartile, lower quartile, and median values were computed for each of the missing climate zones. The resulting quartile values were repeated for some climate zones, as multiple sites had identical conductivity values across quartiles. For example, climate zone 1A had the same values of soil conductivity for the lower quartile, median, and the upper quartile. Climate zone 7AK had identical computed values for the lower quartile and median, and climate zone 8AK had identical computed values for the median and upper quartile. The latitudes and longitudes in the Southern Methodist University file were joined to the corresponding IECC 2012 climate zone. These data were then used to create distributions for ground conductivity by climate zone (Figure 12). Due to limitation of samples in climate zone 1A, the climate zone data were combined with samples from Florida in climate zone 2A. Discrete bins of ground conductivity values were created in ResStock. 21 These bins are [0.5-2.6] Btu/h-ft-°F with a step size of 0.3 Btu/h-ft-°F. Updating the default values to vary nationally will allow for a better estimate of the soil conductivity, absent additional information.

## 2.2.7  Ground Heat Exchanger Sizing Methodology

Several software tools (Table 4) have emerged based on the need for a way to optimize ground heat exchanger design to minimize the size required to meet building loads and perform satisfactorily over the life span of the system. The general design approach involves the designer specifying ground thermal properties, number and arrangement of boreholes, the heat pump loads (building loads or the loads on the ground heat exchanger), and the in-borehole design of the ground heat exchanger. Design methodologies involve specifying ground heat exchanger size directly based on longer-term building loads estimation using peak design conditions (design days) or determining the ground heat exchanger size using finer time-series resolution (often from monthly or hourly building loads data). The iterative approach uses an initial ground heat exchanger size determination using borehole constraints and heat pump specifications guided by the building loads, then iteratively adjusts the ground heat exchanger size until it meets the userspecified design criteria for the maximum and minimum entering fluid temperatures.

Ground heat exchanger sizing design practices or methods are often categorized based on the time resolution into five levels from level zero to four (L0 to L4) (Spitler and Javed 2016). The level zero (L0) practice is to use rules of thumb relating ground heat exchanger length to building peak heating or cooling loads or installed equipment capacity. The L0 tools are applicable to only smaller systems and heating-only applications (J. D. Spitler 2016). However, peak heating and cooling loads are variable year to year and do not show a clear correlation with annual heating and cooling loads, resulting in highly variable entering fluid temperature values across different years. Because of this limitation, several other techniques have been developed by researchers over the years that involve 'pulses' of heating and cooling loads. The L2 methods uses six pulses per year (three pulses for each heating and cooling season: annual, monthly, and a peak of user-specified duration). The L3 methods uses monthly peak pulses, and finally, the L4 methods uses hourly simulations. The time step resolution used in the design methodology has a significant effect on the accuracy of the predicted size requirements.

OpenStudio-HPXML currently uses an L1 approach to characterize the ground heat exchanger; see Appendix B for more details on the methodology. Residential building HVAC sizing is often determined by design-day calculations (ACCA Manual J). This approach results in a peak load calculation and a resulting HVAC system size to meet the peak load. The methods specified in ACCA Manual J and ACCA Manual S are implemented in OpenStudio-HPXML. See Section 2.4.2 GHP Sizing Methodology for the load inputs for the ground heat exchanger sizing.

During the HVAC sizing routine, no information about the monthly or hourly building loads is present. The result is that L2 through L4 approaches cannot be easily integrated into OpenStudio-HPXML. The integration of L2 to L4 approaches would require that the EnergyPlus model be simulated with an ideal load system to get an estimate of the time-series building loads. These loads would then need to get processed into the inputs necessary for the L2 to L4 approach. Then the ground heat exchanger L2 to L4 approach could be calculated. The impact of this level of integration would double the runtime of the OpenStudio-HPXML models as two annual simulations and file input/output would be required. The increase in runtime is the main reason OpenStudio-HPXML is staying with the Level 1 ground heat exchanger characterization algorithm.

The documentation distributed together with the g-function library (Spitler, et al. 2021) describes the interpolation methodology that a design tool can use to iteratively adjust and choose a suitable depth for a correctly sized ground heat exchanger. Each layout within each configuration in the original g-function library provides g-functions for five different segments of depth for each borehole, for the depths of 24 m (79 ft), 48 m (157 ft), 96 m (315 ft), 192 m (630 ft) and 384 m (1,260 ft). In actual energy simulation using OpenStudio-HPXML (using the EnergyPlus simulation engine), the borehole configurations generated will have different borehole depths than one of the discrete bins provided in the OpenEI g-function library. Hence, interpolation is necessary between the values given in the g-function library for an OpenStudio-HPXML run.

## 2.3  Electric Panel Modeling

This section summarizes the electrical information for the ducted heat pump upgrade. Electrical information is used in ResStock to assess whether the dwelling unit's electrical panel would become space constrained or over capacity due to this upgrade.

The electric load assumption for all GHP upgrades is borrowed from another heat pump upgrade, the air-to-air heat pump without electric backup. This is a limitation of this work.

The power rating of a ducted heat pump system is the sum of the power rating of the compressor, the air handler, and any electric backup allowed to operate simultaneously, which are summarized in Table 7. The heat pump compressor power rating is estimated based on its design heating capacity using a regression equation derived from product labels. The air handler power rating is estimated from the design airflow rate and OS-HPXML assumption of fan power. The electric resistance backup power rating is directly converted from its heating capacity at an efficiency of 100%. For more information on the modeling assumptions, see the Technical Reference Document (Reyna, et al. 2025).

Table 7. Default Power Rating of Ducted Heat Pump System Components

| Component            | Power Rating [W]                                  | Note                                                     |
|----------------------|---------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Heat pump compressor | (0.631 * Heating_Capacity [kBtu/h] + 1.615) * 240 | Regression of Minimum Circuit Amp, multiplied by voltage |
| Air handler          | Air_Flow [cfm] * Fan_Power [W/cfm]                | Default to OS-HPXML assumptions                          |

Ducted heat pumps require at least one 208/240V circuit, each of which is protected by a doublepole breaker. The specific number of circuits depend on whether the heat pump has backup heating (dual-fuel or electric) and how its indoor and outdoor units are configured along with the backup. Table 8 summarizes the wiring configuration for ducted heat pumps assumed in ResStock 2025 Release 1. For example, a dual-fuel heat pump is assumed to have a 120V air handler on one circuit and another 240V circuit for the compressor. For a heat pump with an electric backup, the air handler is commonly 240V to allow for the strip heat to be on the same circuit. This is also the assumption in ResStock 2025 Release 1. The maximum overcurrent protection allowed per circuit is assumed to be up to 50A. Therefore, more circuits may be required for larger heat pump systems, which correspond to the use of multiple compressors and/or heat kits in real life.

Although upgrading to a heat pump replaces both the heating and cooling systems, it may require more electrical circuits and breaker space than what becomes available by removing the existing HVAC equipment. The heat pump upgrade may also add more connected load to the panel, especially when the backup is sized for emergency use. The power rating of heat pump systems is driven by their heating configuration and the presence of electric resistance backup. Therefore, homes in colder climates are more likely to face a capacity constraint when upgrading to a heat pump since a backup is often required to supplement the heat pump performance and they are more likely to have fuel heating previously.

Electrical panel constraint modeling is added to ResStock 2025 Release 1 via OpenStudioHPXML measures in 2025. As new electric loads are added into the home during an improvement project, the electrical panel may not have enough capacity or space headroom to accommodate these new circuits. Thus, the panel may become constrained, requiring an electrical upgrade that may include upsizing the service entrance. Panel constraints can also be mitigated in other ways without the panel or service being upsized, by using load shedding devices, circuit sharing devices, or a smart panel to allow more loads to be connected; lowpower, combination, or smart appliances to reduce electric loads at the source; slim tandem breakers or subpanels for more space; and more. The panel constraint estimate from the upgrade measure allows for the costing of any potential electrical upgrade or mitigation, thus helping to capture the total cost of upgrade more holistically across the housing stock.

Panel capacity and space constraints are modeled using a service load and a breaker space calculation, respectively. The load calculation is based on 2023 National Electrical Code's loadbased method in Section 220, which guides how the load diversity should be estimated and summed to estimate the total load connected to the panel. When this total load converted to ampacity exceeds the service rating of the panel, the panel is considered capacity constrained in ResStock 2025 Release 1. Similarly, the panel is considered space constrained when the total occupied breaker space counted from the bottom-up exceeds the rated space count of the panel. The panel's capacity and space ratings are probabilistically sampled based on other housing characteristics such as heating fuel and the presence of certain appliances. Users are encouraged to reevaluate these constraint metrics according to other considerations, such as a margin of safety to account for uncertainty in the modeling process. For more details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al. 2025).

## 2.4 OpenStudio-HPXML Measure Inputs

This measure includes a water-to-air GHP with a single-stage compressor, variable-speed fan rated at a heating coefficient of performance (COP) of 3.8 and a rated cooling energy efficiency ratio (EER) of 18.6. The ground heat exchanger is a rectangular and vertically drilled single Utube design with enhanced grout and piping. This section will describe the inputs, sizing methodology, and modeling methodology for both the heat pump and the ground heat exchanger.

There are numerus ResStock arguments assigned in the HVAC Heating Efficiency characteristic. The arguments being specified (different than the default OpenStudio-HPXML defaults) that help distinguish these technologies from other heat pump upgrade measures are below.

## 2.4.1  GHP Backup System

GHPs typically do not have integrated backup systems because they are designed to operate efficiently across a wide range of temperatures by leveraging the relatively constant ground temperature. The inherent stability and efficiency of GHPs eliminate the need for integrated backups in most installations. The GHP upgrade measures will not be modeled with integrated backup systems.

## 2.4.2  GHP Sizing Methodology

The design heating and cooling loads are calculated in accordance with Air Conditioning Contractors of America (ACCA) Manual J (ACCA 2016), and the equipment size is selected based on ACCA Manual S (ACCA 2014) using a routine included in OpenStudio-HPXML. ACCA Manual J is a method for calculating residential building loads. The heating and cooling design temperatures are picked as the '99%' heating outdoor temperature and '1%' cooling outdoor temperature; that is, only 1% of hours in the year are colder or hotter, respectively, than the design temperature. ACCA Manual S uses these load calculations to select appropriately sized HVAC equipment, prioritizing cooling load to avoid issues like short cycling and poor humidity control. Using the dwelling unit's characteristics and location, the sizing strategy will be set to heat\_pump\_sizing\_methodology=MaxLoad . According to the OpenStudio-HPXML documentation:

MaxLoad : Autosized heat pumps have their nominal capacity sized based on the larger of heating/cooling design loads, while taking into account the heat pump's reduced capacity at the design temperature, such that no backup heating should be necessary.

## 2.4.3 Grout and Pipe

For both the grout type and pipe type, HPXML provides inputs as 'standard' or 'thermally enhanced.' OpenStudio-HPXML uses grout conductivity of 0.75 Btu/h-ft-°F for standard grout (IGSPHA 2014) and 1.2 Btu/h-ft-°F 26 for thermally enhanced grout (Liu, et al. 2021). OpenStudio-HPXML uses pipe conductivity of 0.23 Btu/h-ft-°F for standard pipe and 0.4 Btu/hft-°F for thermally enhanced pipe (Liu, et al. 2021). Per conversations with industry, systems being installed today typically use thermally enhanced grout and pipes. The GHP measures use 'thermally enhanced' grout and pipe conductivities.

## 2.4.4 Single-Stage GHP Heating and Cooling Efficiency

The compressor type significantly impacts the EER and COP of a GHP by affecting how efficiently the system transfers heat and matches the load. Two-stage and variable-speed compressors can fine-tune this process, reducing energy losses and improving heat exchange efficiency, especially under partial load conditions. Single-stage compressors use more energy through frequent on-off cycles. These extra cycles require more energy to start up and cause inefficient operation, either by overworking or underworking, leading to temperature fluctuations and unnecessary energy use.

## 2.5 Limitations and Concerns

1. The ground heat exchanger design is based on a Level 1 method (described in Section 2.2.7), and other more detailed methods have been established. Given that this workflow explicitly targets residential buildings, and the approach is used to model multifamily buildings on a unit-by-unit basis (described in more detail below as an additional limitation and concern), ground heat exchangers are relatively small, and individual boreholes have relatively little interaction with each other. Level 1 methods are therefore more appropriate for the simpler geometries that occur in residential buildings. In addition, the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow is very explicitly designed with speed as a key priority. Annual hourly simulations of a dwelling unit are designed to take seconds rather than minutes, which enables other applications like BEopt™ and ResStock to run thousands to hundreds of thousands of simulations quickly to help analyze the optimal building design and the housing stock at a national scale. Implementing the more detailed approaches would require time-series loads, which would require running building models multiple times (once to get the loads, then again with the GHP installed), more than doubling the run time. Given these considerations, a Level 1 approach is considered appropriate for the goals of this particular workflow. Technical advisory group feedback has also indicated that a Level 1 approach is appropriate for many residential applications.
2. The g-function library is static. The library of g-functions is static and is not planned to be updated frequently. It is recommended that over time, the library be updated with the outputs of GHEDesigner as a future effort. This introduces some degree of maintenance burden if and when new approaches (or new, more accurate g-functions) are introduced. This can also introduce uncertainty over time about where certain inputs came from, as exemplified by the currently implemented g-functions that are poorly documented. To address this, thorough documentation (such as this document, but also in the OpenStudioHPXML documentation and the code itself) will clearly state the source of all empirical data, as well as any assumptions made within the workflow. The team also created a direct connection to an existing g-function library so that if the library is updated, new gfunctions can easily be downloaded and integrated into the workflow.
3. Large uncertainty exists in soil thermal property data. To account for this, documentation will point users to some of the best available resources with thermal properties of different soil types, as well as recent work that estimates the average soil conductivity as a function of climate zone (X. W. Liu 2016). This can help guide users to make more informed guesses on soil properties if desired. However, given the large amount of uncertainty that exists, we will not implement location-specific defaults to avoid giving the impression of a level of precision that does not exist.
4. OpenStudio-HPXML models multifamily buildings on a unit-by-unit basis, rather than modeling all units within a building simultaneously. There are several implications to this modeling assumption for GHPs. The ground heat exchanger for a single unit is much smaller than would be required for the entire building, which means different g-functions would be used for this approach, and less interaction between boreholes occurs in the current approach. This likely means that this approach

- underestimates the total number of boreholes required for supporting the whole multifamily building. This may also underestimate the costs associated with installing a GHP, especially around the ground heat exchanger sizing. While costs may be estimated for the whole building and then attributed to individual units, the reduced number of boreholes will also lead to lower estimated installed costs. Given the complexity of dealing with the interactions (which are not a simple function of the overall size of the building, but depend on several other factors as well), this is documented as a known limitation and area of caution when understanding the results that come from modeling multifamily buildings and community-scale GHP installations. Note that large high rises (&gt;8 stories) are excluded from having GHPs applied entirely because of this limitation.
5. GHP performance curves are based on a single unit. The approach to modeling the GHP used here is based on generating a performance map for a single unit, rather than trying to generate 'generic' curves that represent the performance of this technology as a product class. Some care has been taken in picking the unit used to specify the performance curves. The approach is to pick a product with rated performance that seems representative of the typical efficiency seen in this class of product relative to other ENERGY STAR-rated GHPs. The performance curves are also based on a product from a major U.S. based manufacturer of this technology with a long track record to try to ensure that performance is closer to representative, rather than picking the product with the highest efficiency on the market today or the newest product to hit the market. There is some inherent uncertainty in picking any single set of performance curves to represent an entire class of product, but the NLR residential team has had success with this approach representing multiple products of similar design (same refrigerant, similar condenser lengths, etc.) for other technologies like ASHPs and heat pump water heaters. Unit-specific product curves have been used to represent heat pump water heater performance in the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow, and validation efforts have shown that the curves do not vary substantially from manufacturer to manufacturer for other technologies. The performance curves used here are also for a single-stage GHP.
6. A new approach to cycling losses is being used for GHPs compared to other residential HVAC systems. This new approach is based on a European standard and has steeper penalties on energy use at very low part-load conditions compared to the approach used for air-to-air systems such as air conditioners and ASHPs. This could potentially lead to GHPs assuming more energy is wasted during cycling, particularly in climates where there are unbalanced loads (heavily heating- or cooling-dominated climates) or mild climates when comparing these options in different upgrade scenarios.
7. Calculations are performed for only a single year. OpenStudio-HPXML is a tool designed primarily for annual calculations, rather than calculations over multiple years. For GHPs, if the heating loads are substantially larger than the cooling loads, this may lead to a thermal imbalance (Saaly and Maghoul 2019). Over the course of multiple years of operation, extracting heat from the ground can lead to changes in the soil temperature. This leads to decreases in the outlet temperature from the ground heat exchanger and reduced efficiency of the GHP. The magnitude of this change depends on the size of the ground heat exchanger, the difference in heating and cooling loads, and how long the system has been operating. As ground temperature changes over the course of a period

much longer than the annual simulations performed here, it is difficult to account for this effect over the lifetime of the GHP. Additional work would be required to try to correct for this effect over the lifetime of the system.

## 3  Results

This measure replaces the existing applicable HVAC equipment with a new GHP system. The measure requires an existing system to be in place, as some inputs from the existing system are recycled for use with the new system. Specifically, this measure implements a single-stage compressor, variable-speed fan water-to-air heat pump system with a vertical ground heat exchanger.

Figure 25 shows the average heating COP for the single-stage GHP. The COP does not include duct losses, which may be substantial, particularly in existing homes where ducts are often located in unconditioned spaces and are not upgraded as part of the GHP measure. These losses can significantly degrade overall system performance relative to the rated equipment COP. The reported COPs focus solely on the efficiency of the equipment itself.

Observed heating COPs range between 2.0 and 2.4 across climate zones, with lower values in the coldest states. Colder regions not only experience lower ground temperatures (which reduce heat pump efficiency due to decreased entering water temperatures), but also tend to have higher annual heating loads, further stressing the system. Additionally, these COP values reflect simulated operation conditions that diverge from the idealized lab test settings under which manufacturers report rated COPs. In practice, GHPs often operate at part-load conditions where cycling is frequent, especially during shoulder seasons. This introduces cycling losses not captured in steady-state ratings.

Despite these inefficiencies, GHPs still deliver substantial energy savings over conventional combustion systems. Even high-efficiency condensing furnaces top out at annual fuel utilization efficiencies (AFUEs) around 0.96, and many existing systems-especially older, non-condensing furnaces-operate in the range of 0.6 to 0.8. Therefore, even a GHP with a field COP in the 2.0 to 2.4 range offers a significant performance improvement over a typical fossil fuel heating system.

## 3.4  Electric Panel Impact

This section details the impact the single-stage GHP upgrade will have on electrical panels.

First, Figure 28 and Figure 29 show information about the ResStock 2025 Release 1 baseline housing stock electric panel distribution. Figure 28 shows in the first column, 'All', that about 35% of all dwelling units have 0 or only 1 breaker space available. Figure 29 shows in the first column that 97.7% of electric panels have no existing capacity constraint for the baseline housing stock, but that the other 2.3% of electric panels already have an existing capacity constraint.

Capacity constraints in the existing baseline stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. However, it's important to recognize that this level of uncertainty applies to all panel results, including those that look reasonable. Hence, the capacity constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on.

Before this single-stage GHP retrofit would take place, the panels experiencing a capacity constraint would likely need to address this issue first. Users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose pre-existing capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.

## Residential GHP Mechanical Equipment Types and Efficiencies

This section reviews (1) the typical mechanical equipment types and their market penetration in the U.S. residential building stock, (2) the equipment rated heating and cooling efficiencies along with historical ENERGY STAR specifications, and (3) ENERGY STAR certified unit shipments and market penetration. The discussion in this section helps prioritize what type of heat pump is modeled in the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow and what default efficiency values are reasonable based on when the equipment was installed.

## GHP Mechanical Distribution System Market Penetration

There are four major types of GHP systems based on heat transfer system design:

## 1. Water-to-Air

In these types of GHP systems, the ground heat exchanger is composed of either an open- or closed-loop circuit containing water or a water-antifreeze solution. The heat transfers between the ground heat exchanger and the heat pump system, which operates on a vapor-compression refrigeration cycle. The use of refrigerant is limited to within the heat pump system itself. The heat transfer between the heat pump system and the interior of the building is achieved through forced air movement through heat exchangers transferring heat between the heat pump system and the indoor air through ducts.

## 2. Water-to-Water

In these types of GHP systems, the ground heat exchanger is the same as in water-to-air systems. However, the heat transfer to the indoor environment is done using a second hydronic loop. Heat is exchanged between the ground heat exchanger and the heat pump system operation on a vapor-compression refrigeration cycle. The hydronic loop absorbs heat from the heat pump system via heat exchanger(s) and circulates that heat to various parts of the building using one or more closed circuit hydronic (water) loops running through baseboard hydronic radiators or radiant floors. Water-to-water systems perform much better in space heating than space cooling in residential buildings, with some manufacturers not recommending using water-to-water heat pumps for residential cooling (Dandelion Energy 2023).

## 3. Combined Systems (Water-to-Air and Water-to-Water Hybrids)

These systems use a combination of water-to-air and water-to-water systems to exchange heat between the ground heat exchanger and the indoor environment.

## 4. Direct Geo-Exchange

These systems use a refrigerant directly in the ground heat exchanger, instead of a waterantifreeze mix, to exchange heat between the heat pump system and the ground. The heat exchange on the building-interior side can either be a water-to-air, water-to-water, or combined system. These systems are less commonly used due to the risks of corrosion to the buried copper tubing carrying the refrigerant, which sits in direct contact with the soil, leading to potential system failure as well as the potential for environmental issues such as groundwater contamination. As a result, these systems are also more tightly regulated by local jurisdictions.

## Ground Heat Exchanger Configurations

There are two types of ground heat exchanger systems for GHPs: open- and closed-loop systems.

## 1. Open-Loop Systems

Open-loop systems use a well or a surface body water (like a local pond or lake) directly as heat exchange fluid that circulates through the GHP system. Feasibility of the use of open-loop systems, however, depends on location, local codes, and regulations (U.S. DOE. 2023).

## 2. Closed-Loop Systems

Closed-loop GHP systems use a constant volume of a working fluid (usually a mixture of water and antifreeze) that circulates in a closed loop (piping that is joined end-to-end), absorbing heat in one segment and rejecting heat through another segment of the closed loop without any exchange of mass of the working fluid between the heat pump system and the surrounding environment as in an open-loop system. Based on how the ground heat exchanger are installed, there are four major types, two horizontal closed loop, one vertical closed loop, and one pond/lake closed loop (U.S. DOE. 2023).

## a. Horizontal Closed Loop

Horizontal ground heat exchanger systems are deemed by U.S. DOE as generally the most costeffective closed-loop configuration for residential (particularly for new construction) installations where sufficient land is available (U.S. DOE. 2023), but are less commonly installed as many sites don't have the land area or are retrofits. The trenches for the horizontal loop systems are generally dug about 4 ft deep. The most common layouts use either:

- Two pipes, one buried at 6 ft and the other at 4 ft, or
- Two pipes placed side-by-side at 5 ft in the ground in a 2 ft wide trench.

The Slinky method of looping pipe allows the use of more pipe in a shorter trench, resulting in lower installation cost.

## b. Vertical Closed Loop

Vertical ground loop GHP systems are common for large commercial buildings and schools because the land area required is small compared to horizonal loop systems. The boreholes for vertical loop systems are typically approximately 4 inches in diameter and are drilled approximately 20 ft apart. The boreholes are 100 to 400 ft deep. Two pipes connected at the bottom with a U-bend to form a loop are inserted into the hole and grouted to improve performance. The vertical loops are connected with horizontal manifolds placed in trenches (U.S. DOE. 2023) (Self et al. 2013).

## c. Pond/Lake Closed Loop

If the installation site has adequate access to a body of water, such as a pond or lake, a surface water system may be the most suitable option. A supply line pipe is run underground from the building to the water and coiled into circles at least 8 ft under the surface to prevent freezing. There is no specific way or orientation of looping for this type of system, but the coils should only be placed in water source that meets minimum volume, depth, and quality requirements (U.S. DOE. 2023). Rivers are not ideal for this application due to their unpredictable behaviors, including flooding and droughts that can damage loop systems, as well as hazards due to moving debris (Omer 2008), (Ma 2004).

## Ground Heat Exchanger Configurations in the U.S. Residential Stock

Across the data sources reviewed, there is general agreement that most of the U.S. residential ground heat exchanger configurations are closed-loop horizontal or closed-loop vertical systems. Horizontal and vertical closed-loop systems make up 84%-93% of the ground heat exchanger installations. Ground heat exchangers that use groundwater or surface water make up 4%-16%. The only source with reporting on direct geo-exchange systems, Battocletti and Glassley, reported that these systems made up about 3% of ground heat exchanger installations (Battocletti and Glassley 2013). There is some disagreement across data sources about the relative prevalence of horizontal and vertical ground heat exchanger systems. Liu et al. 2019 (Liu, et al. 2019) and Battocletti and Glassley (Battocletti and Glassley 2013) state that vertical ground loops are more common, while Lund and Boyd (Lund and Boyd 2016) state that horizontal ground loops are more common. The Lund and Boyd 2016 study focuses on residential systems only, while the other two studies combine residential and commercial. It is possible that the Battocletti and Glassley and Liu et al. sources predict a higher fraction of vertical ground loop configurations because they include commercial ground heat exchanger systems, which are typically vertical installations. Where sufficient land is available, residential horizontal ground loop systems are the most cost-effective ground heat exchanger option (U.S. DOE. 2023).
