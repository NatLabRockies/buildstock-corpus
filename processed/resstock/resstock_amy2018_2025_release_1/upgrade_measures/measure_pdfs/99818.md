## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit end-use load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover most high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. This results in a time-series profile broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model represents the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in (Reyna, et al. 2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are provided in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (National Laboratory of the Rockies 2025).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape package: light air sealing, attic floor insulation, and duct sealing and insulation upgrade.

The advanced air sealing, attic floor insulation, and duct sealing and insulation upgrade achieved 14% modeled total site energy savings (461 TWh) for the U.S. residential building stock (Figure 3). These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 18% stock natural gas savings (261 TWh)
- 9% stock electricity savings (141 TWh)
- 19% stock fuel oil savings (33 TWh)
- 18% stock propane savings (27 TWh).

## 1  Technology Summary

This upgrade package, which includes light air sealing, attic floor insulation, and duct sealing and insulation, combines three key areas of a retrofit measure. As stated in previous reports about these topics, the goal of these upgrades is to reduce heat losses in winter and retain cool air in the conditioned space during summer. Air sealing alone does not eliminate the need for insulation (U.S. Department of Energy 2025). Insulation and air sealing work best together to improve comfort and energy efficiency. Therefore, they are often implemented as a package to address complementary aspects of a home's energy performance, and their combined effect is much greater than any single measure alone. Together, these upgrades help to lower energy bills, enhance indoor comfort, and improve indoor air quality year round (Lou, Phoung and Parker 2026).

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

## Light Air Sealing

The light air sealing methodology in this measure package is reused from an existing measure, and the background information is restated here (Brossman, White and Phoung 2026). Air sealing involves identifying and closing large and small gaps, cracks, and penetrations in the home's primary or secondary pressure boundaries, such as around windows, doors, attic hatches, and plumbing or wiring holes (U.S. Department of Energy 2025). These leaks, whether large or small, accumulate and can allow a substantial amount of heated or cooled air escape. By reducing air leaks, air sealing improves indoor comfort, maintains more stable temperatures, and reduces the workload on the heating and cooling system. Air sealing is especially important in older homes, which typically have more leaks (Green Building Alliance 2025).

Refer to the light air sealing documentation for more information on this topic (Brossman, White and Phoung 2026).

## Attic Floor Insulation

The attic floor insulation methodology in this measure package is also reused from an existing measure, and the background information is restated here (Phoung, Lou and White 2026). Insulating the attic floor reduces heat transfer between the living space and the unconditioned attic. In colder climates, this keeps heat inside during winter. In warmer climates, it helps keep the living space cooler. The effectiveness of insulation is measured by its R-value; a higher R-value indicates better resistance to heat flow. Installing insulation in existing homes typically involves blowing loose-fill insulation over the attic floor, especially in unfinished attics. The optimal insulation level depends on the climate zone and the home's existing insulation. Refer to the attic insulation documentation for guidance on recommended R-values (as established by International Energy Conservation Code) and installation approaches (Phoung, Lou and White 2026).

## Duct Sealing and Insulation

The duct insulation and sealing methodology in this measure package is similarly reused from an existing measure, and the background information is restated here (Lou and Liu 2026). Ducts distribute heated or cooled air throughout the house. In many homes, especially older ones, these ducts have leaks that allow conditioned air to escape into unconditioned or semi-conditioned spaces like attics or crawlspaces. As a result, the system must work harder and use more energy to maintain the desired temperature. Duct sealing addresses these leaks using mastic, metal tape, or an aerosol-based sealant, depending on the situation. Sealing ducts improves system efficiency, maintains more consistent room temperatures, and enhances indoor air quality by preventing dirty attic or crawlspace air from entering the ducts. Duct sealing can also reduce unintentional pressure imbalances, which may cause indoor air quality or combustion safety concerns. See the duct sealing documentation for more information on methods and materials (Lou and Liu 2026).

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

If this work were to be done as a retrofit, the correct order for implementing this upgrade package is: (1) light air sealing, (2) duct sealing and insulation, and (3) attic floor insulation. The building science behind this ordering has been previously described in another report (Lou, Phoung and Parker 2026), and the information is restated here. Sealing leaks before adding insulation ensures that the insulation performs as intended. It also makes leaks easier to identify and reduces the burden on field staff since they are not working around additional insulation. Once air sealing targets are met, it is essential to address the HVAC system by sealing any leaky ducts to prevent the loss of conditioned air, particularly in unconditioned spaces like attics or crawlspaces. Attic floor insulation should be installed last. While highly effective, it must follow air and duct sealing to avoid covering up critical areas that require sealing and to ensure ducts running through the attic are properly sealed and insulated beforehand.

Therefore, we assume that upgrades will be implemented in the following sequence: light air sealing is applied first to applicable dwelling units, with the goal of achieving a 25% reduction in the infiltration rate measured by an ACH50 change.

After air sealing, HVAC ducts in applicable homes are sealed and insulated to achieve the following:

- A maximum of 5% duct air leakage to the outside
- Duct insulation level of R-8.

Attic floor insulation is the upgraded in applicable dwelling units to the following levels:

- R-49 in climate zone 1
- R-60 in climate zones 2 through 8.

Note: when modeling these upgrades in ResStock, the order does not affect the outputs of the model. All upgrades are applied at the same time to the model file. More details about the modeling assumptions for these three measures can be found in the accompanying documentation (Brossman, White and Phoung 2026) (Phoung, Lou and White 2026) (Lou and Liu 2026).

## 2  Applicability

The light air sealing upgrade is applied to dwelling units with infiltration rates greater than or equal to 10 ACH50.

The attic floor insulation upgrade is applied only to dwelling units with unfinished attics (vented and unvented attic types in ResStock). Specifically, the upgrade is applied when attic floor insulation is below R-49 in climate zone 1 and below R-60 in climate zones 2 through 8.

Duct sealing applies to dwelling units with duct leakage to the outside greater than 6% or duct insulation rated below R-6. Dwelling units with 0% leakage to the outside and uninsulated ducts are not applicable, as these configurations typically indicate that the ducts are located entirely within conditioned spaces (Lou and Liu 2026).

Further details on the applicability criteria and residential building stock saturation for each of these three measures can be found in the accompanying documentation (Brossman, White and Phoung 2026) (Phoung, Lou and White 2026) (Lou and Liu 2026).

## 3  Modeling Approach

The modeling approach of the three individual measures can be found in the accompanying documentation (Brossman, White and Phoung 2026) (Phoung, Lou and White 2026) (Lou and Liu 2026).

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025 to assess whether the existing panel has enough capacity or breaker space to accommodate new loads from an energy retrofit. For details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al. 2025). This measure does not introduce new electrical loads or circuits, so it has no impact on the electrical panel. However, electrical panel metrics are included as a standard output for this and all other envelope measures. Any panel constraints appearing in the upgrade results reflect pre-existing conditions, as this measure does not worsen them.
