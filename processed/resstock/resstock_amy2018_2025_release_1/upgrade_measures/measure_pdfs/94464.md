## Executive Summary

This report documents the development and simulation of the Efficient Electric Vehicle (EV) Adoption with Level 2 (L2) Charging and Demand Flexibility (DF) measure within ResStock™. ResStock is a physics-based, bottom-up modeling tool designed to represent the diversity of the U.S. housing stock and estimate building energy use and bills for scenarios at both regional and national scales.

The Efficient EV Adoption with L2 Charging and DF measure adds one high-efficiency EV and one L2 charger to every occupied dwelling unit and enables DF by suspending charging during predefined on-peak hours. EVs are modeled to have a 15% improvement in efficiency compared to the baseline. This approach models a future scenario that combines the effects of widespread EV adoption, improved EV efficiency technology, and managed charging behavior on the residential energy sector. Results show that full adoption increases national residential site energy use by approximately 6.3%, driven entirely by additional EV charging demand. However, efficiency improvements reduce total EV electricity consumption by about 15% relative to an equivalent scenario using baseline EVs. While DF does not significantly alter total energy consumption, it effectively reduces on-peak electricity demand, shifting load to off-peak hours. These shifts can lead to rebound effects as charging resumes and slightly increase the risk of unavailable battery capacity for a small share of households. The broad applicability of the measure allows users to combine results with the baseline and other EV scenarios to simulate different levels of adoption and participation. These custom scenarios can be designed to model EV technology penetration and behavior, and leverage ResStock granularity to construct adoption scenarios based on housing attributes or occupant demographics.

This measure provides a method for analyzing the interactions between residential energy demand and transportation electrification. By aligning charging behavior with other occupantdriven loads, users can evaluate strategies to balance household energy needs with grid reliability. The results highlight the potential of managed charging to mitigate peak loads and the need for continued innovation in flexible load management, while demonstrating the benefits of EV efficiency improvements. As EV adoption grows and grid integration technologies advance, this work supports data-driven decisions that promote a more efficient, resilient, and affordable energy future.

## Measure Motivation

Utilities, auto manufacturers, charging providers, and EV supply equipment manufacturers are working together to develop technologies (such as smart chargers) and services (such as time-of use rates) that enable efficient EV-grid integration. With potential continued EV adoption (over 10% of sales in the United States in 2024 [IEA 2025] and 25.3% of sales in California [California Energy Commission 2025] in 2024), coordination between the residential energy sector, transportation sector, and electric grid will play a key role in maintaining affordability and reliability. Without such coordination, increases in peak demand may require costly grid upgrades, increase electricity prices, and increase the risk of outages or degraded service.

This ResStock ™ (Reyna et al. 2025) measure applies one EV with a 15% efficiency improvement and an L2 charger to each occupied dwelling unit model in the baseline scenario and enables DF for all the chargers so that they suspend charging during peak periods.  The selected efficiency improvement reflects the average of expected fuel economy changes over the next 5 to 10 years for 300-mile and 400-mile range compact, small SUV, and midsize SUV vehicles in the 2024 Annual Technology Baseline data 1 . All existing units with level 1 (L1) chargers are upgraded to L2.  By modeling widespread adoption of EVs, chargers, and DF, ResStock users can combine models from the baseline, relevant measures without DF, and this measure to create their own unique EV and DF saturation scenarios. This allows nuanced scenarios in which only segments of households adopt EVs or shift their charging load. Such a method can be used to model EV and charger adoption scenarios and examine the resulting energy and power impacts on the residential building sector. The DF implementation in this measure helps quantify the benefit of a DF program and can contribute to alleviating the potential stress that a local electric grid may incur due to the charging of EVs at peak hours.

## 2  Technology Summary

## 2.1  Introduction to Electric Vehicles in ResStock

EVs are an increasingly important component of residential energy demand, with charging behavior often correlated with other household loads due to shared occupancy and behavioral patterns. The current ResStock baseline reflects 2023 EV saturation levels, which corresponds to a small number of representative dwelling unit models with EVs (1.05% of all datapoints). For each EV-owning dwelling unit, ResStock assigns specific vehicle properties, driving behavior, and charging behavior dependent on location, demographics (income and tenure), or occupancy - factors that also influence other household load profiles. While the baseline includes a limited number of EVs, ResStock's EV measures enable scenario analysis of future adoption and charging patterns.

This measure comprises three components: adoption of an EV 15% more efficient than baseline EVs, adoption of an L2 charger, and EV charging DF. EV charging power in ResStock depends on the vehicle charger type, whereas EV charging demand depends on the characteristics of the EV and driver behavior. When an EV is assigned to a dwelling unit in ResStock, the characteristics of the vehicle, driving behavior, and charging behavior are predetermined in the sampling routine, using distributions sourced from the 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) (U.S. EIA 2020) and other datasets (Speake et al. 2025).

ResStock leverages physics-based modeling, where high-level EV and driving behavior ResStock inputs are translated to EnergyPlus inputs to model the charging energy demand of an individual dwelling unit, as shown in Figure 1. The ResStock EV characteristics that inform the energy model and influence the charging energy demand include the (1) EV battery/vehicle type, (2) annual vehicle miles traveled (VMT), and (3) at-home charging fraction. The battery/vehicle type determines EV efficiency and battery capacity, while VMT influences the time spent driving, which in turn determines charging demand. The at-home charging fraction describes the share of the vehicle's electricity demand met by the residential charger. These inputs are assigned to all households in the ResStock baseline regardless of whether the EV is modeled. The charger type is not predetermined for all dwelling units and is instead explicitly defined in a measure. The EV charger power is assumed constant; however, the timing and duration of charging events are highly variable across models due to varying vehicle parameters, driving behaviors, and occupancy patterns. The underlying EV model accounts for charging and discharging losses by scaling the driving power with ambient temperature.

![Image](94464_images/image_000002_c922764a7518cdd77103698ef6a1175093675f54a763a27ea77e3739f2e3c4fa.png)

Baseline EV charging availability schedules indicate when a vehicle is plugged in and available to charge. These schedules are determined by the dwelling unit's occupancy schedule, whereas the periods of active charging are dependent on the availability schedule and the EV's state of charge (SOC). EV charging availability schedules in ResStock are generated stochastically based on the presence of an individual occupant assigned as the driver. During an 'away period' for the driver, the vehicle is assumed to be driven immediately after the occupant leaves and just before they return home. While the baseline charging availability schedule is dependent only upon the driver's occupancy status, the duration and magnitude of charging energy depend on the VMT, the charger power, vehicle properties, and fraction charged at home, as discussed further in Section 4.1. The driving schedules are dependent on stochastic occupant behavior, but do not explicitly capture seasonal variations in travel.

More detailed information regarding baseline EV modeling and scheduling in ResStock can be found in Speake et al. (2025), which provides further details on EV stock characterization, model input generation, battery modeling, and analysis of baseline results.

## 3  Applicability

The 'Efficient EV Adoption With Level 2 Charging and DF' measure assigns an efficient EV, an L2 charger, and a flexible charging schedule to all occupied dwelling units. This logic corresponds to 87.9% of the existing housing stock. Dwelling units with an EV in the baseline are upgraded to a more efficient EV, and units with an L1 charger are upgraded to an L2 charger. Other inputs that influence charging demand, such as annual miles driven or fraction charged at home, remain unchanged from the baseline. Only a single EV is adopted per dwelling unit, as modeling multiple vehicles is not currently supported. An EV demand flexibility measure is applied to all units so that EV charging energy is avoided during peak periods. This measure design allows flexibility when analyzing scenarios of adoption. For example, a user can select a percentage of a specific segment to adopt this measure while using the results from the baseline simulation for the remaining models. Units in multi-family buildings are included in the measure. Although multi-family households may not own the charging infrastructure or see its impact on their utility bills in practice, we model them consistently with other housing types, treating EV energy as a direct household energy cost. We also assume that multi-family chargers are always available, which may not be true in practice, and therefore the charging event durations may not reflect actual events.

## 4  Modeling Approach

## 4.1  Electric Vehicle Charging and Discharging Modeling

To model baseline residential EV charging demand in ResStock, our approach: (1) characterizes regional inputs that affect EV battery parameters, driving behavior, and charger characteristics; (2) assigns these inputs as arguments to individual dwelling unit models; (3) generates occupancy-driven charging and discharging schedules; and (4) simulates building energy models with integrated EVs. We associate each EV with an at-home charger, and report only the charging energy of the dwelling unit, as charging energy away from home is not modeled. No vehicle-to-building energy exchange is considered in ResStock. To apply this measure to the baseline, we repeat Steps 2-4 using updated inputs to assign EV ownership, EV efficiency, charger level, and DF schedules.

More detailed information regarding baseline EV modeling and scheduling in ResStock can be found in Speake et al. (2025), which describes the EV stock characterization, model input generation, battery modeling, and analysis of baseline results.

## Electric Vehicle Stock Characterization

Included in the baseline ResStock inputs are probability distributions that describe the likelihood of EV-related attributes being assigned to dwelling units. These EV-related inputs are part of ResStock's broader stock characterization process, which defines detailed building, occupant, and technology characteristics for the U.S. residential building stock (see Section 3 Reyna et al. [2025] for more detail). EV input distributions are sampled to produce EV ownership profiles for each building model. The baseline of ResStock assigns each dwelling unit a value for EV ownership, the at-home charging fraction, the vehicle type that provides the battery capacity and efficiency, and the VMT per year. If a dwelling unit has an EV, an EV charger is applied, and its power draw is determined based on the charger level. Plug-in hybrid electric vehicles are not currently modeled in ResStock.

The ResStock EV implementation draws data from the TEMPO ™  model (Muratori et al. 2021), EIA RECS 2020 (U.S. EIA 2020), the National Household Travel Survey (Federal Highway Administration 2022), vehicle registration data from Experian 2 , the No Place Like Home study (Ge et al. 2021), and the Autonomie model 3 . The distributions of these options reflect 2023 saturation levels of EV technology and operation in the United States, ensuring that EV-related measures capture expected diversity 4 . All dwelling units are sampled with percentage of vehicle charge at home, a vehicle type, and the VMT. These options are only relevant in the baseline for dwelling units with an EV but are used to define vehicle and driving characteristics for dwelling units that adopt an EV in this measure. Table 1 describes the ResStock characteristics, or dependencies, that influence the distribution of EV-related options. The dependencies shown directly influence the EV inputs, but the dependencies themselves also have upstream inputs, which have an indirect influence on EV characteristics.

## Vehicle Schedules

We generate two EV schedules as inputs to the vehicle battery model, (1) a charging availability schedule, which indicates when a vehicle is plugged in and available to charge, and (2) a driving schedule, during which the EV battery is discharging energy. Both schedules are derived from the dwelling unit's occupancy schedule, and the driving schedule also depends on the assigned ev\_hours\_per\_week argument. The durations of charging energy events are dependent on the charging availability schedule and the SOC. Figure 3 shows a snapshot of relevant schedules for a single EV driver. The schedule's values represent the fraction of each 15-minute timestep during which the event occurs, and values less than one correspond to events shorter than 15 minutes.

## Electric Vehicle Modeling

The charging availability and driving schedules are aligned with the stochastic occupancy model (based on American Time Use Survey data) in OpenStudio-HPXML. We calculate the total hours driven per week using the ev\_average\_mph (calculated from Federal Highway Administration [2022]) and ev\_miles\_per\_year arguments from the 'Electric Vehicle Battery' and 'Annual Miles Driven' input files, respectively, which inform the charging and discharging schedule generation. The battery model determines the actual power draw during the charging and discharging periods. An example of what these schedules look like is presented in Figure 3. The charging schedule algorithm assumes that the occupant plugs in their vehicle as soon as they arrive home and keeps it plugged in until they leave. Thus, the charging availability schedule is identical to the occupancy schedule of the driver, but the schedule of actual charging typically differs. This scenario makes the battery eligible to be charged anytime the EV is at home if the SOC is below the maximum level. Whether the charging happens and at what power level is managed by the battery and charger modeling. Additionally, not all EV owners are modeled as charging exclusively at home. The 2020 RECS included a question about what fraction of charging is done at home, with the options being 100%, 80% to 99%, 60% to 79%, etc., which was translated to a ResStock input file and sampled to assign the 'Home Charging Fraction' input. This input scales the average energy consumption rate of the battery model, ensuring that the resulting charging energy reflects only the portion of charging that occurs at home. The charging power remains fixed regardless of the at-home charging fraction, and only the vehicle discharge power is affected. The underlying assumption in using this approach is that 2020 RECS respondents implement the fraction of their charging in terms of energy, as opposed to time.

EV charging and discharging energy are modeled within OpenStudio-HPXML, which leverages a lithium-ion battery model in EnergyPlus. The 'Electric Vehicle Charger' input directly influences charging power draw, which is assigned a constant value dependent on the charger level. The L2 charger type in this measure has a power draw of 5.69 kilowatts, which reflects the average EV charging power output of a 240-volt outlet. The other ResStock inputs in Table 2 influence the average EV energy consumption rate as well as the duration of driving events, which will reflect in the duration and energy demand of charging events. The 'Home Charging Fraction' input adjusts for the actual charging energy provided by the at-home charger, however, we do not directly alter the number charging events but instead scale the average EV energy consumption rate.

The average EV energy consumption rate can be considered the average driving power output under design conditions. For each household, this output is calculated by:

1. Determining the expected annual discharge energy (kilowatt-hours) by multiplying the EV energy efficiency (kilowatt-hours per mile) by the annual miles (miles)
2. Calculating the average EV energy consumption rate by dividing the annual discharge energy (kilowatt-hours) by the number of hours driven per year.

Although we do not include all time-dependent variables impacting discharge events, we do incorporate a model that adjusts the average EV energy consumption based on the dwelling unit site ambient temperature at each time step. Ambient temperature plays a large role in EV energy usage, attributed primarily to the climate control systems of the vehicle cabin and battery. We deploy a curve of the discharge power versus ambient temperature 6 in the EnergyPlus Energy Management System 7 to capture the higher energy demand associated with cold-weather operation relative to mild or hot weather.

The 'Efficient EV Adoption With L2 Charging and DF' measure assigns the 'Electric Vehicle Ownership' parameter to 'Yes', the 'Electric Vehicle Charger' as 'Level 2', and the 'Electric Vehicle Efficiency Increase' to '15%.' The 'Electric Vehicle Efficiency Increase' parameter is not assigned during the baseline characterization but is instead used in measures to scale the ev\_energy\_efficiency argument. This measure divides the previously assigned efficiency by 1.15 to reflect a 15% efficiency improvement. All remaining EV-related inputs are assigned during the baseline sampling routine as described in this section and as further detailed in Speake et al. (2025). This approach preserves both the diversity and the expected dependencies that influence vehicle type and operation. The other parameters, which are also set by this measure in relation to the DF, will be discussed in subsequent sections.

## Electric Vehicle Modeling Limitations

The modeled EV charging energy may not always align with the driving demand, as it is constrained by the charging schedule and charger type. Different combinations of EV-related inputs can thus lead to a discrepancy between charging demand and charging availability. This approach can result in reduced charging energy, which is quantified with a custom output variable 'unmet driving hours', in which the EV is scheduled to be in use but does not have the required SOC to complete its driving hours. Unmet driving hours are seen more often in dwelling units with L1 chargers that lack the higher power output of L2 chargers, and charge mostly at home. Section 5 includes figures demonstrating the prominence of unmet driving hours in this measure. Unmet driving hours can be an important metric when designing vehicle and charger adoption scenarios. Input combinations with a higher number of unmet driving hours may be less realistic and, as a result, may be given less weight in scenario design.

We assume a direct relationship between home occupancy and driving/charging events, which results in similar daily schedules throughout the year, with distinct patterns for weekdays and weekends. Although this simplifies modeling, it may underpredict or overpredict individual charging or discharging events, as real-world behavior is more variable. Similarly, we apply the 'fraction charged at home' input directly to the discharging power, reducing charging loads evenly across the year. Although this might be realistic for some drivers who split their charging between home and other locations, others may instead scale their number of at-home charging events, where only certain days are used to charge at home. In this case, our approach captures the annual demand but may underestimate the magnitude or duration of individual charging events. This simplification may also lead to underestimation or overestimation of unmet driving hours, which in practice depend on alignment between charging locations and at-home hours.

## Demand Flexibility Approach

The 'Efficient EV Adoption With L2 Charging and DF' measure references peak period definition files, detailed in the following subsection, and sets the charging schedule to zero during peak hours as shown in Figure 5. Although this behavior may resemble a load-shedding event, where energy consumption is curtailed without a compensatory increase in other periods, it works more like a shift event because the battery model compensates, within its limits, by extending the charging duration after the peak period. EVs can still be fully charged before the next driving event in most cases when charging overnight. This distinction arises because the charging availability schedule solely dictates when the vehicle is plugged in, whereas actual energy consumption is governed by the battery model. This situation is illustrated in Figure 5 for a single building test run. During the peak period, the charging availability schedule is set to zero. This setting results in charging being suspended during the peak period, after which the vehicle completes charging. In we can see that the charging energy consumption in this measure (middle graph) is same as that in an equivalent measure without DF (top graph) - only split into two sessions by the peak period. Because this measure includes the adoption of L2 charging, EVs are likely to fully charge before their next driving event, even when the charging start time is shifted.

Depending on the timing of the peak period in relation to the vehicle charging/discharging schedule, there may not always be sufficient time to charge the vehicle to completion after the peak period-the occupant might leave before the charging is completed. The charging is interrupted around 18:00 due to the peak period, but by the time the peak period ends, the occupant is no longer at home, and the EV can no longer be charged. To apply the DF schedule to existing charging schedules, the 'Demand Flexibility Electric Vehicle' parameter is set to 'Enabled' in ResStock. As shown in Table 3, setting that parameter to 'Enabled' will set the ev\_flex\_enabled argument to 'True' and the flex\_random\_shift\_minutes argument to 30 in ResStockArgumentsPostHPXML, where the EV DF module is called. The flex\_random\_shift\_minutes argument is used to avoid coincident peak by offsetting the peak period randomly by up to ±30 minutes for each building. This offset will make some buildings start their peak period up to 30 minutes early or up to 30 minutes late (i.e., the shift can be any of the following: +30 min, +15 min, 0 min, -15 min, -30 min). This random shifting is assigned on a building-by-building basis and not on a per-event basis. In a real deployment, this staggering of individual homes would be accomplished through a combination of intentional control and inherent time delays and equipment states found across the portfolio at any point in time. Although this measure may create new concurrent peaks, this feature limits the unrealistic scenario in which all EV charging loads start simultaneously. A similar approach is applied in the ResStock thermostat DF measure.

## Peak Period Definition

When analyzing the impact of load flexibility, it is necessary to define the target time periods when DF will be dispatched to reduce the load. These time periods are commonly referred to as on-peak hours. On-peak hours can be identified in different ways depending on the goal for example:

- Reducing System Peak Power: Grid-wide net load data can be analyzed to identify hours with the highest demand, and DF can be dispatched to help lower the overall system peak power requirements.
- Responding to time-of-use (TOU) Pricing: Utility rates for individual customers can be analyzed to identify times with the highest electricity prices. This approach is applicable only for customers with TOU rates. DF can be dispatched to reduce individual customer utility bills.
- Minimizing Grid Operation Costs: Grid-wide operational cost data can be analyzed to identify hours with the highest operational costs, and DF can be dispatched to help lower the overall system operation costs.

For this measure, the goal is to quantify the load flexibility's technical potential to reduce system peak power in 2050. This project uses 2050 net load data from Cambium 2024's Mid-case scenario (Gagnon et al. 2025) to identify the peak hours for each state, categorized by summer months (June to September), winter months (December to March), and shoulder seasons (April to May and October to November). The Cambium data are taken from a publicly available dataset that provides future load predictions and other related metrics. The mid-case scenario within Cambium 2024 adopts moderate estimates for key inputs, including technology costs, fuel prices, and demand growth. The identified peak hours are then applied consistently as the dispatch time for each day within the corresponding months for each state. The on-peak period timing by state is shown in Table 4.

## Demand Flexibility Modeling Limitations

In this measure, we are assuming that all chargers participate in the DF program. Although this is unrealistic, the goal of this measure is to produce a dataset that can be mixed with the baseline and other measures in the desired proportion to study the impact of desired DF penetrations. Similarly, we assume that the chargers respond to DF every day, which may not be realistic but allows studying the impact of DF on any day of the year. Because DF is applied independently each day, we do not capture multi-day charging strategies where vehicles shift surplus or deficit energy across multiple days.

Our approach to modeling DF can result in a steep ramp-up and ramp-down of residential sector electricity demand. Although the measure partially alleviates this by randomly shifting the peak periods by ±30 minutes, high adoption of this measure may pose challenges to the grid. This measure implements DF by completely turning off charging during the peak period. Chargers may be able to reduce charging power during peak periods, increase charging power in anticipation of an upcoming peak period, or provide other more complex load management not modeled in this measure. This measure assumes that the peak periods are based on the system peak period of the 2050 net load data from Cambium's Mid-case scenario, which may not be how each utility would execute a DF program. When there is misalignment of the ResStock peak and the Cambium system peak, the rebound due to DF can lead to an even higher sector peak than that of the baseline.

This measure also assumes that the occupant behavior remains unaffected by the DF. For example, even when vehicles are insufficiently charged due to responding to DF, the occupant neither overrides the charging nor alters their driving pattern (for example, waiting until the vehicle is sufficiently charged). This assumption is primarily a limitation of the modeling implementation, where driving and charging behaviors are not altered during the simulation. We do not include any scenarios in which L1 chargers are subject to DF. Although EVs with L1 charging can be operated to avoid peak periods, we assign L2 chargers to all homes prior to shifting the schedule. This action reduces the risk of incurring unmet driving hours, and L2 chargers are more likely to have managed charging features that enable occupants to more easily participate in DF programs.

Figure 11 shows the daily average electricity demand in Colorado during summer and winter seasons under three scenarios: the baseline, EV adoption with L2 charging, and efficient EV adoption with L2 charging and DF (this measure). The shaded red regions denote the peak demand hours.

The DF measure successfully reduces demand during the peak period in both seasons compared to the L2-only scenario. However, this reduction leads to a rebound in demand immediately following the peak window, and a change in peak timing. The new peak timing in the DF scenario is closely tied to the peak window, which reflects both residential and commercial sector peaks and may not align with the baseline peak for residential loads. As a result, the DF scenario does not always shift the modeled peak later in the day. In the winter, the peak window aligns with the baseline residential peak, so the shifted load creates a new, later peak time due to the rebound. In summer, the baseline peak occurs before the peak window begins, so the demand shift does not change the peak timing. However, demand shifting still reduces peak magnitude compared to the L2-only scenario.

While the total daily electricity consumption with and without DF will remain the same, the DF measure shifts load away from high-demand hours in both seasons. Further, the introduction of more efficient EVs reduces the overall charging energy demand, but does not influence the peak, as the charging power remains constant in each scenario. This strategy helps mitigate peak demand but also illustrates the potential for rebound effects and new peaks, which can inform design and evaluation of EV demand flexibility strategies.

## 6  Discussion

As shown in Section 5.3, analyzing results in isolation can suggest unfavorable impacts to peak demand.  This  occurs  because  the  measure  assumes  that  100%  of  the  units  adopt  EVs  and participate in the DF shift, which is not realistic. We model this measure in this way to maintain flexibility in the scenario analysis. However, users should blend results from this measure with the baseline or other measures in the desired proportion. For example, if we assume that only 25% of the dwelling units adopt efficient EVs and participate in the DF schedule but the remaining stock adopts a typical EV with an L2 charger, we can analyze a custom scenario. The weighted average of  this  scenario  is  shown  in  Figure  12,  where  we  combine the results of this measure with an equivalent measure without efficient EVs and DF in a 1:3 proportion to simulate a DF penetration level of 25%. We can see that when the DF penetration is reduced, the rebound peak is substantially reduced as well.

Another point to keep in mind when using the results of this measure is that the peak definitions are based on the 2050 Mid-case Cambium 2024 net load data, but we are applying these to the current residential sector building stock. This approach is meant to provide data for a DF potential in a future scenario and, as such, should be mixed with the baseline in the desired proportion to model that future scenario.

## 7  Future Work

Several opportunities for improving this measure are listed here. Their implementation may depend on future advancements in technology and demand-side programs, such as more sophisticated EV smart charging technology or a grid-managed EV demand response.

- To develop a more complex DF strategy in which charging start times are randomized between the end of the on-peak period and the latest time required for a full charge, reducing the rebound peak seen with high DF penetrations.
- To develop rate-responsive charging flexibility based on TOU or dynamic pricing.
- To build an opt-out feature where chargers would ignore the peak period if the vehicle SOC was too low.
- To reduce the charging power during the peak period instead of completely suspending the charging.
