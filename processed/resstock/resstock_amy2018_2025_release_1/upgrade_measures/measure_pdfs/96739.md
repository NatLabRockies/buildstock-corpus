## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit end-use load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in Reyna (2025) [1]. Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project [2].

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: attic floor insulation for unfinished attics.

This upgrade demonstrated 2.2% total site energy savings (76 TWh) for the U.S. residential building stock (Figure 8). These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 3.7% stock natural gas heating savings (42 TWh)
- 4.0% stock electricity heating savings (10 TWh)
- 2.4% stock electricity cooling savings (9.4 TWh).

## 1  Technology Summary

Attic floor insulation for unfinished attics is designed to reduce heat transfer between the unconditioned attic space and the conditioned living areas below. This thermal separation helps improve energy efficiency, lower heating and cooling costs, enhance indoor comfort, and manage moisture within the home. This section provides an overview of the insulation materials commonly used for attic floors and outlines the recommended R-values based on U.S. climate zones, as established by the International Energy Conservation Code (IECC). These specifications inform the insulation levels included in this measure upgrade.

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

Attic floor insulation for unfinished attics involves placing insulation material between or over the attic floor joists to improve the thermal boundary between the living spaces below and the unconditioned attic spaces above. The primary intention behind attic insulation is to reduce heat transfer to improve energy efficiency, lower heating and cooling costs, increase thermal comfort, and managing moisture within the home [3], [4], [5]. Common attic floor insulation methods for unfinished attics include batt and roll insulation, loose-fill and blown-in insulation, and sprayed foam and foamed-in-place [5]. Batt and roll insulation, typically made from fiberglass, mineral wool, or cotton, is straightforward to install between attic floor joists but must be fitted carefully to avoid gaps or compression, which can compromise performance. Loose-fill (blown-in) insulation, typically made from cellulose, fiberglass, and mineral wool, is typically blown into place using special equipment. Sprayed foam and foamed-in-place, typically made with cementitious, phenolic, polyisocyanurate, and polyurethane materials, is applied using either low-pressure kits (commonly used for small-scale applications) or high-pressure sprayers for larger coverage areas. Both types are pressurized and expand on contact to fill gaps and irregular spaces. All of these methods are effective ways to add insulation in existing unfinished areas with irregular shape; see Table 1 for more details [3], [5].

Note that prior to insulating, attic air sealing is recommended for maximizing insulation effectiveness and reducing air-transported moisture from the conditioned space into the attic. Air sealing reduces air infiltration to help ensure that insulation performs optimally without compromising thermal resistance due to air leakage [3]. Refer to the air sealing documentation for more details [6]

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

## 1.2.1 R-Value

To measure and evaluate the effectiveness of attic floor insulation for unfinished attics, the primary consideration is thermal resistance, which is measured by R-value (ft²·°F·hr/Btu). The R-value quantifies the insulation's resistance to heat flow; higher values indicating greater insulating effectiveness. R-values are standardized and widely recognized by energy codes to ensure consistency and comparability, such as the International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) [7], [8]. For attic floor insulation specifically, recommended R-values vary by climate zone, ranging from R-30 in warmer climates to R-60 or higher in colder regions [8]. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) provides specific recommendations for attic insulation R-values based on climate zones; see details in Table 2. As shown in Table 2, units with uninsulated attics are recommended to install more insulation to achieve higher final R-values. In contrast, homes with 3-4 inches of existing insulation (approximately R-11 to R-15) show the amount of additional insulation that should be added, not the final total R-value. Table 3 summarizes achieved R-values from different empirical studies. These tables serve as reference points for retrofit applications.

## 2  Applicability

The attic floor insulation upgrade logic is based on a threshold approach, where homes are eligible for retrofit if their existing attic insulation R-value is below the IECC 2021-recommended level for their respective climate zone (Table 2). However, a minimum threshold is set such that the existing R-value must be at least R-10 values lower than the maximum recommended level to qualify for the upgrade. This constraint is applied to avoid inefficient retrofits with marginal benefit, since adding insulation to already well-insulated attics provides diminishing returns in energy savings and may not be cost-effective. Target R-values are assigned by climate zone using DOE-recommended R-values in Table 2 as guidance. Table 3 shows R-values that are typically achieved through retrofits from previous empirical studies. The following logics are applied to existing units:

1. Units have existing attics (automatically exclude multifamily units and mobile homes).
2. Existing attics have an R-value at least R-10 lower than targeted attic insulation level specified for their climate zone in Table 2.
3. Use the upper bound of recommended attic insulation by climate zone in Table 2 to simulate the highest energy savings scenario.

Note that in Table 2 and Table 3, the highest bound of R-value range is used in the simulations to represent the most energy-efficient scenarios. Previous studies are referenced to demonstrate that these R-values are reasonable as an upper bound. But, in some cases, our simulations test higher R-values than are typically installed. To determine eligibility for an upgrade, the existing attic must be at least 10 ft²·°F·hr/Btu (R-value) below the target for its climate zone. For example, a unit with existing R-29 won't get upgraded to R-30 because the marginal benefit of adding 1 R-value is negligible. The R-10  below target rule ensures that upgrades are applied only where they provide meaningful savings. Also, by including only dwellings with attics, this will automatically only include single-family units because ResStock only models attics for single-family units, whereas multifamily models do not include attics. ## 3  Modeling Approach

This section outlines how the attic floor insulation upgrade is modeled in ResStock using the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow. ResStock assigns each dwelling unit a set of building characteristics. This includes attic type, insulation level, and climate zone by using probabilistic sampling logic informed by national housing stock data. Attic floor insulation is modeled as R-value for the ceiling assembly. These values reflect insulation material installed between and/or over the attic floor joists in unfinished, vented attic spaces. This modeling assumes that insulation is installed uniformly across the attic floor and that the attic remains unconditioned and vented. Attic insulation performance is influenced by the insulation material and its installed thickness, both of which determine the R-value. However, ResStock does not explicitly model material types; it focuses instead on the total thermal resistance for the entire assembly.

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025 to assess whether the existing panel has enough capacity or breaker space to accommodate new loads from an energy retrofit. For details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide [1]. Attic floor insulation does not introduce new electrical loads or circuits, so they have no impact on the electrical panel. However, electrical panel metrics are included as a standard output for this and all other envelope measures. Any panel constraints appearing in the upgrade results reflect pre-existing conditions, as this measure does not worsen them.

## 3.1  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

The ResStock building stock energy model contains conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that are representative of the contiguous U.S. residential building stock. These are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each sample has an assigned option for each characteristic for each dwelling unit to be simulated. ResStock utilizes the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio ® and EnergyPlus ®  through the use of an HPXML file. For reference, the state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository when the ResStock 2024 Release 2 dataset was created can be found here. Assumptions about the attic floor insulation upgrades that are not explicitly defined in ResStock are taken care of in the BuildResidentialHPXML measure.

## Default Inputs From OpenStudio-HPXML for Ceiling Insulation Upgrade

The ResStock inputs in the table above are sent to OS-HPXML. Any values that are not specified by ResStock are calculated using the defaults in the BuildResidentialHPXML OpenStudio measure. In this case, rather than using the nominal ceiling insulation R-value, OS-HPXML uses the effective R-value when constructing the ceiling assembly. Effective R-value represents the overall thermal resistance of the entire ceiling assembly, not just the insulation material. It accounts for additional layers such as drywall, air films, structural framing, and other materials that contribute to or detract from heat flow resistance. As a result, the effective R-value is typically higher than the insulation-only R-value.

## 5  Discussion and Conclusion

This analysis shows that attic floor insulation upgrades yield small energy savings across climate zones. Among the 235,950 applicable units (42.9%), total energy consumption decreased by 2.2%, with savings realized in heating and cooling end uses. As expected, greater absolute and percent savings were observed in older homes, colder climates, and units with lower baseline insulation levels. In many homes, particularly those with baseline insulation near R-30 or higher, the incremental improvement to R-49 or R-60 results in smaller incremental energy savings. As insulation levels increase, each additional increment of R-value produces smaller marginal reductions in heat flow. Therefore, from an energy-savings perspective, attic insulation alone may not always produce strong return on investment (ROI), especially in newer homes or milder climates.

That said, focusing only on annual energy savings may undervalue the measure. Attic insulation improves indoor temperature stability, reduces peak load intensity, and enhances occupant comfort by limiting radiant heat transfer and surface temperature asymmetry. In colder climates, increased insulation can reduce risks associated with condensation and moisture-related durability issues. In hot climates, it reduces attic-to-living-space heat gain during peak afternoon hours. These comfort and resilience benefits are not captured in site energy metrics but are often drivers of homeowner decision-making. Another important consideration is measure interaction. The attic floor insulation may be more impactful when implemented as part of a broader envelope package, such as combining insulation with air sealing, duct sealing, or HVAC upgrades.
