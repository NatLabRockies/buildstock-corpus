## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in site energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson et al. 2022).

This document focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: Residential Two-Stage Geothermal Heat Pump (4.0 COP, 20.5 EER). This document builds on details established in the single-stage document (Maguire et al. 2025) to detail differences in the approach to modeling this higher efficiency and more commonly deployed, type of geothermal heat pump. Specific EnergyPlus ® objects and product specific curves used are highlighted along with showing the results of this measure compared to the baseline and single-speed geothermal heat pumps. Twospeed heat pumps are able to save even more site energy and on utility bills than single-speed products, albeit at the expense of a higher first cost.

## 1  Technology Summary

## 1.1  Introduction to Geothermal Heat Pumps

Geothermal heat pumps (GHPs) are highly efficient systems that provide space heating and cooling by using the relatively stable temperatures found below the Earth's surface. GHPs exchange heat with the ground, where temperatures remain more constant year-round. This enables GHPs to operate efficiently and reliably in a wide range of climates. GHP systems typically consist of three main components: a ground heat exchanger, a heat pump unit, and an air distribution system within the building.

There are several types of GHPs, including single-stage, two-stage, and variable-speed systems.

- Single-stage GHPs operate at full capacity whenever they run, which can lead to energy inefficiencies under part-load conditions (when the system needs less than its maximum output).
- Two-stage GHPs improve upon this design by adding a second, lower-capacity stage. This allows the system to operate more efficiently during mild conditions while still having the capability to switch to full output when demand peaks.
- Variable-speed GHPs represent the most advanced option, using inverter-driven compressors and fans to continuously adjust their output in response to real-time heating and cooling needs. Unlike single-stage or two-stage systems that operate in discrete steps, variable-speed systems can modulate across a wide range of capacities. This precise control enhances energy efficiency, provides more stable indoor temperatures, reduces humidity swings, and minimizes noise from cycling.

Like single-stage systems, two-stage GHPs draw on the ground's thermal properties to deliver heating and cooling via a ground heat exchanger and an indoor heat pump. The difference lies in the system's ability to adjust its output. By operating in a low stage under mild conditions, a twostage GHP reduces energy use, improves comfort by maintaining more stable temperatures and humidity, and can result in quieter operation. When heating or cooling demand increases (such as during extreme weather) the system shifts to high stage for full-capacity operation.

Although two-stage GHPs typically come with higher initial capital costs than single-stage models, they are usually more energy efficient and can lead to lower lifetime operating costs (Trane 2023). Due to these advantages, two-stage systems have become the most commonly installed type of GHP in the residential market, capturing the largest share among GHP technologies according to the technical advisory group (U.S. EPA, 2025).

The intent of this GHP measure is to reflect the characteristics of a typical water-to-air, two-stage GHP selected from the 2025 ENERGY STAR ®  Most Efficient GHP list (U.S. EPA, 2025). The modeled system assumes a vertical ground heat exchanger configuration, which is a common and space-efficient option for residential installations.

## 2  Modeling Approach

## 2.1  Applicability

Using the same applicability criteria as the single-stage GHP, the two-stage compressor, waterto-air heat pump with a vertical ground heat exchanger upgrade measure applies to all dwellings with:

- Non-shared (in-unit) HVAC and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane
- Shared (across multiple units) HVAC and a building height of &lt;8 stories and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane
- Dwelling units with ducts
- Dwelling units with soil thermal conductivity greater than 0.8 Btu/hr-ft-°F.

With these applicability constraints, the measure upgrade applies to roughly 71% of the existing residential building stock. 6.4% of the residential units that receive the GHP upgrade lack existing space cooling systems in the baseline. Units that are excluded from this measure, as well as more details about GHP modeling and assumptions, are more fully described in the documentation for the single-stage GHP measure (Maguire et al. 2025).

Applicability of this measure upgrade is based on ResStock housing characteristics that determine the available options and the options' details (Reyna, et al., 2025). Specifically, the 'HVAC Heating Efficiency' characteristic in ResStock is used to apply each respective heat pump option. 'HVAC Has Ducts' describes the presence of ducts in the dwelling. 'Heating Type' describes the baseline heating fuel of the dwelling. 'HVAC Shared Efficiencies' describes the shared HVAC system of the dwelling, if applicable. 'Geometry Building Type Height' describes the height and building type of the dwelling. More information on the ResStock housing characteristics can be found in the ResStock Technical Reference Guide. 1

## 2.2.1  Two-Stage GHP Heating and Cooling Efficiency

This measure includes a water-to-air GHP with a two-stage compressor rated at a heating coefficient of performance (COP) of 4.0 and a rated cooling energy efficiency ratio (EER) of 20.5. The ground heat exchanger shape and properties are the same as used for the single-stage GHP: rectangular and vertically drilled single U-tube design with thermally enhanced grout and piping. This section will describe the inputs and modeling methodology for the heat pump. Refer to the single-stage GHP model documentation (Maguire et al. 2025) for inputs and methodology not discussed in this document.

The ResStock arguments assigned in the 'HVAC Heating Efficiency' characteristic (different than the default OpenStudio-HPXML defaults) that help distinguish the two-stage GHP from the single-stage GHP are:

- Heat pump compressor type
- Heat pump heating efficiency
- Heat pump cooling efficiency.

For this measure, the compressor type is two-stage, as stated by the measure definition.

## 2.2.2  Two-Stage GHP Performance Curves

GHPs are highly efficient systems that use the relatively constant temperature of the Earth to provide heating and cooling for buildings. Accurately modeling the performance of GHPs in building energy simulation tools like EnergyPlus ® is critical for evaluating system performance, energy consumption, and cost-effectiveness under different operating conditions.

Unlike single-stage equipment, two-stage and variable-speed GHPs can operate at multiple capacity levels, allowing them to better match building loads and improve part-load efficiency. To capture this behavior in EnergyPlus, the

Coil:Cooling:WaterToAirHeatPump:VariableSpeedEquationFit and

Coil:Heating:WaterToAirHeatPump:VariableSpeedEquationFit objects are used. These components rely on a suite of performance curves to simulate how the system responds to changes in water and air temperatures, as well as airflow and water flow rates. Despite 'VariableSpeed' in the name, this object is the best EnergyPlus model for two-stage equipment as well as fully variable-speed.

Each curve provides a mathematical relationship between a key input (e.g., entering water temperature, airflow fraction) and the resulting system output, such as capacity or efficiency. These relationships are derived from manufacturer data, laboratory testing, or calibrated models and are essential for producing accurate, realistic simulations of two-stage GHP operation.

To fully represent system performance across both part-load and full-load conditions for heating and cooling, the following sets of performance curves are required:

- The efficiency of the GHP as a function of water and air temperature (biquadratic)
- The capacity of the GHP as a function of water and air temperature (biquadratic)
- The efficiency of the GHP as a function of airflow fraction (quadratic or cubic)
- The capacity of the GHP as a function of airflow fraction (quadratic or cubic)
- The efficiency of the GHP as a function of water flow fraction (quadratic or cubic)
- The capacity of the GHP as a function of water flow fraction (quadratic or cubic).

The EnergyPlus Auxiliary Program: HVAC Performance Curve Fit Tool was used to develop the biquadratic curves for capacity and efficiency as function of temperatures (Big Ladder 2024). The tool is designed for direct expansion coil performance curves, which depend on two air temperatures, but appropriate biquadratic curves can be generated using the same tool if water temperature is used instead of one air temperature. The approach used is consistent for all GHP measures in ResStock, with additional curves being developed for each stage for coils that can operate at multiple stages.

The two-stage GHP performance curves were developed based on data provided in the spec sheet for a 3-ton Tranquility 30 SE two-stage GHP manufactured by ClimateMaster (ClimateMaster 2025). This model is in the same product line as what was used for the single-stage GHP performance curves. The same overall approach described in the performance curve section of the single-stage GHP documentation for deriving performance curves is used for the two-stage GHP system, with some exceptions noted here. The two-stage GHP spec sheets provide data for performance at both part-load and full-load conditions, which means that two separate performance maps are required for the lower and higher stages. Nominal points are at the same temperatures as single-stage equipment (70°F/80°F entering water/air temperature for cooling and 70°F/70°F entering water/air temperature for heating), with the highest airflow and water flow rate (1,200 cfm and 9 gal/min, respectively) provided used for both part-load and full-load conditions as the nominal condition. Off-rated air and water flow rates are accounted for using the curves shown later in this report.

After generating coefficients with the spreadsheet tool from EnergyPlus, the original manufacturer catalog data were compared with the curve-fit data to ensure the correctness of the curve coefficients. These comparisons are documented in Appendix A.

Quadratic curves for the impact of airflow and water flow on efficiency and capacity can be generated by fitting directly to published data of a product at nominal conditions. As these curves are simpler and made up of fewer points, a simple quadratic regression can yield a good fit for each curve. While two-stage GHP systems typically operate at the nominal flow rate for each stage when in low- and high-stage operation, flow rates may be reduced due to equipment faults and degradation, which are modeled within OpenStudio-HPXML, and these curves can capture the impact of faults on the annual performance of affected systems.

Note that a part-load fraction curve is also required for two-stage equipment to capture the performance of this equipment during very low-load situations where the required heating or cooling is less than what is provided at the lower stage. The part-load curve used for the twostage GHP is identical to the curve used for the single-stage GHP and is therefore not shown here. Refer to the single-stage GHP documentation (Maguire et al. 2025) for the part-load curve and details of its derivation.

## 2.2.3  EnergyPlus Energy Management System Correction to Pump Power

The EnergyPlus models with the variable-speed water-to-air coil object for two-stage and variable-speed water-to-air GHP systems did not by default model the expected behavior of the pump for these GHP systems. To modify the default behavior to try to more accurately model the pump behavior, especially in cases where the GHP is in part-load conditions, a custom energy management system (EMS) script was developed and added to the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow. EMS is a custom scripting language packaged with EnergyPlus that allows users to write short custom programs to modify the behavior of certain components of the model. In addition to developing the standard EnergyPlus GHP models, an EMS program was developed to overcome the limitations in current EnergyPlus capabilities to model the two-stage water-to-air GHP systems:

1. There is an improper limit on the node water flow rate found in EnergyPlus. This limit will cause the pump water flow rates to be capped at the first-stage nominal flow rate, and the remaining water flow is bypassed. It will likely cause warnings/errors complaining about unrealistic water temperatures due to the reduced water flow rates, which largely impacts the accuracy and stability of simulation. This limitation was fixed by the ResStock team and will be included in future EnergyPlus releases from 25.1.0 onward. See the merged EnergyPlus pull request 3 for the full details of this change.
2. EnergyPlus models a constant water mass flow rate equal to the first-stage flow rate during part-load conditions, where the heat pump may only be running for part of the time step, and the average flow rate over the time step may be less than the minimum even with a two-stage pump. This affects the pump energy calculation, which is fixed by the EMS program described below. In addition, an EnergyPlus issue 4 was created to

[3  https://github.com/NatLabRockies/EnergyPlus/pull/11005](https://github.com/NatLabRockies/EnergyPlus/pull/11005)

[4  https://github.com/NatLabRockies/EnergyPlus/issues/10936](https://github.com/NatLabRockies/EnergyPlus/issues/10936)

report this defect to the development team. Ideally the defect will be fixed in the future version of EnergyPlus, and then the EMS workaround can be eliminated or simplified.

In this case, an EMS program is used to recalculate the pump power in every time step. It involves two steps:

1. Calculate the correct pump power if part-load conditions exist. The internal EnergyPlus pump energy calculations do not handle the pump part-load ratio correctly in the use case. The pump part-load ratio at each time step is recalculated in the EMS program based on pump volume flow rates and heat pump coil speed levels/ratios. The part-load ratio is assumed to be equal to the fraction of pump full-load power.
2. Apply the calculated fraction to calculate and override the pump power consumption at each time step.

Even though EMS programs offer flexibility for users to override some aspects of EnergyPlus simulation and achieve the desired energy results, there are still some limitations to using EMS programs. The EMS program described above is included in OpenStudio-HPXML, but other EnergyPlus modelers would not have easy access to it and may not know about the limitations requiring its use. EMS also adds complexity to the models, may impact simulation runtime, and may cause one-time-step lag on reporting the energy consumption of components controlled via EMS. Therefore, if the EnergyPlus issue being reported can be fixed in the future versions, it will largely simplify the workflow and benefit more users of these EnergyPlus objects.

Combining all the efforts above, OpenStudio-HPXML is capable of modeling the desired pump operation and giving satisfying pump energy results for two-stage GHP systems.

## 2.3  Limitations and Concerns

Some limitations unique to two-stage GHPs include:

1. The two-stage GHP uses EMS to account for energy consumption of the pump when modeled as operating below the lowest stage. A change to the EnergyPlus source code would be ideal and more widely accessible than the EMS-based approach used here, but source code changes can be complicated, time-consuming, and require expertise with the C++ based source code. Future runs will also use the bug fixed version of EnergyPlus, and the impact of the EMS workaround described here will need to be re-evaluated with the newer releases.
2. Desuperheater curves were not developed for two-stage equipment. Although data are available for desuperheater performance at both stages, adding a desuperheater to a GHP is relatively uncommon and assumed to not happen in ResStock when applying GHPs as an upgrade. This capability may be added to OpenStudio-HPXML in the future, which would make it available to be leveraged in ResStock for future runs.

Note that the limitations listed in the single-stage GHP measure documentation apply to all GHPs modeled in ResStock, particularly when it comes to modeling of the ground heat exchanger.
