## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, natural gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the highimpact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson, et al., 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: Natural Gas Water Heater upgrade. This measure installs a high-efficiency condensing natural gas tankless water heater to all dwelling units that are not currently using solar thermal, heat pump, or gas tankless water heaters and have access to natural gas from space heating, water heating, cooking, clothes drying, pool heating, or spa heating. With a more compact design and a higher efficiency than their conventional fuel storage water heater counterparts, natural gas tankless water heaters provide hot water on demand and are gaining popularity in the market. With 55.7% of the U.S. housing stock applicable for this upgrade, this measure demonstrated:

- A total modeled savings potential of 118 TWh per year in site energy and $10 billion per year in energy costs based on 2024 state-average fuel rates.
- An average modeled savings of 1,560 kWh per year (5.1%) in site energy and $136 per year (4.9%) in energy bills for applicable occupied dwellings.
- 60% of the site energy savings come from natural gas and 36% from electricity.
- An increase in site energy for 4.2% of applicable dwellings and an increase in energy bill for less than 0.1% of applicable dwellings.

## 1  Technology Summary

This measure models installing a high-efficiency natural gas tankless water heater into all U.S. homes that are not already using a heat pump, gas tankless, or solar thermal water heaters and have access to natural gas based on these major end uses: space heating, water heating, cooking, clothes drying, pool heating, or spa heating. The replacement technology is a high-efficiency natural gas tankless condensing water heater with a uniform energy factor (UEF) of 0.95 and a burner capacity of 199 kBtu/h, the highest allowed size within the residential application (US Code, 2026).

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

Nearly all U.S. homes use a water heater to supply hot water for sinks, baths/showers, and appliances, such as dishwashers and clothes washers. A water heater can be a stand-alone in-unit appliance or a centrally located system that serves multiple units in a building. Storage water heaters heat water in a tank to store for later use, while tankless water heaters heat water for immediate use. Water heaters can use electricity, natural gas, propane, fuel oil, wood, or other fuels to heat the water. Fuel-based water heaters are equipped with a flue vent to release exhaust gas, and this vent can be atmospheric or powered by a small blower fan.

Tankless water heaters are designed to produce hot water on demand. Instead of having a tank to store hot water, they are equipped with burners several times larger in capacity than tanked water heaters. Tankless gas water heaters are a fast-growing subsector within the gas water heater market (Granda, 2024) (Horizon Databook, 2025) because they provide 'endless' hot water, are more efficient, and are more compact than conventional gas storage water heaters (ReportLinker, 2023). The sales of gas water heaters in 2023 were estimated to be 4.2 million for water heaters with storage (81%) (AHRI, 2025) and 1 million for tankless (19%) (Wachunas, 2025). Although gas storage water heaters are still dominant based on total sales, the sales are slowly declining, with shares replaced by electric and gas tankless options, the latter of which is expected to grow by at least 10% per year (Wachunas, 2025). Mirroring this trend, there are more tankless options than storage on the gas water heater market. As shown in Table 1, the number of gas tankless water heater offerings from two major retailers and the ENERGY STAR ®  certified products list is consistently much higher than their conventional storage counterparts.

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

## 1.2.1 Efficiency

Gas tankless water heaters are generally more efficient than gas storage water heaters, resulting in lower energy consumption and energy utility bills. This is because they do not need to keep a large volume of water heated, which avoids heating cycles that other water heaters employ to reheat the tank. As a result, their overall energy loss is less, primarily due to reduced combustion efficiency losses and cycling. They are also able to supply more hot water in high-demand situations than tank water heaters because they are not limited by the storage tank volume. The UEF is an industry standard metric used to quantify the energy efficiency of residential water heaters, replacing the energy factor (EF) since 2017 (Jutras, 2024). All UEFs come with a usage bin or category and should only be compared with other UEFs of the same bin. A higher UEF indicates that the water heater is more efficient in converting energy into hot water within its specific usage bin.

Most gas storage water heaters have a UEF between 0.54 and 0.90, whereas tankless gas water heaters usually have a UEF between 0.81 and 0.98 according to AHRI's directory of certified product performance (2026). ENERGY STAR Version 5.0 sets the UEF requirement to be 0.95 for gas tankless water heaters and between 0.81 and 0.86 for gas storage water heaters, depending on the tank size and usage bin (ENERGY STAR, Water Heater Key Product Criteria, 2025). Water heaters that meet this UEF requirement are generally condensing (for both tank and tankless), meaning that more heat from the flue gas is absorbed by the water via condensation, which leads to cooler exhaust, thereby boosting efficiency. The best-in-class gas tankless water heaters have a UEF between 0.95 and 0.98 compared to between 0.88 and 0.90 for the best gas storage water heaters. Tankless water heaters are also much more compact, making them a popular choice, particularly for homes where space is a constraint or a premium, such as multifamily dwellings.

Tankless gas burners are typically 140-200 kBtu/h compared to 40-100 kBtu/h for 40- to 60-gal gas storage water heaters. Because of their larger burners, they require more fuel input to operate than storage water heaters. This could mean an expensive upgrade to the indoor gas distribution system that sometimes includes the service main. Tankless water heaters need a supply line of at least ¾-in. diameter. Longer piping runs and burners with higher heat rates require a larger pipe to maintain pressure, so upsizing the gas line depends on how close the water heater is to the main and what water heater it is replacing. These limitations could not be modeled or excluded from the measure due to a lack of data on gas distribution characteristics.

Tankless water heaters have a turndown ratio that describes how wide a range they can modulate their output. A higher turndown ratio allows the unit to modulate output more precisely, reducing cycling losses across a wider range of demand, especially during low-demand periods when cycling losses would otherwise be significant (Intellihot, 2024). ResStock cannot accurately model the turndown ratio of tankless water heaters at the current time resolution of 15 minutes and, as a result, may be overestimating their energy savings potential by underestimating their cycling losses.

## 1.2.2 Sizing

Tankless water heater sizing depends on the maximum hot water flow rate and temperature rise. Right-sizing ensures that the water heater is delivering the required hot water demand without increasing the equipment upfront cost or operational inefficiencies. Hot water demand is primarily driven by the number of occupants, but it can be influenced by many other factors, including the type of occupants and the number and type of hot water appliances and fixtures. Temperature rise refers to the difference between the hot water setpoint and the mains water temperature, which varies by climate zone and time of year. The hot water setpoint is typically 120 ° F in manufacturers' brochures (Rheem, 2026) whereas energy simulations, including ResStock, assume 125 ° F following RESNET standard (2016). Generally, the larger the household, and the colder the climate, the larger the tankless water heater heat rate (kBtu/h) will need to be. Table 2 summarizes the performance range for ENERGY STAR-rated gas tankless water heaters for the U.S. market, retrieved in May 2025 (ENERGY STAR, 2025). Most tankless gas water heaters on the market are at or near the sizing limit for residential applications: 180199 kBtu/h, likely reflecting consumer preference for higher-power tankless water heaters that could better guarantee that their hot water needs are always met.

For this measure, we replace any applicable housing segments with a gas tankless water heater rated at 199 kBtu/h, the maximum allowable for residential application, per market trends. As such, there is no distinction based on household size or climate region.

## 1.2.3 Impact of Flue Vent

All fuel-based water heaters need to pull in air for combustion and require a way to safely vent the exhaust. This additional vent can add to the overall infiltration of the house and needs to be balanced to prevent dangerous back-drafting of the flue gas (Moffatt, 1991). Lower-efficiency water heaters typically have a large vertical vent that is either atmospheric or powered with a blower for better flow control. Higher-efficiency water heaters use a smaller powered vent that expels the exhaust gasses outside at a lower temperature (State Water Heaters, 2026). Resizing the vent for the new water heater can add to the upfront cost of the measure, but it can improve the airtightness of the house, reducing space heating and cooling demand. ResStock captures this impact of flue vent on infiltration in its water heater modeling. It assumes that the water heater and the space heating system share a flue vent when both systems are in the conditioned space and require ventilation, as indicated by the parameter 'Has Flue or Chimney in Conditioned Space' in Table 5 in Section 3.1; therefore, the measure can remove the fuel water heater flue vent and reduce infiltration only when the flue vent is stand-alone and the existing space heating system does not require ventilation.

## 1.2.4 Electrical Requirement

Natural gas tankless water heaters consume a small amount of power for ignition and controls. For example, the power supply required for a line of Rheem high-efficiency condensing tankless water heaters is 2A at 120V (Rheem, n.d.). While a dedicated circuit is recommended (but not required) by the manufacturer for reliability and warranty protection, we assume the water heater is powered from an existing 120V outlet, with no other loads on the circuit if necessary as a dedicated outlet. Per the National Electrical Code (NEC) (NFPA, 2023), fuel-based, non-motor appliances, like gas water heaters, are generally excluded from electrical panel load calculations due to their low electric power draw, therefore, the water heater is not considered a new load on the panel. For fuel appliances with a motor (e.g., gas dryer) or dual-fuel HVAC systems, the electrical portion of the load would be included in such calculations.

## 2  Applicability

This measure installs a high-efficiency condensing natural gas tankless water heater to all homes that are not currently using a solar thermal, heat pump, or gas tankless water heater and have access to gas. This includes homes already using natural gas for water heating, space heating, cooking, clothes drying, pool heating, or hot tub/spa heating. Although ResStock models gas fireplaces, grills, and lighting, their presence is infrequent and random and thus not included in the gas access evaluation. Based on this assumption, 55.7% of the housing stock receives a highefficiency condensing natural gas tankless water heater upgrade, as shown in Table 3. Note that 'other fuel' and 'wood' water heaters are mapped to coal and wood cord energy consumption in the output, respectively. These fuel types are excluded from the published energy results due to low confidence in the estimates, meaning they appear in the housing characteristics but not in the reported energy consumption. As a result, homes with water heaters of these fuel types will appear to show an increase in site energy consumption that does not reflect real energy changes from the upgrade.

## 3  Modeling Approach

ResStock models one water heater for every home and all water heaters as a stand-alone appliance, with diversity in efficiency and installed location informed by Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) 2020 microdata (US EIA, 2024). Most water heaters are installed in the conditioned space or adjacent to the thermal envelope, with more than 40% in the living space and 11% in heated basements. ResStock does not model centralized water heaters in multifamily buildings, which exist in approximately 14% of the stock, according to the 'Water Heater in Unit' parameter in ResStock. 1 Instead, a portion of the central water heater's energy consumption is approximated for the modeled dwelling unit using a stand-alone water heater model located in a conditioned mechanical space outside the dwelling unit. The out-of-unit location prevents the water heater from interacting with any in-unit systems, but the model does not account for any realistic hot water distribution and recirculation losses, which can be significantly more, depending on the location of the dwelling. This means that the baseline water heating energy for multifamily units with out-of-unit central water heating is generally underestimated.

In ResStock, the tankless water heater model has no operational limits and will therefore always meet any level of instantaneous hot water demand, unlike real-world units, which are constrained by their burner capacity and turndown ratio. Hot water draw schedules are stochastically modeled (Hendron, Burch, &amp; Barker, 2010), and the total demand is tabulated from fixture (shower, sink, and bath) use and the associated distribution losses, which vary by the number of occupants, and from hot water appliances, i.e., dishwashers and clothes washers. Multipliers are additionally applied at random to each hot water demand to reflect usage diversity. An impact of this usage diversity is that a small family can use a lot of water when assigned large multipliers and vice versa, and unmet hot water load is possible. This means that the modeling approach could slightly underestimate energy consumption of any storage water heaters in the baseline; however, because these edge cases are rare, the impact is limited. Refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Documentation for more details on the modeling process of water heating (Reyna, et al., 2025).

## 3.1  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

The ResStock building stock energy model contains conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that are representative of the U.S. residential building stock. These are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each sample has an assigned option for each characteristic for each dwelling unit to be simulated. ResStock uses the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio ® and EnergyPlus ®  through an HPXML file. For reference, OpenStudio-HPXML v1.10.0 was used to produce the ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset.

## 4.1.1 Individual Building Annual

This section presents selected metrics before and after the gas tankless water heater upgrade and percent change. As expected, the total delivered hot water is higher in colder climates; this is because the mains water is colder, so more energy is needed to deliver the same amount of hot water. The amount of delivered hot water remains the same before and after the upgrade, indicating that both the baseline and the upgrade do not have any unmet water heating demand.

The upgrade led to a significant reduction in water heating energy use across all locations. Water heater energy consumption was reduced by 60%, 64%, and 38% for the dwellings in Hawai'i, California, and Alaska, respectively. The percent reduction is lower for the Alaska dwelling sample than Hawai'i and California due to differences in their hot water demand. For Hawai'i and California, hot water demand is low, at 21.3 and 16 gal/day, respectively, due to the low occupant count. As a result, the overall efficiency of the baseline storage water heaters is very low, and more than half of the savings (i.e., 30%+) comes from eliminating the tank loss of the storage water heaters. On the other hand, the Alaska sample has a hot water demand that is three to four times higher than the other samples, at 55.5 gal/day. Consequently, the energy savings are primarily driven by the efficiency gain for the burner (~30%) as the tank-loss-related savings (8%) are overshadowed in comparison.

The overall efficiency of the water heater is provided by the annual average efficiency, calculated as the ratio of the delivered hot water to the energy consumption for the hot water. As show in Table 7, the overall efficiency of the tankless water heater upgrade is 90% for all three samples, indicating that it does not vary by climate zone or hot water demand. This is because ResStock assumes a constant amount of cycling losses regardless of the amount and timing of hot water use. In practice, lower-use homes could have more cycling losses (Bohac, Schoenbauer, Hewett, Lobenstein, &amp; Butcher, 2010); thus, the performance and energy savings potential of the gas tankless water heater upgrade could be overestimated, particularly for lowdemand households.

## 4.1.2 Individual Building Time Series

Section 4.1.1 shows that for the dwelling samples, the high-efficiency natural gas tankless water heater upgrade primarily affects the water heater energy consumption, with no or small changes in other end uses. So, this section analyzes only the 15-minute time-series data of the water heater energy consumption for the three example units, as illustrated in Figure 2. The day Jan. 2, 2018, is selected as a representative day for this analysis.

In all three locations, upgrading to a high-efficiency natural gas tankless water heater reduces the hot water load profile compared to the baseline natural gas storage water heaters. Because the upgrade does not contain any tank loss, its profile represents the actual hot water draw pattern. On the other hand, the incumbent storage water heater periodically cycles on to offset the standby losses and to maintain the tank temperature, creating frequent and sharp peaks approximately 2 hours apart throughout the day. Actual gas storage water heaters might cycle on for a longer duration but less frequently due to a larger deadband on real products than what is assumed in the ResStock model, which still requires the same amount of gas annually to offset the standby losses. After the upgrade, energy use is substantially lower because there are no standby losses associated with keeping a tank of water hot. In Alaska, this day has much more hot water use than the other scenarios, and as a result, the baseline includes extended periods of high energy use, peaking at more than 7 kWh per 15 minutes between 8 a.m. and 12 p.m. The upgrade reduces the peak to 5.7 kWh per 15 minutes, representing a 20% reduction.

## 4.2.1  Annual Energy

By applying a high-efficiency tankless natural gas water heater to all homes eligible for such an upgrade-55.7% of the housing stock-the modeled overnight savings potential is estimated to be nearly 118 TWh in total site energy, with 60% of the savings from natural gas and 36% from electricity, as shown in Figure 3. Figure 4-Figure 8 present the measure impacts for the upgraded and occupied dwellings only, which represent 50% of the stock. Vacant units are excluded because they do not have occupant-driven consumption to illustrate realistic energy savings.

Figure 4 compares the modeled energy end use breakdown of the average home before and after the water heater upgrade. The modeled average energy savings is 1,560 kWh per year, or 5.1% of the total site energy consumption. The savings primarily come from hot water energy reduction, with the electricity, propane, and fuel oil used for hot water reduced to zero due to the fuel switching and the natural gas for hot water reduced by 42% due to replacing the existing gas water heaters with more efficient models. This means that the overall savings from switching all the natural gas storage water heaters (76%) in the housing stock to higher-efficiency condensing gas tankless water heaters more than offsets the natural gas increase from converting the rest of the water heaters (24%)-which are electric resistance, propane, and fuel oil water heaters-to natural gas. This highlights the energy efficiency potential for gas tankless water heater adoption at scale, although the technology does not lead to energy savings for all the dwelling units, as shown in the next few figures.

## 4.4  Electrical Panel Constraints

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025 to assess whether the existing panel has enough capacity or breaker space to accommodate new loads from an energy retrofit. For details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al., 2025).

Switching to a gas water heater generally does not lead to an electrical panel constraint because it does not add new electric load or circuit to the electrical panel. Gas water heaters can be plugged into an existing 120V circuit (which is assumed to be a dedicated outlet if one is required by local amendments to the NEC). They are not considered an electrical load when sizing the electrical panel. Instead, this measure can reduce the total connected load and the number of dedicated circuits when the incumbent water heater being switched out is electric, thus freeing up capacity and breaker space. Any panel constraints appearing in the upgrade results reflect preexisting conditions, as this measure does not worsen them. 2.3% of the existing panels are estimated to already exceed capacity based on code. Pre-existing capacity constraints are an artifact of the panel modeling process, in which panel service ratings are estimated from selected housing variables rather than detailed inventory data. While anecdotal evidence suggests these constraints occur in practice - manifesting as flickering lights or tripping breakers - the lack of empirical data prevents us from confirming their prevalence.
