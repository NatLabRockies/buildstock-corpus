## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover most high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. This results in a time-series profile broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model represents the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in (Reyna, et al., 2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are provided in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (NREL, 2025).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape package: advanced air sealing, attic floor insulation, and duct sealing upgrade.

The advanced air sealing, attic floor insulation, and duct sealing upgrade achieved 22.6% total site energy savings (780 TWh) for the U.S. residential building stock (Figure 5). These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 40% stock natural gas heating savings (458 TWh)
- 36% stock electricity heating savings (92 TWh)
- 21% stock electricity cooling savings (80 TWh).

## 1  Technology Summary

This upgrade package, which includes advanced air sealing, attic floor insulation, and duct sealing, combines three key areas of a retrofit measures. The goal of these upgrades is to reduce heat losses in winter and retain cool air in the conditioned space during summer. Air sealing alone does not eliminate the need for insulation (DOE, 2025). Insulation and air sealing work best together to improve comfort and energy efficiency. Therefore, they are often implemented as a package to address complementary aspects of a home's energy performance, and their combined effect is much greater than any single measure alone. Together, these upgrades help to lower energy bills, enhance indoor comfort and improve indoor air quality year round.

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

## Air Sealing

Air sealing involves identifying and closing large and small gaps, cracks, and penetrations in the home's primary or secondary pressure boundaries, such as around windows, doors, attic hatches, and plumbing or wiring holes (DOE, 2025). These leaks, whether large or small, accumulate and can allow a substantial amount of heated or cooled air escape. By reducing air leaks, air sealing improves indoor comfort, maintains more stable temperatures, and reduces the workload on the heating and cooling system. Air sealing is especially important in older homes, which typically have more leaks (Alliance, 2025). Refer to the air sealing documentation for more information on common leakage points, and best practices for sealing, and prescriptive air leakage rates based on climate zones (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a).

## Attic Floor Insulation

Insulating the attic floor reduces heat transfer between the living space and the unconditioned attic. In colder climates, this keeps heat inside during winter. In warmer climates, it helps keep the living space cooler. The effectiveness of insulation is measured by its R-value; a higher Rvalue indicates better resistance to heat flow. Installing insulation in existing homes typically involves blowing loose-fill insulation over the attic floor, especially in unfinished attics. The optimal insulation level depends on the climate zone and the home's existing insulation. Refer to the attic insulation documentation for guidance on recommended R-values and installation approaches (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025b).

## Duct Sealing and Insulation

Ducts distribute heated or cooled air throughout the house. In many homes, especially older ones, these ducts have leaks that allow conditioned air to escape into unconditioned or semiconditioned spaces like attics or crawlspaces. As a result, the system must work harder and use more energy to maintain the desired temperature. Duct sealing addresses these leaks using mastic, metal tape, or an aerosol-based sealant, depending on the situation. Sealing ducts improves system efficiency, maintains more consistent room temperatures, and enhances indoor air quality by preventing dirty attic or crawlspace air from entering the ducts. Duct sealing can also reduce unintentional pressure imbalances, which may cause indoor air quality or combustion safety concerns. See the duct sealing documentation for more information on methods and materials (Lou &amp; Liu, 2025).

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

To maximize energy efficiency and based on building science principles, the correct order for implementing this upgrade package is: (1) advanced air sealing, (2) duct sealing, and (3) attic floor insulation. Reducing air leakage is the foundation of energy efficiency. It helps control indoor temperatures and moisture and improves the effectiveness of insulation and HVAC systems. Sealing leaks before adding insulation ensures the insulation performs as intended. It also makes leaks easier to identify and reduces the burden on field staff, since they are not working around additional insulation. Once air sealing targets are met, it is essential to address the HVAC system by sealing any leaky ducts to prevent the loss of conditioned air, particularly in unconditioned spaces like attics or crawlspaces. Attic floor insulation should be installed last. While highly effective, it must follow air and duct sealing to avoid covering up critical areas that require sealing and to ensure ducts running through the attic are properly sealed and insulated beforehand.

Therefore, we assume that upgrades will be implemented in the following sequence: Advanced air sealing is applied first to applicable dwelling units, with the goal of achieving 5 ACH50. While some regions require tighter envelopes for new construction, 5 ACH50 reflects the average infiltration rate achieved in empirical studies across representative climate zones and ensures that selected homes do not already comply with 2024 IECC requirements (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a).

After air sealing, HVAC ducts in applicable homes are sealed and insulated to achieve the following:

- A maximum of 5% duct air leakage to the outside
- Duct insulation level of R-8.

Attic floor insulation is the upgraded in applicable dwelling units to the following levels:

- R-49 in climate zone 1
- R-60 in climate zones 2 through 8.

More details about the modeling assumptions for these three measures can be found in the accompanying documentation (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a) (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025b) (Lou &amp; Liu, 2025).

## 2  Applicability

Advanced air sealing upgrades are applied to dwelling units with infiltration rates greater than or equal to 6 ACH50.

Attic floor insulation upgrades are applied only to dwelling units with unfinished attics (vented and unvented attic types in ResStock). Specifically, upgrades are triggered when attic floor insulation is below the following levels: less than R-49 in climate zone 1 and less than R-60 in climate zones 2 through 8.

Duct sealing applies to dwelling units with duct leakage to the outside greater than 6% or duct insulation rated below R-6. Dwelling units with 0% leakage to the outside and uninsulated ducts are not applicable, as these configurations typically indicate that the ducts are located entirely within conditioned spaces.

Further details on the applicability criteria and stock saturation for each of these three measures can be found in the accompanying documentation (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a) (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025b) (Lou &amp; Liu, 2025). ## 3  Modeling Approach

The modeling approach of the three individual measures can be found in the accompanying documentation (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025a) (Phoung, Lou, &amp; White, 2025b) (Lou &amp; Liu, 2025).

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025 to assess whether the existing panel has enough capacity or breaker space to accommodate new loads from an energy retrofit. For details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al., 2025). This measures does not introduce new electrical loads or circuits, so it has no impact on the electrical panel. However, electrical panel metrics are included as a standard output for this and all other envelope measures. Any panel constraints appearing in the upgrade results reflect pre-existing conditions, as this measure does not worsen them.

## 4.1.1 Individual Building Annual Results

Figure 1 shows the annual energy consumption of the example dwelling unit under the full envelope package upgrade and under each of the three individual measures applied separately. Among these four scenarios, heating energy use shows the largest reduction, dropping from 105.5 MBtu in the baseline to 65.3 MBtu with the full upgrade, a 38% decrease. Cooling energy use also decreases slightly, while hot water energy use remains nearly unchanged. All other end uses are unaffected.

The envelope upgrade reduced heat loss from the dwelling, which led to a slight increase in hot water energy consumption. In this example, the water heater is in an unheated basement. By preventing heat loss during winter and retaining cool air during summer, the upgrade improves overall thermal efficiency. However, because the unit is heating-dominated, the reduction in heat loss during winter is more significant than the benefit of retaining cool air in summer. As a result, less ambient heat is released into the unheated basement, causing the water heater to work harder and use slightly more energy to maintain the desired water temperature.

The comprehensive envelope upgrade package reduces the dwelling unit's annual total energy use from 165 MBtu to 122 MBtu, a 26% reduction. In contrast, advanced air sealing, duct sealing and insulation, and attic floor insulation individually reduced energy consumption by 18%, 8%, and 1%, respectively. This demonstrates that the full envelope package maximizes energy efficiency, highlighting the synergistic benefits of combining all three measures.

## 4.1.2 Individual Building Time-Series Results

When the surrounding air is cooler, a refrigerator operates more efficiently and uses less energy. The average indoor temperature over the year increased slightly from 71.28°F to 71.46°F. This slight increase in temperature results in a minor increase in refrigerator energy use. However, the impact is negligible-the change in appliance electricity consumption is 0.21%.
