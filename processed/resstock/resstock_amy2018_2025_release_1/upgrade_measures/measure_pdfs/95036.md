## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact, market-ready (or nearly marketready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An ' end-use savings shape ' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock ™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson et al. 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: Residential VariableSpeed Geothermal Heat Pump (GHP). This document provides the relevant new modeling information for variable-speed systems not previously covered in either the single-stage or twostage documents. Variable-speed GHPs represent the most efficient option available for this technology; they provide the highest site energy savings, with up to 46% for the applicable portion of the housing stock, compared to 31% for less efficient single-stage GHPs. Additional results shown here detail how the savings change for sections of the housing stock with different base heating fuels and in different climate zones, and they show the savings potential by state for both heating and cooling. Utility bills and electric panel impacts are also shown and discussed.

## Relation to Other GHP Models

Unless otherwise noted in this document, it is assumed that certain components of the variablespeed GHP model overlap with (i.e., are the same as) single- and two-stage model components. The following summarizes these overlapping components:

- Technology summary : The variable-speed GHP works using the same general principles as the two-stage GHP, except that it has a variable-speed compressor along with improved rated heating and cooling efficiency values.
- Applicability : The variable-speed GHP applies to the same set of dwelling units as the single- and two-stage GHPs.
- Modeling approach : The variable-speed GHP follows the same basic modeling approach as the two-stage GHP, except that the performance curves are updated, and a pump power energy management system (EMS) program is used. The model of the ground heat exchanger is identical for any GHP, and documentation is included in the single-stage GHP measure document.

See the single-stage (Maguire et al. 2025a) and two-stage (Maguire et al. 2025b) reports for more detailed information about components that do not change between single-stage and two-stage GHPs.

## 1 Technology Summary

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

GHPs are highly efficient systems that provide space heating and cooling by leveraging the relatively stable temperatures below the Earth's surface. By exchanging heat with the ground , where temperatures remain more constant year-round, GHPs can operate with greater efficiency and reliability across a wide range of climates. A typical GHP system includes three main components: a ground heat exchanger, a heat pump unit, and an air distribution system within the building.

There are several types of GHPs, including single-stage, two-stage, and variable-speed systems:

- Single-stage GHPs operate at full capacity whenever they run, which can lead to energy inefficiencies under part-load conditions (when the system needs less than its maximum output).
- Two-stage GHPs improve upon this design by adding a second, lower-capacity stage. This allows the system to operate more efficiently during mild conditions while still having the capability to switch to full output when demand peaks.
- Variable-speed GHPs represent the most advanced option, using inverter-driven compressors and fans to continuously adjust their output in response to real-time heating and cooling needs. Unlike single-stage or two-stage systems that operate in discrete steps, variable-speed systems can modulate across a wide range of capacities. This precise control enhances energy efficiency, provides more stable indoor temperatures, reduces humidity swings, and minimizes noise from cycling.

Although variable-speed GHPs typically have the highest upfront cost among GHP technologies, they deliver superior part-load efficiency and can achieve the lowest overall operating costs over time. As a result, they are often considered the premium choice for homeowners seeking maximum comfort and performance.

The intent of this GHP measure is to reflect the characteristics of a high-performance, variablespeed, water-to-air GHP, modeled after the most advanced products on the 2025 ENERGY STAR ®  Most Efficient 2025 GHP list. 1 The system assumes a vertical ground heat exchanger configuration, which is a common and space-efficient option for residential applications.

## 2 Modeling Approach

## 2.1  Applicability

Using the same applicability criteria as the single- and two-stage GHPs, the variable-speed compressor, water-to-air, vertical ground heat exchanger GHP upgrade measure applies to all dwellings with:

- Non-shared (in-unit) HVAC and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane
- Shared (across multiple units) HVAC and a building height that is less than eight stories and any of the following heating fuels: electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or propane
- Dwelling units with ducts
- Dwelling units with soil thermal conductivity greater than 0.8 Btu/hr-ft-°F.

With these applicability constraints, the measure upgrade applies to roughly 71% of the existing building stock. The 6.4% of the residential units that receive the GHP upgrade lack existing space-cooling systems in the baseline. Units that are excluded from this measure, as well as more details about GHP modeling and assumptions, are more fully described in the documentation for the single-stage GHP document (Maguire et al. 2025a).

The applicability of this measure upgrade is based on ResStock housing characteristics that determine the available options and the options' details (Reyna, et al. 2025). Specifically, the 'HVAC heating e fficiency' characteristic in ResStock is used to apply each respective heat pump option. 'HVAC has d ucts' describes the presence of ducts in the dwelling. 'Heating t ype' describes the baseline heating fuel of the dwelling. 'HVAC shared e fficiencies' describes the shared HVAC system of the dwelling, if applicable. 'Geometry building type h eight' describes the height and building type of the dwelling. More information on the ResStock housing characteristics can be found in the ResStock Technical Reference Documentation . 2

## 2.2  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

For the variable-speed GHP, the same ResStock building energy model and OpenStudio ® -HPXML workflow are used as for the single- and two-stage GHPs.

OpenStudioHPXML's apply\_ground\_to\_air\_heat\_pump() 3 method is used to apply multiple sets of speeds (and their respective performance curves) to water-to-air coil objects that support various speed levels:

- CoilCoolingWaterToAirHeatPumpVariableSpeedEquationFit
- CoilHeatingWaterToAirHeatPumpVariableSpeedEquationFit.

New performance curves, representing variable-speed equipment, were developed and used to calculate the cooling/heating total capacity/energy input ratio (EIR) 4 as a function of temperature, as a function of airflow rate, and as a function of water flow rate. Updates were also made to an EnergyPlus ® EMS program controlling the flow rate of water though the heat pump and ground heat exchanger.

## 2.2.1  Variable-Speed GHP Heating and Cooling Efficiency

This measure includes a water-to-air GHP with a variable-speed compressor rated with a heating COP of 4.4 and a rated cooling EER of 30.9. The ground heat exchanger shape and properties are the same as used for the single- and two-stage GHPs: a rectangular and vertically drilled single U-tube design with thermally enhanced grout and piping. This section describes the inputs and modeling methodology for the heat pump. Some inputs, the sizing methodology, and the modeling methodology for the heat pump and the ground heat exchanger are the same as for the single- and two-stage GHPs. Refer to the section with the corresponding name in those documents for additional details.

Many ResStock arguments are assigned in the HVAC heating efficiency characteristic. The arguments being specified (different than the OpenStudio-HPXML defaults) that help distinguish these technologies from the single- and two-stage GHP are:

- Heat pump compressor type
- Heat pump heating efficiency
- Heat pump cooling efficiency.

For this measure, the compressor type is variable speed, as stated by the measure definition.

## 2.2.1.1  Heating and Cooling Efficiency

Compared to the single- and two-stage compressor types, the variable-speed compressor type can fine-tune the process for how efficiently the GHP system transfers heat and matches the load. Variable-speed compressors use less energy through less frequent on-off cycles than two-stage compressors. Fewer cycles lead to reduced temperature fluctuations and higher efficiency.

## 2.2.2  Variable-Speed GHP Heat Pump Performance Curves

To model variable-speed GHPs with

Coil:Cooling:WaterToAirHeatPump:VariableSpeedEquationFit and Coil:Heating:WaterToAirHeatPump:VariableSpeedEquationFit EnergyPlus objects, several different performance curves are required. At both part load and full load, for both heating and cooling, curves are required for:

- The efficiency of the GHP as a function of water and air temperature (biquadratic)
- The capacity of the GHP as a function of water and air temperature (biquadratic)
- The efficiency of the GHP as a function of airflow fraction (quadratic or cubic)
- The capacity of the GHP as a function of airflow fraction (quadratic or cubic)
- The efficiency of the GHP as a function of water flow fraction (quadratic or cubic)
- The capacity of the GHP as a function of water flow fraction (quadratic or cubic).

The EnergyPlus auxiliary program HVAC Performance Curve Fit Tool was used to develop the biquadratic curves for capacity and EIR as a function of air temperature and water temperature. 8 The tool is designed for direct expansion coil performance curves, but considering the similarity of the curve requirements between these two coils, it is reasonable to use the same tool for GHPs.

After generating coefficients with the spreadsheet-based tool from EnergyPlus, we compared the original manufacturer catalog data with the curve-fit data to ensure the correctness of the curve coefficients. These comparisons are documented in the appendix.

Curves here were generated based on data for a variable-speed WaterFurnace 7 Series 3-ton GHP unit (WaterFurnace SDW7-0018W 9 ). This unit comes from a major U.S. manufacturer and includes data in a format that is easy to translate into EnergyPlus inputs. Although this is a fully variable-speed unit, the specification sheet for this unit only includes the performance of the unit at full-load and part-load conditions, similar to what is shown for two-stage units. Although the model can handle additional speeds, specification sheets for products that included additional speeds did not have all the details necessary to translate into the required EnergyPlus inputs. Curves for the unit performance were derived in a similar manner as for the two-stage unit, and the overall equations for efficiency and capacity as a function of water and air temperature have the same form as what it used for the two-stage 10 and single-stage units. 11 As a result, only two sets of curves, for full and part load, are shown here. Unlike the two-stage coil, a part-load curve is not applied when the load is less than the part-load conditions because the equipment is assumed to be able to correctly modulate to very low part-load ratios, whereas a two-stage system might be cycling during low loads. The model interpolates when operating between full load and part load in a way that can account for the real variable-speed performance. The pump used here is also assumed to be truly variable speed, so water flow rates will vary proportionally to the load rather than running at fixed low and high speeds, like the two-stage equipment.

## 2.2.3  Pump Mass Flow Rate EMS

As mentioned in the two-stage GHP document EMS section, EnergyPlus models with the variable-speed, water-to-air coil object for two-stage systems and variable-speed, water-to-air GHP systems have some deficiency in pump operations and pump energy calculations.

As described in the previous section when discussing the operation of variable-speed GHPs and how that relates to how the performance curves are used, the pump used for a GHP can also modulate across most conditions, with the only exception being that at continuously very low loads (less than 20 -30% of the full-load capacity), the equipment will cycle rather than being able to maintain continuous operation. But as mentioned in the two-stage GHP document, EnergyPlus currently models a constant water flow rate at time steps below the first specified speed in the two-stage and variable-speed GHP models. In addition to what is mentioned in the two-stage GHP document about the EnergyPlus pump power EMS program, a pump mass flow rate EMS program is therefore needed for variable-speed GHP models to account for the varying water flow rates below the first specified speed and to account for the impact on coil performance.

EMS is an advanced feature in EnergyPlus to develop custom control and modeling routines. It allows users to write their own computer programs to override selected aspects of EnergyPlus modeling. In this case, the pump mass flow rate is overridden and recalculated in the EMS program for every time step when the heat pump is operating below the first specified speed. This program is combined with the program previously described in the two-stage GHP report that changes the pump power below the lowest specified speed to correctly model the pump behavior of variable-speed GHP units.

EnergyPlus-predicted heating and cooling loads are reported and used in the EMS program along with the system nominal capacities to calculate the fraction of full load for the GHPs. The pump mass flow rate is calculated based on the fraction of full load. Whenever the pump mass flow rate is below the first specified speed of a variable-speed GHP, it will be overridden to overcome the EnergyPlus limitation mentioned in the two-stage GHP document.

Because the two-stage and variable-speed GHP models use the same EnergyPlus HVAC objects, they share the same basic model structure with different inputs and performance curves processed from their respective equipment manufacturer data. This means that the EnergyPlus node water flow rate limit bug fix described in the two-stage document will be applied to both types of GHP systems when EnergyPlus 25.1.0 or later is used for ResStock.

By combining all these efforts, OpenStudio-HPXML can model the desired GHP operations with the correct interactions between pumps and heat pump coils and give satisfying results for the variable-speed GHP systems.

## 2.3  Limitations and Concerns

In addition to the limitations and concerns discussed in the prior single-stage and two-stage GHP documents, the following concerns are unique to variable-speed GHPs:

- EMS program flow rate calculation: The EMS program used for variable-speed GHPs calculates the pump mass flow rate based on the steady-state heat pump capacities. Although this approach is sufficient for most conditions, it could introduce minor differences in the mass flow estimates, which could slightly affect flow-rate-dependent capacity and efficiency curves.
- Limited compressor speed data: Only two speeds are explicitly modeled due to limitations in the manufacturer data for the variable-speed unit used here. Interpolation between these speeds, along with water flow and airflow-rate-dependent curves, is applied to represent performance across the full operating range.
- Part-load representation: Unlike single- or two-stage systems, no part-load ratio to partload fraction curve is applied for the variable-speed model. In EnergyPlus, the system is assumed to continuously modulate between 0 and 100% capacity, even at very low-load fractions. In reality, at extremely low-load conditions (for example, 5% of the maximum load), even variable-speed equipment might cycle on and off to meet the load seen during

the time step in EnergyPlus. Because of the small amount of time and energy spent at fractions such as 5% or below, this better captures the overall energy performance of this equipment.

## 3.4  Electric Panel Impact

This section details the impact the variable-speed GHP upgrade will have on electrical panels. The modeling assumptions and details about electric panels in ResStock can be found in the single-stage GHP documentation (Maguire et al. 2025a).

First, Figure 36 and Figure 37 show information about the ResStock 2025 Release 1 baseline housing stock electric panel distribution. The first column in Figure 36 , 'All,' shows that approximately 35% of all dwelling units have 0 or only 1 breaker space available. The first column in Figure 37 shows that 97.7% of electric panels have no existing capacity constraint for the baseline housing stock, but the other 2.3% of electric panels already have an existing capacity constraint.

Capacity constraints in the existing baseline stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. But it is important to recognize that this level of uncertainty applies to all panel results, including those that look reasonable; hence, the capacity-constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on.

Before this variable-speed GHP retrofit would take place, the panels experiencing a capacity constraint would likely need to address this issue first. Users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose preexisting capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.
