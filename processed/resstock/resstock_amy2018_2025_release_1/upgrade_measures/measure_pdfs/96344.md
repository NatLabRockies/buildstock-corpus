## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover most high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to energy efficiency retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. This results in a time-series profile broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model is intended to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson, et al., 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: duct sealing and insulation upgrade.

The duct sealing and insulation upgrade demonstrated 7% total site energy savings (240 TWh) for the U.S. residential building stock (Figure 3). These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 11% stock natural gas heating savings (129 TWh)
- 11% stock electricity cooling savings (43 TWh)
- 8% stock electricity heating savings (22 TWh).

## 1  Technology Summary

Ducts are used to distribute conditioned air throughout a house from a forced-air heating or cooling system, such as a central furnace, central air conditioner, or ducted heat pump. This measure applies duct sealing to all homes that use ducts for air distribution. Sealing ductwork located in unconditioned spaces saves energy, improves thermal comfort, and can even improve indoor air quality by patching return leaks on ducts exposed to pollutants. This increases the efficiency and performance of HVAC systems, allowing conditioned air to be distributed more effectively to where it is needed while keeping it clean and separated from dusty areas.

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

It is estimated that 20% -30% of air moving through ducts leaks into unconditioned spaces instead of reaching its intended destination in the home (ENERGY STAR, 2025). This leakage reduces the efficiency of the heating and cooling system. Duct sealing and insulation reduce heat loss through ductwork and the loss of conditioned air to unconditioned spaces, thereby reducing the amount of input energy needed to deliver the desired amount of heating or cooling. According to the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE, 2011), many homes have HVAC ducts located in unconditioned spaces like attics or crawl spaces. For ducts located in conditioned spaces, duct sealing and insulation will have little impact on energy use.

Ductwork is often hidden in walls, ceilings, attics, basements, or crawlspaces, which can make repairs challenging. However, there are several ways to reduce leakage and improve efficiency. Duct sealing can reduce leakage directly and can be achieved by several actions (ENERGY STAR, 2025): (1) repair damaged or disconnected ducts and straighten out flexible ducts that are tangled or crushed; (2) seal all leaks and connections with mastic, UL-Listed tape, or an aerosolbased sealant; (3) seal all duct boots to the drywall or subfloor; (4) include a new filter as part of any duct system improvement; (5) install a filter slot opening cover where missing or where the existing cover does not provide an adequate seal.

In addition to sealing, insulating ducts can help reduce heat loss. Duct insulation is typically applied by wrapping fiberglass insulation around the ducts. Other products, such as spray foam, can also be used to insulate ducts, depending on the application and installation requirements. If insulation is poor, the existing wraps are often replaced with more effective material. ENERGY STAR ®  (ENERGY STAR, 2025) recommends insulating ducts in unconditioned areas (such as attics, crawlspaces, and garages) with insulation that has an R-value of R-6 or higher. The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) Standard Work Specifications require insulating all ducts outside the thermal boundary to a minimum of R-8, and insulating all ducts exposed to the exterior to a minimum of R-12 (NREL, 2025). However, in contrast to ducts located in attics, crawlspaces, or garages, insulating ducts that run within enclosed building envelope cavitiessuch as wall or floor assemblies-can be more challenging. This type of insulation is typically performed as part of broader envelope insulation work.

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

HVAC duct insulation requirements depend on duct location and climate zone. According to IECC 2015, supply and return air ducts must be insulated with at least R-6 insulation when located in unconditioned spaces, at least R-8 insulation when located outside the building in climate zones 1 through 4, and a minimum of R-12 insulation in climate zones 5 through 8.

When located within a building envelope assembly, the duct must be separated from the building exterior or unconditioned or exempt spaces by a minimum of R-8 insulation in climate zones 1 through 4, and a minimum of R-12 insulation in climate zones 5 through 8 (IECC, 2015).

After ductwork improvements, duct insulation can vary based on the duct location within a home. However, for modeling purposes, ResStock assumes uniform duct insulation throughout the entire dwelling unit. After improving insulation on HVAC ducts, the final R-value can range from R-4.2 to R-12, depending on factors such as climate, duct placement, and overall system design. According to research by NLR, increasing duct insulation to R-8 is considered a reasonable target (Andrews, 2001).

This measure assumes insulation is added only to ducts located in unconditioned spaces, wherever accessible (e.g., attics, crawlspaces, or garages). If the ducts are currently uninsulated, they are wrapped with R-12 insulation, such as flexible fiberglass duct wrap or pre-formed insulation. Since IECC requires a minimum of R-6 insulation for ducts in unconditioned spaces (IECC, 2015), ducts that already meet or exceed this standard are not upgraded. Therefore, if the existing insulation is below R-6, the old wrap is removed and replaced with high-performance R12 insulation. Ducts integrated with the building envelope are not modified. For applicable dwelling units, where duct insulation in unconditioned spaces is currently below the IECC requirement, we also assume that ducts integrated with the building envelope assembly fall below code-specifically, less than R-8 in climate zones 1-4 and less than R-12 in climate zones 5-8. Based on these assumptions, this measure assumes = the average duct insulation level across the entire dwelling unit reaches an R-value of R-8 after improvements.

Duct leakage can be defined as a percentage of airflow or as an airflow leakage volume value. The percentage rating is calculated by comparing the duct system's measured leakage rate to the air handler's flow rate. This metric is useful for assessing HVAC system efficiency. The leakage volume value is measured in cubic feet per minute (cfm) airflow leakage per 100 ft 2 of conditioned floor area. It estimates the total air volume leaked from the system, helping to visualize the extent of leakage and estimate the scope of repair work needed. The IECC (2012) limits leakage to 4 cfm per 100 ft 2 of conditioned floor area when the air handler is installed and 3 cfm when it is not installed. Industry standards generally require new duct systems to be sealed to 6% or less leakage, while altered or existing systems may be allowed a leakage rate of up to 15% (Eco Performance Builders, 2025). Therefore, this measure will not seal ducts with a leakage rate of 6% or less.

After improving ductwork, the target for duct leakage after a retrofit is recommended to be 5% (Andrews, 2001). However, many contractors strive for even lower leakage levels. For example, Eco Performance Builders (2025), a retrofit company based in the Bay Area, reports an average duct leakage of under 1% after a retrofit, while PV Heating, Cooling &amp; Plumbing (Peavey, 2025), based in the Atlanta metropolitan area, aims for 4% leakage or less. To reach this target, PV Heating, Cooling &amp; Plumbing technicians seal all duct connections and joints using only duct mastic and high-quality mastic tape. Once a duct leakage test confirms that leakage is below the target threshold, they proceed to insulate the sealed duct areas. Achieving 4% duct leakage requires significant effort. In a typical home, it often takes multiple cycles of sealing and testing to meet this performance standard. Therefore, we assume this measure can achieve a 5% duct leakage rate.

## 2  Applicability

The goal of this measure is to achieve 5% duct air leakage combined with R-8 insulation for ducts located in unconditioned spaces. Ducts in the conditioned spaces do not require treatment because their heat loss goes directly to the conditioned space and is therefore not 'wasted'. This measure applies to dwelling units with either duct leakage to the outside greater than 6% or duct insulation less than R-6. Dwelling units with 0% leakage and uninsulated ducts are not included, as these configurations typically indicate that the ducts are located entirely within conditioned spaces and ResStock reflects this assumption. Though, ducts in real homes can pass through both conditioned and unconditioned spaces, requiring section-by-section treatment. Nevertheless, based on these assumptions, this duct sealing and insulation measure applies to approximately 46.3% of the U.S. housing stock, as shown in Table 1.

## 3  Modeling Approach

ResStock assigns three characteristics related to air distribution ductwork for each housing unit: whether the unit has ducts, the location of the ducts, and a combined characteristic that includes both the level of duct insulation and the amount of leakage from the ducts to unconditioned space. This measure does not change whether ducts are present or their locations; it only changes the duct insulation and leakage. Air leakage and heat gains and losses are calculated for the fraction of the ductwork outside conditioned space. The primary location of the duct system is sampled from the 'duct location.tsv' file. OpenStudio-HPXML assigns the fraction of the duct area in the conditioned space if the duct location is in unconditioned space. If the number of floors above grade is greater than one, then 25% of the duct surface area is assigned to the conditioned space. OpenStudio-HPXML also assigns fractions of supply/return ducts with rectangular and circular cross-sections, which affects the effective R-value for a given nominal duct insulation R-value.

## 3.1  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

The ResStock building stock energy model includes conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that represent the contiguous U.S. residential building stock. These distributions are sampled to create a synthetic stock, with each sample assigned an option for each characteristic for every dwelling unit to be simulated. ResStock utilizes the OpenStudioHPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models that can be simulated with OpenStudio ®  and EnergyPlus ®  using an HPXML file. For reference, the state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository when the ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset was created can be found here.

## 3.3  Electrical Panel

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025 to assess whether the existing panel has enough capacity or breaker space to accommodate new loads from an energy retrofit. For details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al., 2025). Duct sealing and insulation do not introduce new electrical loads or circuits, so they have no impact on the electrical panel. However, electrical panel metrics are included as a standard output for this and all other envelope measures. Any panel constraints appearing in the upgrade results reflect pre-existing conditions, as this measure does not worsen them.

## 4  Results

This section first confirms the intended modeling approach for the upgrade by examining individual dwelling models, then shows the results if the upgrade were applied to the entire applicable building stock.

## 4.1  Single Building Example

This section analyzes three example dwelling units that received duct sealing and insulation upgrades. Before the upgrades, each unit had uninsulated ducts with 20% leakage to the outside. After the upgrades, duct leakage was reduced to 5%, and R-8 insulation was added. The units are in Hawai'i (climate zone 1A), North Carolina (climate zone 3A), and Alaska (climate zone 8AK), representing a range of climate conditions. The floor areas of these three dwelling units are different. Furthermore, the heating and cooling setpoint temperatures are also different. As a result, the baseline heating and cooling energy consumption can vary across the three example dwelling units, appearing either high or low. This section does not compare absolute energy consumption among these three example dwellings. Instead, it focuses solely on the percentage energy reduction achieved by the duct sealing and insulation.

## 4.1.1  Individual Building Annual Energy Consumption

Figure 1 shows the annual energy consumption of three example dwelling units before and after duct sealing and insulation upgrades. The upgrades resulted in reductions across several heatingand cooling-related end uses, including heating, cooling, heating and cooling fans/pumps, heating heat pump backup, and associated backup fans/pumps.

The example dwelling unit in Hawai'i (climate zone 1A) uses electricity for both heating and cooling. Its total cooling energy consumption (including cooling and cooling fans/pumps) was reduced from 46.5 MBtu to 38.6 MBtu, representing a 17% reduction. Because heating demand in Hawai'i is negligible, heating savings are not analyzed for this location.

The example dwelling unit in North Carolina (climate zone 3A) also uses electricity for both heating and cooling. Its cooling energy consumption was reduced from 11.3 MBtu to 9.0 MBtu, a 20% reduction. Additionally, total heating energy consumption (heating, heating fan/pumps, heat pump backup, and associated fans/pumps) was reduced from 19.7 MBtu to 14.7 MBtu, a 25% reduction.

The example dwelling unit in Alaska (climate zone 8AK) uses fuel oil for heating and has no cooling system; therefore, cooling savings are not analyzed for this location. Its total heating energy use (heating and heating fans/pumps) was reduced from 167 MBtu to 135.7 MBtu, representing a 19% reduction.

The percentage reduction in energy use for these three example dwelling units is similar to findings from a field study conducted by Jump et al. (1996). In that study, 24 houses in the Sacramento, California, area were tested before and after duct retrofitting. The average air leakage was reduced from 18% to 9% after the retrofit, resulting in an average 18% decrease in HVAC energy use (Jump, Walker, &amp; Modera, 1996).

## 4.1.2 Individual Building Time Series

Section 4.1.1 shows that for the example dwelling units, only heating- and cooling-related energy consumption changed as a result of the duct sealing and insulation upgrade. So, this section analyzes 15-minute time-series data for those end uses across the three example units, as shown in Figure 2.  Heating end uses include heating, heating fans/pumps, heating heat pump backup, and associated backup fans/pumps. Cooling end uses include cooling and cooling fans/pumps. January 2 is selected to examine the heating time-series data, while July 2 is selected to examine the cooling time-series data. The duct sealing and insulation upgrade consistently reduce energy use throughout the day in all example dwelling units.

In North Carolina (climate zone 3A), as shown in Figures 2(a) and  2(b), the upgrades significantly lower summer cooling peaks in the afternoon as well as winter heating peaks in the morning and evening.

In Hawai'i (climate zone 1A), as shown in Figure 2(c), the upgrades help reduce cooling loads throughout the day. For the example dwelling in Hawai'i, the sudden spikes in cooling energy consumption at 3:15 and 22:15, and the sharp drops at 7:15 and 12:15, are driven by changes in the cooling setpoint temperature. At 3:00 and 22:00, the setpoint decreases-from 67.5°F to 66°F and from 70°F to 67.5°F, respectively-triggering increased cooling energy use over the following 15 minutes. In contrast, at 7:00 and 12:00, the setpoint rises-from 66°F to 68.5°F and from 68.5°F to 70°F-eliminating the cooling demand and resulting in zero energy use for the next 15 minutes.

In Alaska (climate zone 8AK), as shown in Figure 2(d), the upgrades result in a steady reduction in baseline heating energy use across all hours.

Overall, the results demonstrate that duct sealing and insulation upgrades are effective in improving energy efficiency across a range of climates, especially during periods of high thermal demand.

Table 5 shows the annual energy consumption savings percentage per upgraded dwelling unit. As discussed earlier, most savings occurred in heating and cooling end uses. However, there is only a small change in hot water-related end uses. When both the water heater and ducts are in the same place (e.g., unheated basement, attic), hot water energy consumption can be slightly affected due to reduced heat transfer from the ducts. Furthermore, changes in indoor temperature also slightly impact hot water energy consumption. Overall, the effect on hot water energy use is minimal-less than 1%.

There is also a very small change in appliance electricity use, primarily due to refrigerators. Refrigerators transfer heat from the interior to the surrounding air. When the surrounding air is cooler, refrigerators operate more efficiently and use less energy. The average indoor temperature over the year decreased slightly from 71.28°F to 71.26°F. This marginal cooling results in a slight reduction in refrigerator energy use. However, the impact is negligible-the change in appliance electricity consumption is 0.01%.
