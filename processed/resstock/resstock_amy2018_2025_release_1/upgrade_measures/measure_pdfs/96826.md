## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in Reyna et al. (2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson, et al., 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure: Heat Pump Water Heater (HPWH) upgrade. This measure installs a high-efficiency 240V HPWH to all dwelling units that are not currently using a HPWH or a solar thermal water heater. The HPWH uses the refrigerant cycle to extract heat from the surrounding air to produce hot water at an efficiency several times higher than conventional electric resistance technology. With 99.5% of the U.S. housing stock applicable for this upgrade, this measure demonstrated:

- A total modeled savings potential of 280 TWh per year in site energy and $19 billion per year in energy costs based on 2024 state-average fuel rates.
- An average modeled savings of 2,210 kWh per year (8.2%) in site energy and $151 per year (5.3%) in energy bills for applicable occupied dwellings.
- 73% of the site energy savings come from natural gas and 19% from electricity.
- An increase in site energy for 1.5% of applicable dwellings due to an increased runtime of their inefficient heating systems (as the HPWH pulls heat from the conditioned space when installed in or near it).
- An increase in energy bills for 5.6% of applicable dwellings due to site energy increase and cost ratio between electricity and fuels.
- Electrical panel constraints affect one in five applicable dwellings; based on modeling, 17.9% do not have enough space and 2.7% do not have enough capacity for the upgrade.

## 1  Technology Summary

This measure models installing a high-efficiency 240V HPWH into all U.S. homes that are not already using a heat pump or solar thermal for water heating. The equipment has a uniform energy factor (UEF) no less than 3.3 and a first hour rating (FHR) no less than 45 gal/h.

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

A HPWH is a high-efficiency electric storage water heater that uses a refrigerant cycle to extract heat from the surrounding air to produce hot water. A 240V HPWH, also known as a hybrid water heater, has backup electric resistance elements inside the tank to allow several operation modes: heat pump only, electric resistance only, or hybrid (i.e., the high-power electric resistance only turns on to supplement the heat pump in high demand situations). The refrigerant cycle leverages phase-changing fluid to transfer more heat than the electricity it consumes, resulting in an efficiency level several times greater than that of electric resistance heating, which has nearly 100% efficiency (IEA, 2022).

For electric water heaters, HPWHs are the only technology type that can meet ENERGY STAR ® Version 7.0 efficiency requirement, which is set to an FHR of 45 gal/h and a UEF of 3.3 for 240V models (ENERGY STAR, 2025c). Note that 120V HPWHs, characterized by no or lowpower electric backup elements, are also ENERGY STAR qualified, but they are not considered in this measure.

The FHR indicates how much hot water a water heater can deliver in an hour of operation and is often used when sizing for an application. UEF is a metric used to quantify the energy efficiency of water heaters under standard test conditions (Jutras, 2024). The UEF test procedure includes a draw profile that is intended to reflect realistic hot water usage for that water heater and tank size. The FHR for a water heater determines which of four usage levels are used in the UEF test procedure. Thus, a UEF has a companion usage bin label and should only be compared with other UEF of the same bin. A higher UEF indicates that the water heater is more efficient in converting energy into hot water within its specific usage bin. HPWHs generally have an FHR between 45 and 87 gal/h and a UEF rating between 3.34 and 4.14. In comparison, an electric resistance water heater has a UEF of 0.9 to 0.95. HPWHs account for only 2% of all water heaters sold in 2024, but are gaining popularity due to a growing awareness of their efficiency and cost savings potential. HPWH sales grew by 35% from 2022 to 2023 (Wachunas, 2024).

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

## 1.2.1 Efficiency

In an electric HPWH, cold water comes in from the bottom of the tank and is heated by the heat pump coil or the backup elements. As hot water rises and goes out from the top, it creates a temperature gradient or a stratified tank. A tempering valve can be added to the outlet to mix the hot water with cold water to prevent scalding at the point of use, typically by keeping the temperature of water drawn after the tempering valve below 125°F. This also lets the tank temperature to be set higher, allowing for more stored energy and reducing the risk of legionella growth in the tank. Table 1 summarizes the range of performance of ENERGY STAR rated HPWHs available in the U.S. market according to EPA data retrieved in May 2025 (ENERGY STAR, 2025a).

Table 1. ENERGY STAR 240V HPWH Performance Ratings

| Volume (gal)   | FHR (gal/h)   | UEF       |
|----------------|---------------|-----------|
| <45            | 46-66         | 3.4-3.88  |
| 45-55          | 65-76         | 3.35-3.93 |
| 56+            | 70-87         | 3.34-4.14 |

## 1.2.2 Sizing

Storage water heater sizing depends on the first hour rating (FHR) per Uniform Plumbing Code. Right-sizing ensures the water heater is delivering the required hot water demand without increasing the equipment upfront cost or operational inefficiencies. Hot water demand is primarily driven by the number of occupants but can be influenced by many other factors, including the type and behavior of occupants, number and type of hot water appliances and fixtures, and length of distribution. Because number of occupants can change over time while physical characteristics like number of bedrooms and bathrooms are typically fixed, sizing is sometimes done in terms of these characteristics instead. Generally, the larger the household, the larger the house, and the larger the water heater. Additionally, because HPWHs recover slower than other storage water heaters when operating in heat pump mode, EnergyStar recommends to upsize for more storage capacity (ENERGY STAR, 2025b). NextGen program's minimum sizing requirement, which depends on the number of bedrooms and bathrooms (ENERGY STAR, 2025b).

## 1.2.3 Impact of Flue Vent

All fuel-based water heaters need to pull in air for combustion and require a way to safely vent the exhaust. This additional vent can add to the overall infiltration of the house and needs to be balanced to prevent dangerous back-drafting of the flue gas (Moffatt, 1991). Replacing fuelbased water heaters with HPWHs can improve the airtightness of the house and reduce space heating and cooling demand when their flue vent is eliminated. ResStock assumes that the water heater and the space heating system share a flue vent when both systems are in the conditioned space and require ventilation, as indicated by the parameter 'Has Flue or Chimney In Conditioned Space' in

Table 6 in Section 3.1. Therefore, the measure can remove the fuel water heater flue vent and reduce infiltration only when the flue vent is standalone and the existing space heating system does not require ventilation.

## 1.2.4 Impact of Location

The location of a HPWH can be a major consideration when it comes to its performance and impact on occupant comfort, and manufacturer guidance varies by climate region (ENERGY STAR, 2025b). The HPWH draws heat from the ambient air and exhausts air that is cooler and drier to the same space unless ducted. HPWH performance can be boosted in a warm environment (e.g., a utility room that offers waste heat from other appliances). In colder climates, installation in cold, unconditioned spaces, such as garages and outside, should be limited. Unvented small spaces, such as a closet, can also present a challenge, but can be mitigated with passive venting or short duct runs to another conditioned location (Larson &amp; Larson, 2022). While ResStock models water heater location diversity, it does not model the impact of tight closet spaces or ducting options for HPWHs. HPWHs can be slightly larger and taller than conventional storage water heaters due to the heat pump and larger tank to account for slower recovery, which can limit where they can be installed. They also require a drain for condensate or the addition of a condensate pump if a drain is not available. ResStock does not account for these limitations and assumes the existing water heaters can be swapped out for the HPWH without changing the installation location within the home.

When located within the conditioned space, the cooling from HPWH can lead to an energy increase for space heating and decrease for space cooling. This interactive effect is captured in ResStock and its net impact on space heating and cooling can vary by climate region. The cooling and dehumidification can also impact occupant comfort. Lower humidity makes it feel cooler, potentially allowing for a higher space cooling setpoint to achieve the same comfort. ResStock does not currently model indirect humidity impacts, such as that on setpoint, or the use of humidifiers or dehumidifiers in the housing stock. ResStock also assumes an 'interaction factor' of 1, where the HVAC responds to every unit of heat taken from the space and adjusts its operation accordingly to maintain setpoint. Actual data from homes shows this interaction can vary widely and is often less than 1 depending on where in the home the water heater is installed.

## 1.2.5 Electrical Requirement

The HPWH options considered in this measure require a 208/240V circuit protected by a doublepole breaker, which typically requires two breaker spaces. The equipment power rating is driven by the size of the backup elements, which are typically 4,500 W and require a 30A breaker (AOSmith, 2026). This information is used in ResStock to assess whether the home's electrical panel would be constrained in terms of space or capacity due to this upgrade.

## 2  Applicability

This measure replaces existing water heaters with 240V HPWHs for dwelling units without a HPWH or solar thermal. HPWH tank size is determined according to the number of bedrooms and number of occupants, as shown in Table 3. The HPWH upgrade is done in place without a location change. Based on the above assumption, 99.5% of housing stock receives the HPWH upgrade, as shown in Table 4. Note, 'other fuel' and 'wood' water heaters are mapped to coal and wood cord energy consumption in the output, respectively. These fuel types are excluded from the published energy results due to low confidence in the estimates, meaning they appear in the housing characteristics but not in the reported energy consumption. As a result, homes with water heaters of these fuel types will appear to show an increase in site energy consumption that does not reflect real energy changes from the upgrade.

## 3  Modeling Approach

ResStock models one water heater for every home and all water heaters as a standalone appliance, with diversity in efficiency and installed location informed by Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) 2020 microdata (US EIA, 2024). Most water heaters are installed in the conditioned space or adjacent to the thermal envelope, with over 40% in the living space and 11% in heated basements. ResStock does not model multifamily centralized water heaters in buildings, which exist in about 14% of the stock according to the 'Water Heater In Unit' parameter in ResStock. 1 Instead, a portion of the central water heater's energy consumption is approximated for the modeled dwelling unit using a standalone water heater model located in a conditioned mechanical space outside the dwelling unit. The out-of-unit location prevents the water heater from interacting with any in-unit systems, but the model does not account for any realistic hot water distribution and recirculation losses, which can be significantly more depending on the location of the dwelling. This means the water heating energy for multifamily units with 'out-of-unit' central water heating is generally underestimated for both the baseline and the upgrade, as they go from a shared water heater to a shared HPWH.

In ResStock, all HPWHs are modeled using a stratified tank model that operates in a hybrid mode with realistic thermostatic control. A stratified tank realistically captures the thermocline that exists inside the tank as hot water is drawn out from the top and cold water comes in the bottom. The heat pump reheats the bottom half of the tank, which is adjacent to the condenser. The tank is controlled using a weighted average of a measured upper and lower temperature. In hybrid mode, the water heater first turns on the heat pump and only resorts to the backup elements in high demand scenarios where the temperature drops substantially. Hot water draw schedules are stochastically modeled (Hendron, Burch, &amp; Barker, 2010), and total demand is tabulated from fixture (showers, sinks, and baths) use, which varies by the number of occupants, and from hot water appliances (dishwasher and clothes washer). Multipliers are additionally applied at random to each of the hot water demands to reflect usage diversity. An impact of this usage diversity is that a small family can use a lot of water when assigned large multipliers and vice versa, and large unmet hot water load is possible. When there is unmet load but no explicit comfort metric currently to track it, it makes it difficult to compare the energy consumption before and after an upgrade. However, as these edge cases are rare, the uncertainty is limited. Refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide or more details on the modeling process of water heating (Reyna, et al., 2025).

1 'Water Heater In Unit' is a misnomer since the parameter denotes whether the water heater is centralized and shared rather than the status of the water heater's physical location.

The condenser is wrapped around the bottom half of the tank under the jacket insulation, while the inlet air conditions vary based on installation location. ## 3.1  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

The ResStock building stock energy model contains conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that are representative of the U.S. residential building stock. These are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each sample has an assigned option for each characteristic for each dwelling unit to be simulated. ResStock utilizes the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio and EnergyPlus through an HPXML file. For reference, OpenStudio-HPXML v1.10.0 was used to produce ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset.

## 3.2  HPWH Upgrade

In the model articulation step of the workflow, each building characteristic assigned in ResStock is translated to OpenStudio-HPXML parameters. An OpenStudio-HPXML default value is used when there is no input from ResStock for a required field. A measure upgrade is modeled as changes to specific building characteristics of a model articulated using the baseline building stock characteristics. This section will go over the technical details of HPWH upgrade changes in the ResStock workflow.

## 3.3  Electrical Panel

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025. As new electric loads are added into the home during an improvement project, the electrical panel may not have enough capacity or space headroom to accommodate these new circuits. Thus, the panel may become constrained, requiring an electrical upgrade that may include upsizing the service entrance. Panel constraints can also be mitigated in other ways without the panel or service being upsized, by using load shedding devices, circuit sharing devices, or a smart panel to allow more loads to be connected; low-power, combination, or smart appliances to reduce electric loads at the source; slim tandem breakers or subpanels for more space; and more (Redwood Energy, 2022). The panel constraint estimate from the upgrade measure allows for the costing of any potential electrical upgrade or mitigation, thus helping to capture the total cost of upgrade more holistically across the housing stock.

Panel capacity and space constraints are modeled using a service load and a breaker space calculation, respectively. The load calculation is based on 2023 National Electrical Code's loadbased method in Section 220 (NFPA, 2023), which guides how the load diversity should be estimated and summed to estimate the total load connected to the panel. When this total load converted to ampacity exceeds the service rating of the panel, the panel is considered capacityconstrained in ResStock. Similarly, the panel is considered space-constrained when the total occupied breaker space counted from the bottom-up exceeds the rated space count of the panel. The panel's capacity and space ratings are probabilistically sampled based on other housing characteristics such as heating fuel and the presence of certain appliances. Users are encouraged to reevaluate these constraint metrics according to other considerations, such as a margin of safety to account for uncertainty in the modeling process. For more details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al., 2025).

## 4.2.1  Stock Annual Energy Impacts

By applying a high-efficiency HPWH to all homes eligible for such an upgrade-99.5 of the housing stock-the modeled overnight savings potential is estimated at nearly 280 TWh in total site energy, with 73% of the savings comes from natural gas and 19% from electricity, as shown in Figure 6. On average, the HPWH upgrade increased space heating energy more than its energy savings from space cooling, resulting in a small net increase in HVAC energy. This is due in part to there being slightly more space heating than cooling in the housing stock. In some cases, where the HPWH is installed in the conditioned space and space heating is provided by a low-efficiency heating equipment, the increased demand for heating can override the HPWH savings, leading to a net increase in total energy. This is shown in Figure 8, where small fractions of dwelling units using conventional, non-heat pump heating (totaling 1.5% of the overall applicable stock) see a small increase in site energy consumption after the HPWH upgrade.

In the ResStock models, it is assumed that the 'interaction factor' between any point source of heating or cooling in a space and the thermostat is 100%. For a HPWH, this means that every unit of heat taken out of the living space and put into the tank for water heating has some impact on the indoor temperature. During the heating season, this causes the HVAC equipment to run more, while during the cooling season this is a net benefit that reduces the run time of cooling equipment. The actual interaction factor in homes can vary substantially depending on where the water heater is relative to the thermostat and is very difficult to quantify, although some experiments that attempted to quantify this have found the impact can be less than 1, and in some cases even less than 0.5, depending on the configuration of the home (Widder, Metzger, Petersen, &amp; McIntosh, 2017). As a result, the impact of HPWHs on HVAC loads in this study may be larger than what is seen in practice in the field.

## 4.2.2  Stock Aggregated Time-Series Impacts

To illustrate the impact of the HPWH measure on the aggregated load profile, Figure 12 presents the modeled total residential stock electricity profile before and after the measure for HI, CA, and AK during their respective peak day in 2018. The impact varies significantly in each of the three states, which are in very different climates. The upgrade reduced HI's summer electric peak on 2018-09-30 16:30:00 local time from 0.78 GW by 8.5%, mostly because the bulk of the water heating load is already electric and benefits from the efficiency gain. For CA, which is also summer peaking, the upgrade reduced its electric peak on 2018-07-07 17:00:00 local time from 32.17 GW by only 0.5%. This is due to a higher share of fuel water heaters in CA, which adds new electric demand when replaced. This added demand offsets the efficiency gains from replacing electric water heaters with HPWH, resulting in a lower overall peak demand reduction. On the other hand, AK is winter-peaking, and instead of a peak load reduction like CA and HI, its electric peak on 2018-02-03 19:45:00 local time is increased by the upgrade from 0.43 GW by 9.2%, a relatively large increase. This is because in addition to new electric loads from replacing many fuel water heaters, similarly to CA, the measure increases AK's electric space heating by making the electric heaters, generally lower efficiency than the HPWH, run longer to make up the heat lost to the HPWH, thereby increasing the electric system peak. As illustrated by the examples, HPWH can significantly impact the electric grid load shape depending on the saturation of fuel water heaters and the interaction between HPWH and HVAC.

## 4.4  Electrical Panel Constraints

Figure 15 shows the distribution of modeled panel constraints overall (see 'all' column) and by water heater fuel. It is estimated that about 20% of the homes upgrading to a HPWH overall face either a breaker space or a capacity constraint, while about 80% have no constraints. Breaking down those constrained, 2.7% are constrained by capacity while 17.9% are constrained by space. 95% of the homes that face a panel constraint are those that previously have a fuel water heating system. This makes sense because the HPWH adds 4,500 W of peak load and requires two breaker spaces to install a new 240V circuit for the water heater under normal practice. Nearly four in ten homes with fuel water heaters become panel-constrained, mostly due not having enough space for a new circuit. For homes with electric resistance storage water heaters, the existing circuit for the water heater is reused, and the power rating is similar between the incumbent and the replacement water heater so no new load is added. For homes with electric resistance tankless water heaters, the total connected load is significantly reduced by the upgrade as the high-power tankless water heater is removed.

All post-upgrade constraint statistics includes a minute fraction of homes that are already capacity-constrained in the baseline. Prior to the upgrade, 2.3% of the existing panels are estimated to already be over-capacity, which is possible as the capacity constraint is estimated based on code. In contrast, no existing panel is space-constrained, which makes sense as it is physically impossible to connect more loads than there is space. Capacity constraints in the existing stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. However, it is important to recognize that this level of uncertainty applies to all panel results, including those that look reasonable. Hence, the capacity-constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on. During the HPWH upgrade, any homes already constrained before the upgrade will likely need to address their capacity issue in order to be permitted by code to connect a new load to the panel, whether the upgrade is making the constraint worse or not. Thus, the post-upgrade constraint statistics can be attributed entirely to the upgrade. Users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose pre-existing capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.
