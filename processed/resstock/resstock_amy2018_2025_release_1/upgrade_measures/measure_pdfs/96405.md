## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit enduse load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in (Reyna, et al., 2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (Wilson, et al. 2022).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure-ENERGY STAR ® Version 7.0 Window Upgrade.

The ENERGY STAR Version 7.0 Window Upgrade demonstrated 7% total site energy savings (0.25 TWh) for the U.S. residential building stock (Figure 6). These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 12% stock electricity heating savings (1.3E11 kWh)
- 12% stock natural gas heating savings (3.2E10 kWh)
- 10% stock electricity cooling savings (4.0E10 kWh)
- 11% stock cooling fan and pump electricity savings (7.11E9 kWh).

## 1  Technology Summary

Windows are an important part of the building envelope. They allow light and heat to pass, allow views of the outside world, and separate the interior conditions of the dwelling from the outdoors. This section will review how windows work and include details about the specific window characteristics included in this measure upgrade.

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

As commonly understood, a window is an area of transparent or translucent material in the wall of a building. The National Fenestration Rating Council (NFRC) is an independent, 501(c)(3) nonprofit certification body for the energy performance of windows, doors, and skylights (National Fenestration Rating Council (NFRC), n.d.). NFRC's technical definition of a window is 'An assembled unit consisting of a frame/sash component holding one or more pieces of glazing functioning to admit light and/or air into an enclosure and designed for a vertical installation in an external wall of a residential building. Includes transoms.' (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2022).

Window glazing and window frames and sashes can be made of a variety of materials. Historically, in the U.S., residential windows were made with wood frames supporting a single pane of clear glass. Today, window frames and sashes can be made from wood, aluminum, composite materials, fiberglass, or vinyl (U.S. Department of Energy, 2025). Windows today can have multiple panes of glass separated by gaps, which can be filled with special gases to reduce the heat transfer through the window. The glass can have special coatings on the surface to limit how much visible or infrared light is allowed through (U.S. Department of Energy, 2025). Figure 1 shows cross sections illustrating several different window glazing configurations. Windows with more panes generally allow lower amounts of heat and radiation to come through the window.

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

The appropriate choice of windows for a building depends on many factors. Some of the major energy-related factors include the climate, the orientation of the windows relative to the sun, and the architectural or landscape features surrounding the window. Non-energy-related factors such as material durability, aesthetics, and physical operation (opening and closing) are also important to consider.

## 1.2.1  ENERGY STAR Version 7.0 Residential Window Specification

Because of the wide variety of windows available, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) created the ENERGY STAR Program Requirements for Residential Windows, Doors, and Skylights to define energy-related performance characteristics for windows. Rather than specifying the materials, frame designs, fill gases, or coatings, the program specifies the performance metrics that a product must achieve. These performance metrics are:

- ' U-Factor : The heat transfer per time per area and per degree of temperature difference (Btu/h ft 2 ∙˚ F). The U-factor multiplied by the interior-exterior temperature difference and by the projected fenestration product area yields the total heat transfer through the fenestration product due to conduction, convection, and long-wave infra-red radiation. The lower the U-factor, the better the window insulates.
- 'Solar Heat Gain Coefficient (SHGC) : The ratio of the solar heat gain entering the space through the fenestration product to the incident solar radiation.
- 'Air Leakage : The volume of air flowing per unit time per unit area (cfm/ft 2 ) through a fenestration system due to air pressure or temperature difference between the outdoor and indoor environment.' (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2022).

The minimum performance metrics that a window must achieve to meet the ENERGY STAR Certification Criteria for Residential Windows, Version 7.0 (V7) (published in 2022) is summarized in Table 1. Figure 2 shows a map of the climate zones referenced in the table.

## 1.2.2 Characteristics of Certified Products

As shown in Table 1, the certification requirements establish a range of performance values rather than a singular value for each climate zone. For this report, NLR performed an assessment to understand the range of performance characteristics available in the market as of the time of writing.

The EPA Product Finder is a database of certified products. As of the date of publication, there were more than 1,000 certified window product lines, with hundreds or thousands of individual products within each line (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, n.d.). Because of the limitations of the Product Finder, it was not possible to extract detailed information for every product in every line. Instead, a sample of products was reviewed. According to the websites of several of the largest window manufacturers in the U.S., double hung windows are the most common type of windows in the residential market. NLR's review of four lines of double hung windows covering 50,000 individual window configurations from four of the ten largest U.S. window manufacturers was performed using data available through the NFRC Directory Search linked from the EPA Product Finder (National Fenestration Rating Council, n.d.).

As shown in Table 2, the performance characteristics of just a fraction of all available products show that their performance characteristics span the range of values that allow a product to achieve certification, rather than all products being at one extreme of the performance band.

## 1.2.3  Comparison of ENERGY STAR to Energy Code Requirements

In most places in the U.S., an energy code sets the minimum performance of new windows. For this upgrade, it is important that the modeled windows meet the energy code requirements for the locations they are installed.

To determine the minimum energy code performance by location, the methodology described in the 2024 NLR report Residential Facade Retrofits Modeling: Results and Documentation (Elaina Present, 2024) was followed. The code adoption status by state was updated as of July 2025, accessed from the International Code Council (ICC) website. For each county in the U.S., the ENERGY STAR and IECC climate zones were identified, and the required performance characteristics were assigned to each based on the IECC or state-specific code for the state where that county was located (International Code Council (ICC), 2025). For this exercise, no sub-state energy code requirements were considered.

The unique combinations of energy code minimum performance values, along with the number of counties and dwelling units with these requirements, are shown in Table 3. The most stringent requirement for each of the ENERGY STAR climate zones that applies to more than 2% of the dwelling units is highlighted. This will be used as the benchmark that the modeled ENERGY STAR windows must meet or exceed for each climate zone. The judgment not to base energy code minimum performance levels for each climate zone on requirements that apply to only a tiny percentage of dwellings in a handful of counties was based on the energy code requirement analysis not including individual county- or city-level energy code requirements or reviewing all individual state codes for custom modifications.

## 1.2.5 Infiltration Impacts of Upgraded Windows

As described in most window manufacturer literature, and as indicated by the presence of an air leakage requirement in the ENERGY STAR V7 performance characteristics, air leakage through and around windows plays a role in their energy performance. Unfortunately, literature quantifying this impact using measured data is limited. A 2003 paper (Ian Ridley, 2003) developed a simple model of the reduction that could be expected from replacing windows in the UK and concluded: 'A review of existing data suggests that replacing old leaky windows with modern sealed windows will reduce the background infiltration rate by the order of 0.1 air changes per hour (ach) to 0.3 ach. The actual value being dependent on the number of windows being replaced, the volume of the house and the leakiness of the old windows.' The values given in the paper are at natural pressure. Using the calculations from that paper, this equates to a reduction of 2-6 air changes per hour at 50 pascals (ACH50), which is a common way to describe infiltration levels.

In the ResStock baseline models, infiltration is specified as whole-dwelling ACH50. This input is dependent on climate zone, floor area, and vintage. The mean infiltration rate is generally higher in smaller, older homes in warmer climates. The mean infiltration rate ranges from around 20 ACH50 in older homes to 10 ACH50 in newer homes. There is no explicit relationship between infiltration and window type. However, there is an explicit relationship between window type and dwelling vintage. The resulting correlation is that older homes tend to have both lower-performance windows and higher infiltration. ## 3  Modeling Approach

## 3.1  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

The ResStock building stock energy model contains conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that are representative of the U.S. residential building stock (Reyna, et al., 2025). These are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each sampled dwelling unit has an assigned option for each characteristic. ResStock utilizes the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio and EnergyPlus through the use of an HPXML file. For reference, the state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository when the ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset was created can be found here (NREL, 2025). Assumptions about the window modeling that are not explicitly defined in ResStock are handled by the BuildResidentialHPXML measure.

## 3.3  Limitations and Concerns

There are two key limitations and concerns for this modeling. First, because the requirements for certification allow a range of performance characteristics, this modeling approach inherently cannot reflect the performance of all possible certified products. As described in the results, the choices of performance characteristics even show negative savings for a tiny segment of the stock. In reality, it is likely possible to identify certified products that achieve positive savings for this segment. It is also possible to select certified products that achieve better or worse performance compared to what was modeled.

The second concern is the use of the simplified window glazing model in OS-HPXML. A detailed investigation of the tradeoffs in that modeling simplification was beyond the scope of this effort because changing OS-HPXML was also beyond the scope. However, this simplification may have ramifications on the results.

## 4.2  Stock Energy Impacts

Replacing existing windows with those that meet the ENERGY STAR V7 criteria is estimated to create 7% (0.25 TWh) annual stock site energy savings for residential buildings nationally (see Figure 6). This includes 12% site electricity heating savings (1.3E11 kWh), 12% site natural gas heating savings (3.2E10 kWh), 10% site electricity cooling savings (4.0E10 kWh), and 11% site fan and pump energy savings (7.11E9 kWh). Heating, cooling, and fan energy savings can be partially attributed to the decreased thermal conductance (U-value) of the replacement windows, which generally reduces heating and cooling loads. This reduces the energy requirement of the heating and cooling system, but can also decrease the amount of time HVAC fans and pumps need to run, which explains the energy savings in that category. Similarly, a lower SHGC reduces solar gains to the building, which can also reduce cooling load, but it some cases may increase heating load due to reduced heat gain when the building is actively heating. Because this upgrade is applicable to all dwelling units, the whole-stock impact and applicable segment impact results are identical.

## 4.4  Electrical Panel Impacts

An electric panel constraint occurs when a unit's electrical service panel (the breaker box) does not have enough capacity (amps) or space (breaker slots) to support new electrical equipment or increased electrical load from existing equipment. Upgrading windows does not add additional electric equipment to the dwelling. Depending on the existing HVAC equipment, it generally reduces either equipment runtime or equipment power draw. Therefore, this upgrade is broadly applicable nationwide without concerns related to electrical panel capacity (National Fenestration Rating Council (NFRC), n.d.).
