## Executive Summary

The goal of this work is to develop energy efficiency, demand flexibility, and other retrofit end-use load shapes (electricity, gas, propane, or fuel oil) that cover a majority of the high-impact, market-ready (or nearly market-ready) measures. 'Measures' refers to energy efficiency retrofits that can be applied to buildings during modeling.

An 'end-use savings shape' is the difference in energy consumption between a baseline building and a building with an energy efficiency, demand flexibility, or other retrofit measure applied. It results in a time-series profile that is broken down by end use and fuel (electricity or on-site gas, propane, or fuel oil use) at each time step.

ResStock™ is a highly granular, physics-based, bottom-up model that uses multiple data sources, statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual subhourly energy consumption of the residential building stock across the United States. The baseline model intends to represent the U.S. residential building stock as it existed in 2018. Technical documentation for the inputs and assumptions in the baseline building stock model is available in (Reyna, et al. 2025). Calibration and validation of the baseline model results are available in the final technical report of the End-Use Load Profiles project (https://www.nlr.gov/buildings/end-use-load-profiles.html).

This documentation focuses on a single end-use savings shape measure-Dual Fuel Heat Pump System Upgrade.

The Dual Fuel Heat Pump System Upgrade demonstrated 9.3% total site energy savings (1,097 TBtu) for the U.S. residential building stock (Figure 5). These savings are primarily attributed to:

- 17% stock total heating savings (1,005.6 TBtu)
- 5% stock total cooling savings (65.7 TBtu).

## 1  Technology Summary

The dual fuel heating system in this measure upgrade consists of an air-source heat pump (ASHP) and a natural gas furnace. In this configuration the natural gas furnace heats the dwelling during the coldest outdoor temperatures of the year, while the ASHP cools the dwelling during the cooling season and heats it at mild outdoor temperatures during the heating season. This section provides an overview of the technology used in this measure upgrade and includes details about specific features of the dual fuel systems.

## 1.1  Introduction to Technology

A dual fuel heating system combines two technologies: an ASHP and a natural gas furnace. These systems operate together using the same indoor air handler (fan blower) and ductwork to distribute conditioned air throughout the home. The system is designed to alternate between the ASHP and the furnace depending on outdoor temperature.

In this configuration, the ASHP coil is typically installed in line with the furnace, positioned so that air flows through it after passing through the furnace's heat exchanger. During mild to moderately cold weather, the ASHP serves as the primary heating source, transferring heat from the outdoor air into the home using a refrigerant and a compressor.

As outdoor temperatures fall, the heating capacity of the ASHP decreases. At a specific outdoor temperature, known as the switchover temperature or balance point, the system stops the ASHP and starts the natural gas furnace. From this temperature and below, the furnace provides all heating to the home and operates independently when the ASHP is inactive.

The switchover temperature can be set manually or managed by a thermostat that monitors outdoor conditions and determines which system should be active. This arrangement allows for coordinated operation of the two systems without overlap in heating delivery.

This type of system is commonly used in regions with a wide range of outdoor temperatures and where natural gas is available. More detailed information about the individual ASHP and furnace components is provided below.

## 1.1.1  Air-Source Heat Pumps

ASHPs provide space heating and cooling in buildings. According to the 2020 Residential Energy Consumption Survey from the U.S. Energy Information Administration, only ~16.5% of dwellings in the contiguous United States have ASHP systems as their sole source, with most ASHPs located in warmer regions like the southern United States (U.S. Energy Information Administration 2022).

ASHPs transfer heat from outdoor air to indoor air during heating operation and from indoor to outdoor during cooling operation. During heating, an ASHP's compressor powers a vapor compression cycle that delivers heat to the conditioned space of the dwelling. Unlike fossil fuel combustion or electric resistance heating, ASHPs can heat dwellings with efficiency levels above 100%. For example, average heating coefficients of performance (COPs)-the ratio of heating delivered to energy consumed-range from ~2.0 in colder climates to ~3.0 in hotter climates for ducted air-to-air heat pump upgrades (Praprost, et al. 2026). For reference, an electric resistance heater operates at a COP of 1.0.

## 1.1.2  Gas Furnaces

Natural gas furnaces are used to heat indoor environments. For a conventional ducted furnace, natural gas is ignited in the burner, and the flame heats a metal heat exchanger. A blower fan then moves supply air over the heat exchanger. The exhaust combustion products are vented through a flue in the dwelling to the outdoors. The supply air is distributed through the duct system of the dwelling to heat the indoor space.

In a dual fuel heating system like the one modeled in this measure upgrade, the ASHP cooling/heating coil is placed in line with the furnace, using the furnace's blower fan. This setup prevents both systems from operating simultaneously. For this dual fuel heat pump, a switchover temperature is set at which the ASHP heating mode stops and the natural gas heating system takes over. Some dual fuel systems have a switchover 'band,' a range of temperatures where both the heat pump and furnace can serve the load, but that is not the case in this measure. More information on the switchover temperature is available in Section 1.2.1.5. In this measure upgrade, the backup heating system is either a 92% AFUE natural gas furnace or a 95% AFUE natural gas furnace, depending on the climate zone region.

## 1.2  Technical Measure Details

This ASHP option involves retrofitting an existing heating and cooling system with a ducted ASHP and a natural gas furnace into a building that already has existing ductwork for heating or cooling. The ASHP coil, gas furnace, and blower are installed with the dwelling's existing air distribution system, while the outdoor coil and compressor unit are installed outside.

## 1.2.1  ASHP System

This ASHP option involves retrofitting a ducted ASHP into a building that already has existing ductwork for either heating or cooling. The ASHP coil is installed with the dwelling's existing air distribution system and an outdoor coil and compressor unit are installed outside.

## 1.2.1.1  Compressor Speed

This ASHP option has a single-stage compressor. Single-stage compressors are the most affordable ducted version of baseline efficiency ASHPs that this measure upgrade intends to represent. Single-stage compressors operate at one speed across all operating conditions. Typically, single-stage ASHPs retain less heating capacity at cold temperatures than variable-speed ASHPs because the compressor cannot increase speed in colder temperatures.

## 1.2.1.2  Efficiency Ratings: SEER2 and HSPF2

This ASHP option has efficiency ratings based on the Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2 (SEER2) for cooling and the Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2 (HSPF2) for heating. These ratings are calculated by dividing the system's cooling or heating output (Btu) by the electricity consumed (Wh) over the respective cooling or heating season. SEER2 and HSPF2 became mandatory in 2023,  replacing the energy efficiency metrics of SEER and HSPF. The new metrics are measured with external static pressure increased by up to five times to more accurately reflect field conditions and with an updated building load line for HSPF (Department of Energy 2025).

This measure upgrade's ASHPs have a SEER2 of 15.2 and an HSPF2 of 7.8. These efficiency ratings were selected to reflect the minimum requirements for an ASHP to earn an ENERGY STAR ®  rating. These values represent an average efficiency over a typical cooling or heating season, respectively. The instantaneous efficiency of the ASHP will vary according to outdoor and indoor conditions.

## 1.2.1.3  Sizing Methodology

This ducted ASHP is sized according to the load calculations in ACCA Manual J (Air Conditioning Contractors of America 2016) and system selection in ACCA Manual S (Air Conditioning Contractors of America 2014). ACCA Manual J provides the procedure for calculating the design heating and cooling loads of the building, while ACCA Manual S guides the selection of residential HVAC systems size. ACCA Manual S directs that ASHPs be sized to the dwelling's calculated cooling load with allowable oversizing limits. Oversizing limits are recommended, especially for single-stage equipment, because an ASHP sized to the heating load could be considerably larger than what the cooling load requires. This large difference in required size can lead to indoor humidity problems in some climate zones because short cycling reduces the ASHP's ability to dehumidify the space. ACCA Manual S seeks to avoid humidity-related comfort problems with its guidance on oversizing. These oversizing limits depend on the climate of the dwelling and the type of compressor. The oversizing limits for single-stage (reflective of this measure's compressor), two-stage, and variable-speed ASHPs are 15%, 20%, and 30%, respectively, and are implemented only for dwellings with mild winters or humid climates. For dwellings in dry climates with less mild winters, an extra 15,000 Btu/h capacity can be added.

Because this ASHP option is single-stage, the oversizing allowance for dwellings with mild winters or humid climates is 15%. ACCA Manual S considers a dwelling in a humid climate if the cooling load sensible heat ratio is less than 0.95. It considers a dwelling to have a mild winter if the amount of Heating-Degree-Days-65°F (HDD65F) compared to the amount of Cooling-Degree-Days-50°F (CDD50F) is less than double (i.e., HDD65F/CDD50F &lt;2.0). For dwellings that do not meet either of those requirements (i.e., dwellings with cold winters and dry climates), the ASHP's size is increased by the abovementioned 15,000 Btu/h (1.25 tons of refrigeration).

Because this ASHP option is sized using ACCA Manual S, dwellings in cold climates will receive ASHPs that are likely undersized for their respective heating load since ACCA Manual S sizes based on cooling load. That makes ACCA Manual S sizing a good choice for a single-speed ASHP with a fossil fuel furnace as its backup, as in this measure upgrade.

Additionally, this sizing methodology is best practice when sizing a single-stage ASHP to avoid humidity issues from short cycling. Because this ASHP only has one compressor speed, if it were sized to the entire heating load it is likely that in heating-dominant dwellings it would cycle too quickly when in cooling mode and not remove enough moisture from the indoor air, causing discomfort for occupants, as stated above.

## 1.2.1.4  Capacity Retention

As outdoor temperatures drop below the rated capacity temperature of 47°F, the available capacity of the ASHP decreases. The capacity retained by an ASHP below this temperature is defined by its capacity retention fraction. This ASHP option's capacity retention fraction is 0.5 at 5°F. This capacity retention factor specifies the fraction of the ASHP's rated capacity available at 5°F. For this ducted ASHP option, its capacity at 47°F-equal to the ACCA Manual J cooling load with ACCA Manual S oversizing allowances added-will be reduced by half at 5°F.

## 1.2.1.5  Backup System and Switchover Temperature

This modeled ASHP option uses a natural gas furnace as its backup system. Fossil fuel furnaces and ASHP dual fuel systems cannot provide heating at the same time. Therefore, a switchover temperature must be defined for the ASHP system to switch over to the fossil fuel furnace to fully meet the heating load. To operate this system economically, the switchover temperature is typically set between 25°F and 45°F (Air Source Heat Pump Collaborative 2023). The ideal switchover temperature depends on the efficiencies of the ASHP and furnace systems as well as the prices of electricity and fossil fuel. Lennox recommends a switchover temperature of around 35°F for its products (Lennox 2016). For this measure upgrade, the switchover temperature is also set at 35°F.

## 1.2.1.6  Duct Considerations

With this dual fuel system measure upgrade, there are some circumstances where the ducts in the dwelling are resized when the upgrade option is applied. This occurs if the existing ducts are not large enough to accommodate the ACCA Manual S-sized ASHP being installed. Because ACCA Manual S sized systems are smaller than systems sized for the full heating load of the dwelling, there is less likelihood that duct systems will be resized in this particular measure upgrade.

## 1.2.1.7  Cooling Levels

This measure upgrade is intended to replace existing heating systems, but all dwellings that receive this dual fuel option will also receive whole-home cooling from the ASHP, regardless of whether the dwelling had cooling in the baseline. This will increase cooling energy consumption in dwellings that were not already fully cooled, but it will also provide greater comfort and safety in the simulated homes.

## 1.2.1.8  Electrical Requirements

This section summarizes the electrical information for the dual fuel heat pump upgrade. Electrical information in ResStock is used to assess whether the home's electrical panel would become space-constrained or over-capacity due to this upgrade. Section 3.3 describes the electrical panel constraint modeling in more detail.

The power rating of a ducted heat pump system is the sum of the power ratings of the compressor, the air handler, and any electric backup allowed to operate simultaneously, as summarized in Table 1. The heat pump compressor power rating is estimated based on its design heating capacity using a regression equation derived from product labels. The air handler power rating is estimated from the design airflow rate and the OS-HPXML assumption of fan power. The electric resistance backup power rating is directly converted from its heating capacity at an efficiency of 100%. For more information on the modeling assumptions, see the Technical Reference Document (Reyna, et al. 2025).

Table 1. Default Power Rating of Ducted Heat Pump System Components

| Component            | Power Rating [W]                                  | Note                                                          |
|----------------------|---------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------|
| Heat Pump Compressor | (0.631 * Heating_Capacity [kBtu/h] + 1.615) * 240 | Regression of Minimum Circuit Amp, multiplied by voltage      |
| Air Handler          | Air_Flow [cfm] * Fan_Power [W/cfm]                | Default to OS-HPXML assumptions                               |
| Electric Backup      | Heating_Capacity [kBtu/h] * 293.07                | Convert heating capacity directly to power at 100% efficiency |

Ducted heat pumps require at least one 208/240V circuit, each protected by a double-pole breaker. The specific number of circuits depend on whether the heat pump has backup heating (dual fuel or electric) and how the indoor and outdoor units, along with the backup, are configured. Table 2 summarizes the wiring configurations for ducted heat pumps assumed in ResStock. For example, a dual fuel heat pump is assumed to have a 120V air handler on one circuit and a separate 240V circuit for the compressor. For heat pumps with electric backup, the air handler is commonly 240V to allow the strip heat to be on the same circuit; this is also the assumption in ResStock. The maximum overcurrent protection allowed per circuit is assumed to be up to 50A. Therefore, more circuits may be required for larger heat pump systems, which correspond to the use of multiple compressors and/or heat kits in practice.

While a heat pump upgrade replaces both heating and cooling, it may require more circuits and breaker spaces to install than are freed up by removing the existing HVAC systems. The heat pump upgrade may also add more connected load to the panel, especially when the backup is sized for emergency use. The power rating of heat pump systems is determined by their heating configuration and the presence of electric resistance backup. Therefore, homes in colder climates are more likely to face a capacity constraint when upgrading to a heat pump since a backup is often required to supplement the heat pump performance and these homes are more likely to have previously used fuel heating.

Table 2. Default Voltage and Breaker Space for Ducted Heat Pumps

(Note: ckt: circuit, ODU: outdoor unit or heat pump compressor, IDU: indoor unit or air handler)

| Heat Pump System                                   | Circuits                                   | Breaker Spaces                                                                                   |
|----------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Air-to-air / ground-to- air, ducted, dual fuel     | ckt 1: 240V, ODU ckt 2: 120V, IDU          | ckt 1: 2 * round_up(ODU power [W] / 12000) ckt 2: 1                                              |
| Air-to-air / ground-to- air, ducted, no backup     | ckt 1: 240V, ODU ckt 2: 240V, IDU          | ckt 1: 2 * round_up(ODU power [W] / 12000) ckt 2: 2                                              |
| Air-to-air, ground-to-air, ducted, electric backup | ckt 1: 240V, ODU ckt 2: 240V, IDU + backup | ckt 1: 2 * round_up(ODU power [W] / 12000) ckt 2: 2 * round_up((IDU + backup) power [W] / 12000) |

For natural gas systems, the load calculation generally does not include any electrical load from non-electric equipment or appliances. However, because heating and cooling loads in ResStock are determined based on individual HVAC components, gas furnaces are assigned a small service rating based on the air handler power, which is determined by the design air flow rate. Additionally, the air handler is typically on a 120V circuit, which uses a single-pole breaker. Upgrading to a natural gas furnace does not add any constraint to the electrical panel or service; instead, it frees up panel capacity and space for homes that previously used electric heating.

## 1.2.2  Natural Gas Furnace Systems

The natural gas system included in this measure upgrade consists of a conventional ducted natural gas furnace that shares a blower fan and air handler with the ASHP described in the above section. The following subsections describe the technical aspects of the natural gas furnace system.

## 1.2.2.1  Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency

AFUE is the total annual fuel-generated heat output divided by the total annual fuel input (on a scale of 0% to 100%) over a 12-month period. ANSI/ASHRAE 103-2022 provides a procedure for determining the AFUE of residential central furnaces and boilers, using a test cycle designed to simulate typical heating conditions throughout the year-including seasons (ASHRAE 2022).

A higher AFUE rating indicates greater efficiency in converting fuel into usable heat. Energy lost through exhaust gases, radiation, and other components reduces the furnace's efficiency.

In this dual fuel heating system measure upgrade, a higher efficiency furnace with 95% AFUE is used in colder climates (IECC climate zones 5, 6, and 7) while a slightly lower efficiency furnace with 92% AFUE is used in warmer climates (IECC climate zones 1, 2, 3, and 4).

## 1.2.2.2  System Types

Natural gas furnaces can be either condensing or non-condensing (sometimes called conventional). Non-condensing furnaces have a single heat exchanger or set of heat exchangers designed to transfer only sensible heat from the flue gas to the supply air. This design ensures that the flue gas does not condense in the vent pipe and retains enough heat and buoyancy to vent without a fan providing positive pressure. As a result, non-condensing furnaces use non-airtight metal vent pipes and typically have efficiencies between 80 to 82% AFUE (North NJ HVAC 2025).

Condensing furnaces, in contrast, include an additional heat exchanger designed to recover latent heat from the exhaust gases. This process lowers the temperature of the flue gas to a point where it can no longer be vented without a fan providing positive pressure. The lower temperatures and positive pressure required for venting the flue gas also necessitate different vent materials and airtight vent construction. Condensing furnaces must use venting materials approved by the manufacturer, but most allow the use of low-cost polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipe for venting. The additional heat transfer in condensing furnaces enables them to achieve AFUEs between 90% and 98 (North NJ HVAC 2025)%. Because they generate flue gas condensate, condensing furnaces require a condensate drain line.

A non-condensing furnace typically has a lower upfront cost due to fewer components, but incurs higher operating costs because of lower efficiency. The furnace types modeled in this measure upgrade are condensing furnaces because of their high AFUE ratings.

## 2  Applicability

The Dual Fuel Heat Pump System measure upgrade applies to all dwellings with the following:

- Dwellings with ducts that meet the following criteria:
- o Have a non-shared HVAC system
- o Have at least one appliance or an HVAC system fueled by natural gas
- o Have a heating fuel that is not classified as 'Wood,' 'None,' or 'Other Fuel'
- Dwellings in IECC climate zones 1-4 will receive a dual fuel system with a 92% AFUE natural gas furnace
- Dwellings in IECC climate zones 5-7 will receive a dual fuel system with a 95% AFUE natural gas furnace.

Note that this upgrade explicitly is not applied to buildings without heating in the baseline. With these applicability constraints, the measure upgrade applies to 50.8% of the existing residential building stock.

Applicability of this measure upgrade is based on ResStock housing characteristics that determine the available options and options' details. Specifically, the 'HVAC Heating Efficiency' characteristic in ResStock is used to apply this dual fuel heating system option. 'HVAC Has Ducts' describes the presence of ducts in the dwelling. 'Heating Fuel' describes the baseline heating fuel of the dwelling. 'HVAC Shared Efficiencies' describes the shared HVAC system of the dwelling, if applicable. More information on the ResStock housing characteristics can be found in the ResStock Technical Reference Documentation (Reyna, et al. 2025). The exact logic used in the ResStock project YML file for this dataset is available in the GitHub release of the dataset. The remainder of this section provides discussion for why certain apply logic decisions were made.

## 2.1  Treatment of Dwellings With Shared HVAC Systems

Because there are no reported dwellings in the building stock that have only shared cooling and a personal fuel furnace that also have evidence of an outdoor space for a condensing unit (required for the ducted ASHP portion of the dual fuel system), no dwellings with shared systems will be applied with this measure upgrade.

## 2.2  Dwellings With a Natural Gas Line Hookup

For this measure upgrade, only dwellings with an existing natural gas appliance will be able to support the dual fuel heat pumps. Installing a natural gas furnace into a dwelling without an existing natural gas line would incur more costs than a typical retrofit and may be prohibited based on the availability of natural gas in the dwelling's location There must be at least one existing appliance or HVAC system that uses natural gas for the measure upgrade to apply in the ResStock modeling.

## 2.3  Cooling Level Increase for All Applicable Dwellings

Dwellings that are not 100% cooled by their existing AC system (e.g., room AC providing cooling for just one room of a home) will be 100% cooled by this measure upgrade. This will increase cooling energy consumption of those dwellings that were not already 100% cooled. The cooling level increase for all applicable dwellings is implemented because ASHPs in this measure upgrade will be sized to cool the entire dwelling regardless of sizing methodology used. Although this increases energy use associated with cooling, it also increases health and comfort of the home.

## 2.4  Exclusion of 'Other Fuel,' 'None,' and 'Wood' Heating Fuels

Dwellings with 'Other Fuel,' 'None,' or 'Wood' heating fuels do not receive this measure upgrade because retrofits to these dwellings can lead to confusing results (e.g., false negative savings compared to baseline or installing heating in regions that do not need it) and the number of dwellings this affects is relatively small. 'Other Fuel' includes specific fuels that are not currently represented in ResStock, so there is not an accurate baseline heating energy consumption to compare to. Additionally, there is not a large impact at scale since dwellings with primary 'Other Fuel', 'None' or 'Wood' heating fuels comprise approximately 2.6% of the total residential building stock.

## 3.1  OpenStudio-HPXML Overview

The ResStock building stock energy model contains conditional probability distributions of building characteristics that are representative of the U.S. residential building stock (Reyna, et al. 2025). These distributions are sampled to create a synthetic stock where each dwelling model in the stock has an assigned representative option for each characteristic. ResStock utilizes the OpenStudio-HPXML workflow to translate these dwelling characteristics into building models ready to be simulated via OpenStudio ®  and EnergyPlus ®  through the use of an HPXML file. For reference, the state of the OpenStudio-HPXML repository when the ResStock 2025 Release 1 dataset was created can be found here. Assumptions about the measure upgrades in a ResStock dataset release that are not explicitly defined in ResStock are taken care of in the BuildResidentialHPXML measure.

In the case of heating/cooling upgrades in ResStock Dataset 2025 Release 1, these assumptions are all HVAC-related. This documentation covers the calculations directly related to the dual fuel heat pump, but HVAC controls (e.g., setpoints, cycling) and HVAC distribution (e.g., airflow systems) are out of its scope. For information on how OpenStudio-HPXML handles controls in ResStock Dataset 2025 Release 1 see here and for how it handles distribution see here. ## 3.3  Electrical Panels

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock via OpenStudio-HPXML measures in 2025. As new electric loads are added into the home during an improvement project, the electrical panel may not have enough capacity or space headroom to accommodate these new circuits. Thus, the panel may become constrained, requiring an electrical upgrade that may include upsizing the service entrance. Panel constraints can also be mitigated in other ways without the panel or service being upsized, by using load shedding devices, circuit sharing devices, or a smart panel to allow more loads to be connected; low-power, combination, or smart appliances to reduce electric loads at the source; slim tandem breakers or subpanels for more space; and more (Armstrong, et al. 2022). The panel constraint estimate from the upgrade measure allows for the costing of any potential electrical upgrade or mitigation, thus helping to capture the total cost of upgrade more holistically across the housing stock.

Panel capacity and space constraints are modeled using a service load and a breaker space calculation, respectively. The load calculation is based on 2023 National Electrical Code's load-based method in Section 220 (National Fire Protection Association 2023), which guides how the load diversity should be estimated and summed to estimate the total load connected to the panel. When this total load converted to ampacity exceeds the service rating of the panel, the panel is considered capacity-constrained in ResStock. Similarly, the panel is considered space-constrained when the total occupied breaker space counted from the bottom-up exceeds the rated space count of the panel. The panel's capacity and space ratings are probabilistically sampled based on other housing characteristics such as heating fuel and the presence of certain appliances. Users are encouraged to reevaluate these constraint metrics according to other considerations, such as a margin of safety to account for uncertainty in the modeling process. For more details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide (Reyna, et al. 2025).

## 3.4  Modeling Limitations

At the time of these simulations and dataset release, the heat pump fan airflow rate is used during backup heating mode; this is a bug in the EnergyPlus software. Typically, the airflow rate during backup heating mode would be a lower airflow rate because of the furnace's higher supply air temperature.

## 4.4  Electrical Panel Constraints

Figure 11 shows the distribution of modeled panel constraints overall (see 'all' column) and by baseline heating fuel. It is estimated that 8.0% of dwellings upgrading to the typical cold climate heat pump face either a breaker space or a capacity constraint (or both), while 98.0% have no constraints. Most dwellings that face a panel constraint are those that do not have electricity as their baseline heating fuel (i.e., fossil fuel heated dwellings).

All post-upgrade constraint statistics include a minute fraction of homes that are already capacity-constrained in the baseline. Prior to the upgrade, 2.3% of the existing panels are estimated to already be over-capacity, which is possible as the capacity constraint is estimated based on code. In contrast, no existing panel is space-constrained, which makes sense as it is physically impossible to connect more loads than there is space. Capacity constraints in the existing stock are due in part to the uncertainty associated with the modeling process, where the prediction of the panel size is not based on the presence of all major electric appliances. However, it is important to recognize that this level of uncertainty applies to all panel results, including those that look reasonable. Hence, the capacity-constrained homes in the baseline can be thought of as over-capacity homes that experience frequent flickering of lights or tripping of breakers if/when large appliances are turned on. During the Dual Fuel Heat Pump upgrade, any homes already constrained before the upgrade will likely need to address their capacity issue in order to be permitted by code to connect a new load to the panel, whether the upgrade is making the constraint worse or not. Thus, the post-upgrade constraint statistics can be attributed entirely to the upgrade. Users are encouraged to alternatively analyze the constraint outcomes by excluding homes whose pre-existing capacity constraint is not worsened by the upgrade.
